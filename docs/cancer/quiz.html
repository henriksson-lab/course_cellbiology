Quiz: cancer as a disease of cell biology

Short questions to check your understanding of the lecture Cancer as a disease of cell biology. Try each on paper first, then open the hint or the answer. The tag after each question shows the learning outcome (ELO) it practises.

Q1. Sort these genes into oncogenes and tumour suppressors: RAS, TP53, HER2, RB1, BCR-ABL, APC. [ELO 9]

Hint

Ask for each: does the normal protein push the cell to grow (accelerator), or does it stop, repair or kill (brake)?

Answer

Oncogenes (gain of function): RAS, HER2, BCR-ABL. Tumour suppressors (loss of function): TP53, RB1, APC. APC is part of the complex that destroys β-catenin, so losing it switches Wnt signalling on: a lost brake, even though the result is more growth signal.

Q2. Why is one mutated copy enough for an oncogene, while a tumour suppressor needs both copies to be lost? [ELO 9]

Hint

What does the one remaining normal copy do in each case?

Answer

An activated oncogene makes a protein that is active all the time; the normal protein from the other copy cannot stop it, so one copy acts dominantly in the cell. A tumour suppressor protein is a brake: one working copy still makes enough brake protein, so the cell behaves normally until the second copy is also lost (two hits). The effect is recessive in the cell.

Q3. Children with inherited retinoblastoma often get tumours in both eyes, and earlier than children with sporadic retinoblastoma. Explain why with the two-hit idea. [ELO 9]

Hint

How many hits does each retinal cell already carry from birth in the two groups?

Answer

In inherited cases, every cell already carries one mutant RB1 copy from the fertilised egg. Only one more somatic hit is needed, and with millions of dividing retinal cells this is likely to happen several times, in both eyes, early. In sporadic cases both copies must be lost by chance in the same cell, which is rare: usually one tumour in one eye, later.

Q4. A tumour has lost RB1. Will a CDK4/6 inhibitor such as palbociclib stop its cells in G1? Explain. [ELO 9]

Hint

What does cyclin D–CDK4/6 phosphorylate to let the cell enter S phase?

Answer

No. Cyclin D–CDK4/6 drives the cell into S phase by phosphorylating Rb, which then releases E2F. Without Rb, E2F is already free, so blocking CDK4/6 has nothing to act on: the gate is gone, not just open. CDK4/6 inhibitors are used only in tumours that still have Rb.

Q5. Name two ways a cancer cell can escape the limit on the number of divisions or the trigger for apoptosis, and the lecture topic each belongs to. [ELO 9]

Hint

One concerns the ends of chromosomes, the other the outer membrane of mitochondria.

Answer
  1. Telomerase switched on: telomeres no longer shorten with each division, so the cell avoids senescence and crisis (controlling cell numbers). (2) Too much BCL-2 (or loss of BAX/BAK or of p53): the mitochondria stay closed, no cytochrome c is released and no caspases start, so damaged cells do not die by apoptosis (the apoptosis lecture).

Q6. What is the difference between carcinoma in situ and an invasive carcinoma, and what cellular changes are needed to go from one to the other? [ELO 9]

Hint

Look for the thin sheet of matrix under every epithelium.

Answer

In carcinoma in situ the abnormal cells stay above the basement membrane; they cannot reach vessels and surgery cures. An invasive carcinoma has crossed the basement membrane into the stroma. This needs EMT (E-cadherin lost, polarity lost, a loose crawling cell), proteases such as MMPs that cut the basement membrane and matrix, and migration with actin protrusions and myosin.

Q7. A melanoma with BRAF V600E shrinks quickly on a BRAF inhibitor, then grows back after eight months. Give the evolutionary explanation in three steps. [ELO 9]

Hint

Variation, selection, and what was already in the tumour at the start.

Answer
  1. Variation: the tumour of about 10⁹ cells is a mixture of subclones; a few cells already carried a change that makes them resistant (for example a mutation that reactivates the pathway downstream or a bypass route). (2) Selection: the drug kills the sensitive cells, so the tumour shrinks, but the resistant cells survive. (3) Expansion: with the sensitive cells gone, the resistant clone has space and grows back: relapse. Combining a BRAF and a MEK inhibitor makes it harder for a single change to rescue the cell.

Q8. Why do PD-1 antibodies tend to work better in tumours with many mutations (for example melanoma, or tumours that have lost mismatch repair)? [ELO 9]

Hint

What do T cells recognise on a tumour cell?

Answer

T cells recognise peptides on MHC class I. Mutant proteins give new (foreign-looking) peptides. More mutations give more such peptides, so it is more likely that some T cells already recognise the tumour but are held back by PD-1/PD-L1. Blocking PD-1 releases these T cells; if no T cell recognises the tumour, releasing the brake does little.
