Study questions: cancer as a disease of cell biology

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. List six hallmarks of cancer. For each, name one protein or process from this course that is changed, and say whether the change is a gain or a loss of function. [ELO 9]

Model answer
Hallmark Example Gain or loss
Growth signals on RAS locked in the GTP state; HER2 amplified; BCR-ABL fusion kinase gain
Brakes on division removed Rb or p16 lost; cyclin D/CDK4 amplified loss (Rb, p16) or gain (cyclin D)
Resists cell death BCL-2 overexpressed; p53 lost gain (BCL-2), loss (p53)
Divides without limit telomerase switched on gain
Invasion and metastasis E-cadherin switched off (EMT); proteases (MMPs) up loss (E-cadherin), gain (MMPs)
Blood vessels VEGF released by hypoxic cells gain
Altered metabolism glycolysis even with oxygen (Warburg effect) gain of glucose uptake
Immune escape PD-L1 on the tumour cell gain

Any six with correct examples. Genome instability (lost DNA repair, p53, weak spindle checkpoint) is an enabling feature rather than a hallmark.

2. Explain the difference between an oncogene and a tumour suppressor. Give two ways a proto-oncogene can become an oncogene and two ways a tumour suppressor can be lost. [ELO 9]

Model answer

A proto-oncogene codes for a protein that promotes growth or survival. A gain-of-function change makes it an oncogene; one mutant copy is enough (dominant in the cell). Routes: a point mutation that locks the protein on (RAS G12, BRAF V600E); amplification (HER2, MYC); a translocation that makes a fusion protein (BCR-ABL) or puts the gene under a strong enhancer. A tumour suppressor codes for a brake (Rb, p53, APC, BRCA1/2). It contributes to cancer by loss of function, and both copies must be lost (two hits, recessive in the cell). Routes: deletion, a nonsense or frameshift mutation, loss of the second copy (loss of heterozygosity) or silencing of the promoter by DNA methylation.

3. Describe how the Rb protein controls entry into S phase, and give three different changes that remove this control in cancer cells. [ELO 9] [ELO 7]

Model answer

In G1, unphosphorylated Rb binds E2F and keeps the S-phase genes off. Growth signals raise cyclin D, which activates CDK4/6; they phosphorylate Rb, Rb releases E2F, and E2F switches on genes for DNA replication (and cyclin E, which completes the switch). The CDK inhibitor p16 holds CDK4/6 back. In cancer: (1) RB1 lost (both copies); (2) p16 deleted or silenced; (3) cyclin D or CDK4 amplified; also (4) a viral protein (HPV E7) binds and inactivates Rb. Any one of these lets the cell pass into S phase without a proper signal.

4. p53 is called the guardian of the genome. What does p53 do after DNA damage, and why does losing p53 speed up the accumulation of further mutations? [ELO 9] [ELO 8]

Model answer

DNA damage activates kinases that stabilise p53 (it is normally destroyed quickly by MDM2). p53 is a transcription factor. It switches on p21, a CDK inhibitor, so the cell pauses in G1 to repair; if the damage is too great it drives senescence (permanent arrest) or apoptosis (via BH3-only proteins such as PUMA and NOXA). Without p53, damaged cells keep dividing and do not die, so unrepaired damage becomes permanent mutations and broken chromosomes are passed on: genome instability. That is why TP53 loss is found in about half of all cancers and often marks the transition to a more aggressive tumour.

5. What is genome instability? Give one example at the level of DNA sequence and one at the level of whole chromosomes, and explain why it helps a tumour to evolve. [ELO 9]

Model answer

Genome instability is a raised rate of genetic change in a cell line. Sequence level: loss of mismatch repair raises the point-mutation rate 100–1000-fold; loss of BRCA1/2 forces error-prone repair of double-strand breaks. Chromosome level: extra centrosomes give multipolar spindles, and a weak spindle assembly checkpoint lets cells divide before all chromosomes are attached, so daughters gain or lose chromosomes (aneuploidy). A tumour needs several driver mutations; at the normal error rate (about 10⁻⁹ per base per division) collecting them in one cell lineage would take very long. A higher rate produces more variation for selection to act on, so drivers (and drug resistance) appear sooner.

6. Explain what is meant by clonal evolution and tumour heterogeneity. How can multi-region sequencing show that a tumour is heterogeneous? [ELO 9]

Model answer

Clonal evolution: a tumour descends from one cell; new mutations arise (variation), and cells with mutations that let them divide more or die less outgrow the others (selection). Repeated rounds make a family tree of subclones. Heterogeneity: at any time, the tumour contains different subclones with different mutations and properties. Multi-region sequencing: DNA from several regions of one tumour (and normal DNA from blood) is sequenced. Mutations found in every region form the trunk (present in the founding cell); mutations found in only some regions are branches. In kidney cancers about two-thirds of mutations were not present everywhere, so a single biopsy misses much of the tumour’s diversity.

7. Describe the steps of metastasis, from a carcinoma in an epithelium to a tumour in another organ. Which step is the least efficient? [ELO 9] [ELO 6]

Model answer
  1. Local invasion: cells undergo EMT (lose E-cadherin and polarity), cut the basement membrane and matrix with proteases (MMPs) and crawl through the stroma using actin protrusions and myosin. (2) Intravasation into a blood or lymph vessel. (3) Survival in the circulation: shear forces, immune cells, loss of anchorage (anoikis); platelets can protect them. (4) Arrest and extravasation in small vessels of another organ. (5) Colonisation: survive and grow in a foreign tissue (seed and soil); many cells stay dormant for years. Colonisation is the least efficient step: tumours release very many cells, but only a tiny fraction form metastases.

8. A tumour of 10⁹ cells is treated with drug A. Resistance to A is caused by a mutation present in 1 cell in 10⁷. (a) How many resistant cells do you expect at the start? (b) If resistance to drug B arises with the same frequency, independently, how many cells resistant to both do you expect? (c) What does this predict about one-drug and two-drug therapy? [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. 10⁹ × 10⁻⁷ = 100 resistant cells. (b) 10⁹ × 10⁻⁷ × 10⁻⁷ = 10⁻⁵: almost certainly none. (c) With drug A alone, the 100 resistant cells survive and regrow: relapse. With A + B together, a cell must carry both rare changes, which almost certainly does not exist at the start, so the tumour can shrink further and a cure is possible, unless a double mutant arises during therapy while many cells are still dividing. This is the reason for combination therapy, as in HIV and tuberculosis. In practice side effects limit which drugs can be combined, and resistance can also come from non-genetic changes or drug pumps such as P-glycoprotein.

VG-level questions

9. [VG] Two siblings carry the same inherited mutation in one copy of TP53 (Li–Fraumeni syndrome). One develops a sarcoma at age 15, the other no cancer by age 40. (a) Explain how a dominantly inherited cancer risk fits with TP53 being a tumour suppressor. (b) Suggest why the siblings differ. (c) Design an experiment, with tumour and blood samples, to test whether the sarcoma followed the two-hit model. [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. Each sibling inherited one mutant copy, so every cell has hit 1. A tumour needs the second copy lost in one cell (recessive in the cell), plus further drivers. Because the body has very many dividing cells, a second hit somewhere is likely during life, so in families the risk is passed on as a dominant trait, but not every carrier gets cancer, and not at the same age (incomplete penetrance). (b) The second hit and the other drivers are random somatic events: chance alone can make one sibling unlucky. Differences in exposure (radiation, smoking, chemotherapy), in other inherited variants, and in which tissue is hit also matter. (c) Sequence TP53 in blood DNA (should show one mutant and one normal copy: germline heterozygous) and in tumour DNA (micro-dissected to be mostly tumour cells). The two-hit model predicts that the tumour has lost the normal copy: loss of heterozygosity (only the mutant allele left), or a second, different mutation in the normal copy. Controls: normal tissue next to the tumour should still have both alleles. Immunohistochemistry for p53 and a functional readout (p21 induction after irradiation of cultured tumour cells) could confirm that no working p53 is left.

10. [VG] A patient with lung cancer carrying an activating EGFR mutation responds to an EGFR kinase inhibitor, but relapses after a year. Biopsies of two growing metastases give different results: in one, EGFR now has a second mutation in the drug’s binding pocket (T790M); in the other, EGFR is unchanged but the MET receptor gene is amplified. (a) Explain each resistance mechanism in terms of cell signalling. (b) Explain, with clonal evolution, why two metastases in one patient can have different mechanisms. (c) Predict what would happen with a new drug that blocks only T790M EGFR, and propose a better strategy. [ELO 9] [ELO 3]

Model answer
  1. T790M is a gatekeeper mutation: a larger amino acid at the entrance of the ATP pocket lowers the binding of the drug, while the kinase still binds ATP, so EGFR signals again despite the drug. MET amplification is a bypass: MET is another receptor tyrosine kinase; with many copies it activates the same downstream pathways (RAS–ERK and PI3K–Akt) without EGFR, so blocking EGFR no longer stops the growth signal. (b) The tumour was heterogeneous before therapy: different subclones (in different places, or seeded to different metastases) carried, or later gained, different resistance changes. The drug selected the resistant subclone in each place, so independent resistance routes evolve in parallel (branched evolution), as multi-region sequencing shows. (c) A drug against T790M EGFR (such as osimertinib) should shrink the first metastasis but not the MET-amplified one, which would keep growing; the patient would progress. Better: biopsy or liquid biopsy to find all mechanisms, then combine drugs (EGFR inhibitor + MET inhibitor), ideally from the start, so that a single change cannot rescue the cell; follow circulating tumour DNA to detect new resistant clones early.

Solutions

E1. Tumour 1 is diagnosed on day 80. It has about 4 000 cells: no cells resistant to A and 3 resistant to B; about 17 % of the cells carry one driver (a darker sector of the tumour: one subclone that grew faster or died less). On drug A the tumour shrinks fast: from 4 000 to only 7 cells about 5 weeks later (day 117). It then grows back, all red (resistant to A), and passes the progression line 177 days after diagnosis. Here no A-resistant cell existed at diagnosis: the resistant cell arose during therapy, in one of the sensitive cells that were still dividing while the tumour shrank. In other tumours (try seeds 2, 4, 9) resistant cells are already present at diagnosis. Either way, the drug selects the resistant clone, and the space freed by the dead sensitive cells lets it grow.

E2. A, then B at relapse: the relapsed tumour 1 is made only of A-resistant cells, which are still sensitive to B, so drug B kills them all: cured about 200 days after diagnosis. A + B together: cured within about 11 days of therapy; no cell resistant to both existed or arose. Ten tumours (default settings): no therapy 0 of 10 cured, progression after about 8 days; A continuous 2 of 10 cured, median time to progression about 130 days (range about 100–180); A then B 4 of 10 cured, and the other six progress again on B after about 250–360 days (cells resistant to both appear while the A-resistant tumour regrows); A + B 10 of 10 cured. A + B is best because a cell must carry two rare resistance mutations at the same time; the chance is the product of the two small probabilities, so such a cell almost never exists, and the tumour is killed before one can arise. Sequential therapy gives the tumour time to grow large again on drug A, and a large, dividing population produces new mutants, including double mutants.

E3. With the defaults, intermittent therapy is worse: 0 of 10 cured, median time to progression about 105 days (range about 85–135), against about 130 days and 2 cures for continuous A. When the drug is off, the tumour regrows to 4 000 cells, so it always contains thousands of dividing sensitive cells, and each division can produce a new A-resistant cell. Without new resistance during therapy, the result reverses: continuous A cures 5 of 10 (the others progress after about 90–150 days), while intermittent therapy cures none but keeps the tumour under control for longer: in 4 of 10 tumours it is still below the progression line after 3 years, and the median is about 175 days. Now only the few resistant cells present at diagnosis matter, and the sensitive cells hold them back by taking up the space they need to divide (and resistance has a cost). So intermittent (“adaptive”) therapy can delay progression when resistance is already present and cure is not possible, but it can backfire when the large sensitive population keeps making new resistant mutants. This is being tested in clinical trials; the model shows why the outcome depends on assumptions.

E4. At ×10, tumours carry many more resistant cells at diagnosis (about 40–100 instead of 0–20), and more drivers. Drug A: 0 of 10 cured, progression after a median of about 45 days (instead of about 130). A + B: still 9 of 10 cured (one tumour progresses after about 130 days, because a double-resistant cell appeared). The slider models genome instability, an enabling feature: lost DNA repair, lost p53 or chromosome missegregation raise the rate of new mutations. More mutations mean more variation, so resistance (and new drivers) appear sooner; combinations stay effective longer because two independent changes are still needed.

E5. Sooner: with no cost, drug A gives a median time to progression of about 80 days (2 of 10 cured), against about 130 days with the default 30 % cost. Resistant cells now divide as fast as sensitive ones, so the resistant clone regrows faster after the sensitive cells are gone, and it is also larger at diagnosis. A cost can come from a mutant target that works less well (a gatekeeper mutation can make a kinase less active), from energy spent on pumping drugs out (P-glycoprotein uses ATP), or from switching to a slower-growing cell state.
