Study questions: mitochondria

Practise explaining the mechanisms in the lecture, then use the quiz for a shorter self-check. G questions cover the core outcomes; VG questions ask you to predict, interpret data or design a test. Try each answer before opening the model answer.

G-level questions

1. Draw a mitochondrion and label its outer membrane, intermembrane space, inner membrane, cristae and matrix. Place the TCA cycle, respiratory chain, ATP synthase head and cytochrome c. Why do the two membranes have different permeabilities?

Model answer

The matrix lies inside the inner membrane and contains most TCA-cycle enzymes (succinate dehydrogenase is complex II in the inner membrane). The respiratory complexes and ATP synthase sit in the inner membrane, including its cristae; the synthase head faces the matrix. Cytochrome c carries electrons along the intermembrane-space side of the inner membrane. The outer membrane contains porins that pass small solutes. The inner membrane requires specific carriers and has low proton permeability, so it can store an electrochemical H⁺ gradient. Cristae increase the area available for energy conversion.

2. Explain how electrons from NADH reach oxygen, which complexes pump H⁺, and how the resulting gradient drives ATP synthesis. Why does succinate-linked entry through complex II yield less ATP per electron pair?

Model answer

Electrons pass I → ubiquinone (Q) → III → cytochrome c → IV → O₂, reducing oxygen to water. Complexes I, III and IV move H⁺ from matrix to intermembrane space: about 10 H⁺ per NADH. The matrix becomes negative and alkaline; both components favour H⁺ return. Return through ATP synthase turns its c-ring and central stalk. The fixed β catalytic sites change conformation to bind ADP and phosphate, form ATP and release it. Electron transport and ATP synthesis are coupled through the gradient (chemiosmosis). Complex II transfers electrons from succinate to Q without pumping H⁺, bypassing I: about 6 H⁺ are pumped per electron pair instead of 10.

3. The matrix is 160 mV negative relative to the intermembrane space and 0.5 pH unit more alkaline. Estimate the magnitude of the proton-motive force at 37 °C, taking 61.5 mV per pH unit. What happens if H⁺ can freely cross the inner membrane?

Model answer

Both electrical and chemical components favour H⁺ entry, so their magnitudes add: 160 + 0.5 × 61.5 ≈ 191 mV. This is the magnitude; Δψ is negative when defined as matrix minus intermembrane-space potential. H⁺ leakage dissipates the stored energy. Electron transport can continue, and often accelerates, but less of its energy is captured in ATP and more becomes heat. A gradient is energy stored across the membrane, not a large reservoir of free protons.

4. How does ATP made in the matrix become available in the cytosol? Explain why the ADP/ATP carrier depends on the membrane potential and why ATP synthesis also needs phosphate transport.

Model answer

The inner membrane does not freely pass ATP or ADP. The ADP/ATP antiporter exports ATP⁴⁻ in exchange for ADP³⁻, moving one net negative charge out of the negative matrix: the voltage favours export. The phosphate carrier imports phosphate with H⁺ (equivalently, phosphate/OH⁻ exchange). These carriers spend part of the proton-motive force, adding roughly one proton-equivalent to the cost per ATP delivered. Porins allow the nucleotides to cross the outer membrane. The mammalian synthase itself uses 8/3 H⁺ per ATP; delivery and proton leaks help explain why practical ATP yields are lower than the ideal rotor ratio.

5. What does human mtDNA encode? Why do mitochondria still need protein import? Distinguish maternal inheritance of mtDNA from inheritance of a mitochondrial disease caused by a nuclear gene.

Model answer

Human mtDNA has 37 genes: 13 proteins, 22 tRNAs and 2 rRNAs. The 13 proteins are core subunits of complexes I, III, IV and ATP synthase; complex II is entirely nuclear encoded. Most mitochondrial proteins are encoded in the nucleus, made on cytosolic ribosomes and imported, often through TOM/TIM. Mitochondrial DNA is normally inherited from the egg, so fathers do not transmit mtDNA variants. Nuclear genes follow their own Mendelian inheritance patterns and can be transmitted by either parent. A mitochondrial disease is therefore not necessarily maternally inherited.

6. Explain heteroplasmy, the threshold effect and the mitochondrial genetic bottleneck. Why can siblings have different symptoms, and why are brain, heart and skeletal muscle often affected?

Model answer

Heteroplasmy means that different mtDNA variants coexist in a cell or person, here normal and disease-causing copies. During egg development, sampling and amplification of mtDNA produce eggs with different mutant fractions (the bottleneck). Fractions can also differ between tissues. Dysfunction becomes apparent when the remaining capacity cannot meet tissue needs: the threshold depends on the variant and tissue, not one universal percentage. Siblings can therefore inherit different mutant loads and have different disease severity; maternal heteroplasmy does not mean that every child must be affected. High energy demand makes brain, heart and muscle particularly vulnerable.

7. Compare mitochondrial fission and fusion. How do they cooperate with mitophagy to maintain the network?

Model answer

Fission, involving DRP1, divides tubules; fusion joins outer membranes through mitofusins and inner membranes through OPA1. Fusion mixes contents and can complement local defects. Fission helps segregate damaged regions and provides mitochondria for distribution during cell division. Selected damaged mitochondria can be enclosed by autophagosomes and degraded in lysosomes (mitophagy). Fragmentation alone does not prove that mitophagy or cell death has occurred: removal must be measured separately.

8. Give two roles of mitochondria beyond ATP production. Explain why both Ca²⁺ uptake and ROS can be useful at moderate levels and harmful in excess. What changes when cytochrome c reaches the cytosol?

Model answer

At ER–mitochondria contacts, released Ca²⁺ enters the matrix through the mitochondrial calcium uniporter, driven by the negative matrix potential. This shapes Ca²⁺ signals and activates metabolic enzymes. Ca²⁺ overload can promote permeability transition and injury. Electron leakage makes ROS: controlled levels participate in signalling, whereas excessive ROS damage lipids, proteins and DNA. Cytochrome c normally transfers electrons between III and IV. After BAX/BAK permeabilise the outer membrane, cytosolic cytochrome c binds APAF1, helping assemble the apoptosome and activate caspase-9 and executioner caspases. Location changes its effect.

9. Compare chemiosmosis in mitochondria and chloroplasts. Which membrane holds the gradient, which side accumulates H⁺, and where does the ATP synthase head face?

Model answer

Mitochondria use the inner membrane: H⁺ are pumped from the matrix into the intermembrane space and return to the matrix-facing synthase head. Chloroplasts use the thylakoid membrane: light-driven electron transport accumulates H⁺ in the thylakoid lumen; H⁺ return to the stroma, where ATP is made. The envelope around a chloroplast is not the membrane across which photosynthetic ATP synthesis is coupled. Both use an electrochemical gradient and a rotary synthase.

VG-level questions

10. [VG] A stress test gives the following representative oxygen consumption rates from equal numbers of viable cells. Assume each phase has reached a stable rate and FCCP has been optimised. Calculate basal mitochondrial respiration, ATP-linked respiration, proton leak, maximal respiration, spare capacity and coupling efficiency. Is maximal respiration also maximal ATP production?

Phase OCR (pmol O₂/min)
Before drugs 120
After oligomycin 40
After FCCP 200
After rotenone + antimycin A 20
Model answer

Non-mitochondrial OCR is 20. Subtract it where appropriate:

  • Basal mitochondrial respiration: 120 − 20 = 100 pmol O₂/min.
  • ATP-linked respiration: 120 − 40 = 80 pmol O₂/min.
  • Proton leak: 40 − 20 = 20 pmol O₂/min.
  • Maximal respiration: 200 − 20 = 180 pmol O₂/min.
  • Spare capacity: 180 − 100 = 80 pmol O₂/min.
  • Coupling efficiency: 80/100 = 80 %.

FCCP allows proton return independently of ATP synthase, and oligomycin is still present. Therefore the high OCR is not high mitochondrial ATP production. OCR measures oxygen use; converting it into ATP production needs additional assumptions. In real experiments use replicates, normalise to cell number or protein, and optimise drug concentrations.

11. [VG] Predict the immediate effects of oligomycin, an uncoupler and cyanide on electron flow, proton-motive force and mitochondrial ATP synthesis in oxygenated cells supplied with fuel. Why can FCCP increase OCR after oligomycin but not after cyanide? What could maintain some cellular ATP?

Model answer
Treatment Electron flow / mitochondrial OCR Proton-motive force ATP synthesis
Oligomycin Falls as the gradient opposes further pumping Initially rises Blocked at synthase
Uncoupler Usually rises if respiratory capacity and fuel remain Falls Falls strongly
Cyanide Falls: complex IV cannot reduce O₂ Decays as H⁺ return without replenishment Falls as the gradient is lost

FCCP bypasses the blocked proton return pathway after oligomycin: pumping and electron flow resume through an intact chain. It cannot bypass blocked electron transfer at IV. The same distinction explains heat production by DNP. Glycolysis can maintain some ATP; a fall in mitochondrial ATP production does not mean all cellular ATP instantly becomes zero. Severe uncoupling eventually impairs cellular function, so the early OCR prediction need not hold indefinitely.

12. [VG] Design a test of the claim that a proton gradient is sufficient to couple an unrelated proton pump to ATP synthesis. Include controls. Then explain why mitochondrial donation can reduce the risk of mtDNA disease but cannot correct a pathogenic nuclear allele.

Model answer

Reconstitute a light-driven H⁺ pump and ATP synthase into sealed lipid vesicles, with orientations that let the pump create a gradient driving H⁺ through the synthase. Supply ADP and phosphate on the synthase-head side. Compare light vs dark, vesicles missing either protein, and complete vesicles with an uncoupler. Measure ATP accumulation and check the gradient independently with a suitable probe. ATP should accumulate when both proteins and light generate a usable gradient, and should fall when that gradient is dissipated. The controls exclude ATP contamination and demonstrate that shared electron-transfer machinery is unnecessary.

Donation transfers the parents’ nuclear material to donor cytoplasm containing mostly donor mtDNA. It reduces transmission of maternal mutant mtDNA, although some may be carried over. A nuclear allele moves with the nuclear material, so donation does not remove it. This is a change of cellular compartment/genome source, not repair of the mutation.

Explore

Demo: mito stress test. Predict the trace, then measure a simulated well and watch the proton circuit. It starts at 1× assay time: one point per six minutes, three points per phase. Select 240× for a quick run (about 4.5 seconds per phase). The particles and rotor are slowed illustrations; the values are from a qualitative model, not patient data. Drug additions persist until you start a new well. The model assumes adequate fuel, an optimised FCCP dose and a fixed non-mitochondrial OCR; it omits glycolytic ATP and long-term cell responses.

E1. Run Healthy cell. After the basal phase, inject oligomycin → FCCP → rotenone + antimycin A, waiting for each phase to finish. Explain the change in OCR and Δp after every injection.

E2. Use the completed trace to calculate ATP-linked respiration and spare capacity yourself. Compare your calculations with the table. Why do you subtract the final residual OCR?

E3. [VG] Predict, then compare Complex I defect with Healthy cell at the same ATP demand. Which results suggest reduced respiratory capacity? Does OCR alone prove a defect in complex I?

E4. [VG] Start a new well and inject FCCP before oligomycin, then finish with rotenone + antimycin A. Which quantities can you still estimate, and which are lost? Repeat with rotenone + antimycin A first.

E5. [VG] Increase ATP demand and repeat the standard sequence in Healthy cell. Predict basal respiration and spare capacity. Compare the high-concentration metformin preset: what conclusion about therapeutic metformin can this model support?

Solutions

E1. Oligomycin closes the ATP synthase proton route: Δp rises, pumping faces more back-pressure, and OCR falls. FCCP opens a proton bypass: Δp falls and OCR rises even though the synthase is still blocked. Rotenone + antimycin A stop chain flux: only non-mitochondrial OCR remains, and the gradient decays.

E2. ATP-linked = last basal OCR − lowest oligomycin OCR; maximal = highest FCCP OCR − final residual; basal mitochondrial = last basal OCR − residual; spare = maximal − basal mitochondrial. The residual comes from oxygen-consuming processes outside the respiratory chain. Seeded measurement noise causes small variation, so calculate from your well rather than expecting exact fixed values.

E3. The preset lowers chain capacity: maximal and spare respiration fall, and ATP production is harder to sustain. Similar traces can arise from other respiratory defects, fuel limitation or fewer viable cells. Check cell number, viability and mitochondrial abundance; use substrate-specific or biochemical tests to locate the defect. The model assumes a complex I defect; it does not diagnose one from the trace.

E4. FCCP before oligomycin preserves an uncoupled maximum and the final residual, but loses the coupled oligomycin comparison: ATP-linked respiration, proton leak and coupling efficiency cannot be recovered by the standard method. Rotenone + antimycin A first stop electron flow, so subsequent injections cannot reveal ATP-linked or maximal respiratory capacity. Basal and residual OCR remain available.

E5. Higher ATP demand increases proton return through synthase and raises basal OCR; with the same chain maximum, spare capacity falls. The metformin preset illustrates what partial respiratory-chain inhibition could do. Its parameters are chosen, not measured at therapeutic exposure; it cannot establish metformin’s clinical mechanism or predict a patient’s response.

The calculation conventions follow the Agilent assay guide.
