Study questions: apoptosis

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Give three biological reasons why cells undergo apoptosis, with one example of each. Why is it important that apoptosis does not cause inflammation? [ELO 8]

Model answer

Development: the tissue between the digits dies so that fingers form; about half the neurons made die if they do not receive enough survival factor; self-reactive T cells die in the thymus. Damage: a cell with badly damaged DNA activates p53, which makes BH3-only proteins (PUMA, NOXA), and dies instead of passing on mutations. Immunity: killer T cells and NK cells make virus-infected or cancer cells start apoptosis (Fas ligand, granzymes). Tens of billions of cells die every day in a healthy adult; if each death caused inflammation, tissues would be inflamed all the time. Because the membrane stays intact and the cells are eaten quickly, the contents never leak, and neighbouring tissue is not harmed.

2. Describe the shape changes of a cell undergoing apoptosis, and link three of them to specific proteins that caspases cut. [ELO 8]

Model answer

The cell shrinks and rounds up, the chromatin condenses against the nuclear envelope and the nucleus fragments, the membrane blebs, and the cell breaks into membrane-wrapped apoptotic bodies that phagocytes eat. Links (any three): cutting ICAD frees the nuclease CAD, which cuts DNA between nucleosomes (DNA ladder of ~200 bp steps); cutting lamins lets the nuclear lamina and the nucleus fall apart; cutting ROCK1 makes it permanently active, so myosin contracts the cortex and the membrane blebs; cutting the flippase and activating the scramblase XKR8 exposes phosphatidylserine (“eat me”); opening pannexin-1 channels releases ATP (“find me”).

3. Explain how initiator and executioner caspases are activated, and why the caspase system works as an amplifying cascade. [ELO 8]

Model answer

Caspases are cysteine proteases that cut after aspartate, made as inactive procaspases. Initiator caspases (caspase-8, -9) have long prodomains (DED or CARD) that bind adaptors. They are inactive monomers; an adaptor platform (the DISC for caspase-8, the apoptosome for caspase-9) brings several together, and in these dimers they become active (induced proximity). Executioner caspases (caspase-3, -7) are dimers held inactive by an uncut linker; an active initiator cuts it and they become active. One active initiator molecule cuts many executioner molecules, each of which cuts many targets: a cascade that amplifies a small signal, similar to a kinase cascade.

4. Name the three groups of the BCL-2 family, give examples of each, and explain how they interact to decide whether MOMP happens. [ELO 8]

Model answer

Effectors (BAX, BAK) form pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane. Guardians (anti-apoptotic: BCL-2, BCL-XL, MCL-1) have a groove that binds BH3 helices; they hold activated BAX/BAK. BH3-only sensors (BIM, BID/tBID, PUMA, NOXA, BAD) are made or activated by stress; their BH3 helix binds the guardians’ groove and blocks them, and some (BIM, tBID) also activate BAX/BAK directly. The decision is a balance: when BH3-only proteins outnumber the guardians, free active BAX/BAK accumulates; above a threshold they assemble into pores, giving MOMP, an all-or-none event in each cell.

5. Describe the intrinsic pathway from DNA damage to the activation of caspase-3. [ELO 8]

Model answer

DNA damage activates p53, which switches on the genes for the BH3-only proteins PUMA and NOXA. They block the guardians (BCL-2, MCL-1 …), so BAX/BAK become active and form pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane (MOMP). Cytochrome c (normally in the intermembrane space, carrying electrons) and SMAC escape into the cytosol. Cytochrome c binds APAF1; with dATP, seven APAF1 form the apoptosome, which recruits and activates caspase-9 through its CARD domain. Caspase-9 cuts and activates caspase-3 (and -7). SMAC removes XIAP, which would otherwise block caspase-9 and -3.

6. Describe the extrinsic pathway triggered by Fas ligand and explain how it is linked to the intrinsic pathway. [ELO 8]

Model answer

Fas ligand (e.g. on a killer T cell) binds and clusters Fas, a death receptor, on the target cell. The receptors’ death domains recruit the adaptor FADD, which recruits procaspase-8 through its death effector domain: the death-inducing signalling complex (DISC). Procaspase-8 molecules are brought together and become active. Caspase-8 cuts caspase-3/7 directly (enough in some cells, e.g. lymphocytes). It also cuts the BH3-only protein BID to tBID, which activates BAX/BAK at the mitochondria → MOMP → cytochrome c → apoptosome → caspase-9, and SMAC removes the XIAP brake. In many cells (e.g. liver cells) this mitochondrial loop is needed to amplify the signal. FLIP can block caspase-8 at the DISC.

7. How are apoptotic cells recognised and removed? Use the terms “find me”, “eat me” and “don’t eat me”. [ELO 8]

Model answer

Find me: the dying cell releases ATP and UTP (through pannexin-1 channels opened by caspases) and lipids, which attract phagocytes such as macrophages. Eat me: caspases switch off the flippase and activate a scramblase, so phosphatidylserine appears on the outer leaflet; phagocytes bind it with receptors (directly or through bridging proteins). Don’t eat me: healthy cells display CD47, which inhibits phagocytosis. Eating apoptotic cells (efferocytosis) makes macrophages release anti-inflammatory signals (TGF-β, IL-10). If clearance fails, the cells eventually break open and can trigger inflammation and autoimmunity.

8. You treat cultured cells with a drug and want to know whether they die by apoptosis. Describe three methods and what each one measures. [ELO 1] [ELO 8]

Model answer
  1. Annexin V / PI by flow cytometry: annexin V (fluorescent, with Ca²⁺) binds exposed phosphatidylserine; PI enters only cells with a broken membrane. Gives the percentage of live (−/−), early apoptotic (annexin V+ PI−) and late apoptotic (+/+) cells. (2) Western blot for cleaved caspase-3 (the 17 kDa fragment appears) and cleaved PARP-1 (116 → 89 kDa): shows that executioner caspases are active. (3) TUNEL: terminal transferase adds labelled nucleotides to DNA ends made by CAD; works on cells and on tissue sections. Also possible: DNA ladder on a gel, live imaging with caspase reporters or fluorescent cytochrome c, a caspase activity assay with a DEVD substrate. A pan-caspase inhibitor that blocks death supports apoptosis.

9. Explain the mechanism of venetoclax and why it works especially well in chronic lymphocytic leukaemia (CLL). Name two problems that can arise in treatment. [ELO 8] [ELO 9]

Model answer

Venetoclax is a BH3 mimetic: a small molecule that fits the BH3-binding groove of BCL-2 (very selectively; it hardly binds BCL-XL or MCL-1). CLL cells make very much BCL-2 (often because a deletion on chromosome 13 removes the microRNAs miR-15a/16-1), and this BCL-2 holds a lot of BIM: the cells are primed for death. Venetoclax displaces BIM, BIM activates BAX/BAK, and the cells undergo MOMP and apoptosis within hours. Problems: tumour lysis syndrome (so many cells die at once that K⁺, phosphate and uric acid damage the kidneys; hence a 5-week dose ramp-up), and resistance (cells that rely on MCL-1 or BCL-XL, or BCL-2 mutations such as G101V that lower drug binding). Neutropenia also occurs.

VG-style questions

10. [VG] In C. elegans, loss of ced-3 lets all 131 doomed cells survive, while loss of ced-9 makes many extra cells die and kills the embryo. A double mutant lacking both ced-9 and ced-3 is viable and has no extra cell deaths. (a) What does this tell you about the order of the two genes in the pathway? (b) Name the human counterparts and explain why the human pathway has an extra step. [ELO 8]

Model answer
  1. In the double mutant the ced-3 phenotype (no death) wins: this is epistasis. CED-9 acts upstream of CED-3 and works by inhibiting it (through CED-4); without CED-3 there is no executioner, so removing the inhibitor CED-9 no longer matters. The order is EGL-1 ⊣ CED-9 ⊣ CED-4 → CED-3 → death. (b) CED-3 = a caspase (caspase-9/-3), CED-4 = APAF1, CED-9 = BCL-2, EGL-1 = a BH3-only protein. In the worm, CED-9 holds CED-4 directly on the mitochondrial surface. In mammals BCL-2 does not bind APAF1; instead the BCL-2 family controls BAX/BAK and MOMP, and APAF1 is activated by cytochrome c released from the mitochondria. This extra step gives a point of no return (MOMP) and links cell death to the mitochondria’s state, and it releases SMAC at the same time.

11. [VG] A patient with CLL responds well to venetoclax but relapses after two years. The leukaemia cells now have normal BCL-2 levels without mutations but three times more MCL-1. (a) Explain why the cells survive the drug. (b) Predict the effect of adding an MCL-1 inhibitor, and one risk of doing so. (c) Design an experiment with the patient’s cells to test your explanation in (a). [ELO 8] [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. When venetoclax blocks BCL-2, BIM and active BAX/BAK are released, but MCL-1, which venetoclax does not bind, captures them. With three times more MCL-1 the guardians still outnumber the BH3-only proteins, free BAX/BAK stays below the threshold, and no MOMP occurs. (b) Blocking MCL-1 as well removes the last guardian: free BAX/BAK rises above the threshold and the cells should die quickly (a strong combination). Risk: many normal cells depend on MCL-1 (e.g. heart muscle cells, blood stem cells), so toxicity (heart damage, low blood counts) and tumour lysis are possible. (c) Take the patient’s leukaemia cells (before and after relapse), treat them in culture with venetoclax alone, an MCL-1 inhibitor alone and both, at several doses, and measure apoptosis by annexin V/PI flow cytometry and cleaved caspase-3 after 4–24 h. Confirm the mechanism by knocking down MCL-1 (siRNA or CRISPR): this should restore venetoclax sensitivity. Co-immunoprecipitation can show that BIM moves from BCL-2 to MCL-1 after venetoclax. Controls: untreated cells, a pan-caspase inhibitor (death should be blocked), and normal lymphocytes.

12. [VG] A student treats a cell line with an anticancer drug and adds a pan-caspase inhibitor (zVAD). The inhibitor blocks annexin V staining and the cleavage of PARP-1, but after 48 h almost the same number of cells have died, now with swollen mitochondria and a broken membrane. Explain this result. [ELO 8]

Model answer

The caspase inhibitor blocks everything downstream of the caspases: no PARP cleavage, no scramblase activation (no annexin V), no tidy apoptotic morphology. But the drug acts upstream, through the BCL-2 family, so MOMP still happens. After MOMP the mitochondria lose cytochrome c, the electron transport chain fails, ATP falls and the mitochondria are damaged, so the cell dies anyway, more slowly and by a messier, necrosis-like death with membrane rupture. MOMP, not caspase activation, is the point of no return in the intrinsic pathway. (Caspase inhibition can also switch some cells to other regulated death types, the topic of the next lecture.) For therapy, this means that blocking caspases alone rarely saves cells after MOMP.

Explore

The demo is a qualitative threshold model of the intrinsic pathway in a dish of 150 cells. You set the amounts of BH3-only proteins (“stress”), BAX/BAK, BCL-2 and MCL-1 (relative units; a healthy lymphocyte has about 1 of each) and add venetoclax (dose in multiples of its K_d for BCL-2). In each cell, guardians bind BH3-only proteins one to one; BH3-only proteins that are left over activate BAX/BAK, and active BAX/BAK that no guardian captures is “free”. If free BAX/BAK is above a threshold, the cell undergoes MOMP, all or none, and it does so sooner the further it is above the threshold. Then cytochrome c leaves the mitochondria (the cell turns pink), caspases come on (the cell shrinks and blebs), phosphatidylserine appears outside (annexin V, green ring) and, hours later in a dish where nothing eats the cells, the membrane breaks (PI, red nucleus). Every cell has its own random amounts (about 30 % variation), so the strip plot shows free BAX/BAK in each cell against the threshold, and the flow plot shows annexin V/PI as in the lecture. The percentage at the bottom is computed for 24 h at once; the cells and the flow plot follow the clock.

Illustrative numbers, not fitted to data. Left out: the different BH3-only proteins and which guardians they prefer, BCL-XL, how BAX/BAK assemble into pores, IAPs and SMAC, and phagocytes. Time runs at 1× real time by default; MOMP takes about an hour or more, so choose a faster speed to watch a day.

E1. Choose Healthy lymphocyte and the speed 1 h/s. How many cells die in 24 h? Look at the strip plot: where do the cells lie relative to the threshold? Now raise the stress slider to 1.5, 2, 3 and 4. Read the percentage that dies each time. Why does the population respond gradually, although each cell’s decision is all or none? [ELO 8]

E2. Choose CLL cell (high BCL-2), then CLL cell + venetoclax. Compare the fraction that dies in 24 h and when it happens. Pause at about 2 h, 3 h and 6 h and read the flow plot. In which order do the cells move through the quadrants, and why? Use the balance bars to explain why a CLL cell is called “primed”. [ELO 8] [ELO 1]

E3. [VG] Predict first. Choose MCL-1-dependent cell + venetoclax. Will this cell die? Write down your prediction, then check. Now lower MCL-1 step by step to 3, 2 and 1 (as an MCL-1 inhibitor would). What happens? What does this mean for a patient whose CLL relapses with high MCL-1? [ELO 8] [ELO 9]

E4. Choose CLL cell + venetoclax and set BAX/BAK to 0, then 0.2 and 0.5. What happens to the fraction that dies and to the timing? What does this tell you about the role of BAX/BAK, and what would a cancer cell gain by losing them? [ELO 8]

E5. Choose Healthy lymphocyte and add venetoclax at 1×, 10× and 100× K_d. Compare with CLL cell (high BCL-2) at venetoclax 1× and 3.2× K_d. Why does the same drug kill CLL cells far more easily than normal lymphocytes? Then set the CLL cell to BCL-2 = 2 without the drug, and compare with venetoclax 1×: what do you notice? [ELO 8] [ELO 9]

Solutions

E1. Healthy lymphocyte: 1 % die in 24 h; almost all cells sit far to the left of the threshold, because the guardians (BCL-2 + MCL-1 = 2.2) easily hold the BH3-only proteins (0.8). Stress 1.5: 19 %; 2: 41 %; 3: 84 %; 4: 96 %. Each cell undergoes MOMP or not, but cells differ in their amounts of each protein, so they cross the threshold at different stress levels. As stress rises, more and more cells cross: the population curve is graded although each cell is a switch. This is what single-cell imaging shows in real cells.

E2. CLL cell without drug: 7 % die in 24 h; with venetoclax: 100 %, the first after about 36 min and half by about 1.7 h. At 2 h the flow plot shows about 79 % live and 21 % early (annexin V+ PI−); at 3 h about 6 % live and 94 % early; at 6 h about 21 % early and 79 % late (annexin V+ PI+). The order is always live → early → late: caspases expose phosphatidylserine within about half an hour of MOMP, while the membrane breaks only hours later (in a dish, without phagocytes). “Primed”: without the drug the guardian bar (4 BCL-2 + 0.6 MCL-1) is longer than the BH3-only bar (2.5), so the cell lives, but most of the BCL-2 is busy holding BIM. The cell is alive only because of BCL-2; blocking BCL-2 sets a large amount of BH3-only protein free at once.

E3. The cell survives: only 13 % die (8 % without the drug). Venetoclax removes BCL-2 (0.6), but MCL-1 (4) is untouched and still holds all the BH3-only proteins. Lowering MCL-1 to 3 → 37 %, 2 → 74 %, 1 → 100 % die. A relapse with high MCL-1 is resistant to venetoclax alone; combining it with an MCL-1 inhibitor (in clinical trials) should restore killing, at the risk of harming normal cells that need MCL-1.

E4. BAX/BAK = 0: no cell dies, whatever the BH3-only proteins do; there is no pore maker, so no MOMP. At 0.2: 83 % die, and much later (median about 4.6 h instead of 1.7 h), because free BAX/BAK is only a little above the threshold. At 0.5: 100 %, median about 2.2 h. BAX/BAK are the essential effectors of the intrinsic pathway (in mice lacking both, cells resist almost all intrinsic stimuli). A cancer cell that loses them (or makes less) escapes death from DNA damage and from BH3 mimetics; low BAX is one known resistance mechanism.

E5. Healthy lymphocyte with venetoclax 1×: 5 %; 10×: 13 %; 100×: 19 %. CLL cell with venetoclax 1×: 52 %; 3.2×: 95 %. The normal cell holds little BH3-only protein, and part of its protection is MCL-1, so even with all BCL-2 blocked the guardians are almost enough. The CLL cell depends on BCL-2 to hold a lot of BIM, so a little drug tips the balance. This gap is the therapeutic window (in patients, some normal blood cells such as neutrophils are also lost). CLL with BCL-2 = 2 and no drug gives the same 52 % as venetoclax at 1× K_d: at a dose equal to its K_d the drug occupies half of the BCL-2, which has the same effect as having half as much BCL-2.
