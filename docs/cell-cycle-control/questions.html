Study questions: the cell-cycle control system

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Name the four phases of the cell cycle and state what happens in each. What is G0, and give an example of a cell type that is usually in G0. [ELO 7]

Model answer

G1 (gap 1): the cell grows, reads signals from outside and decides whether to divide. S (synthesis): the DNA is replicated, from 2N to 4N. G2 (gap 2): the cell checks that replication is complete and the DNA intact, and prepares for mitosis. M: mitosis (separation of the chromosomes) and cytokinesis (division of the cytoplasm). G0 is a resting state outside the cycle, entered from G1; most cells of the adult body are in G0, for example neurons, heart muscle cells or (until injury) liver cells. A typical human cell line needs about 24 h per cycle, of which M takes about 1 h; G1 is the most variable phase.

2. What is a cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK)? Explain why CDK activity rises and falls during the cycle even though the amount of CDK protein stays constant. Name the cyclin–CDK pair that acts in G1, at G1/S, and in M. [ELO 7]

Model answer

A CDK is a protein kinase (serine/threonine) that is inactive on its own and becomes active only when bound to a cyclin. Cyclins are made at one stage and destroyed at another, so each CDK is active only in the window when its cyclin is present. Activity is further controlled by an activating phosphate (added by CAK), an inhibitory phosphate (added by Wee1, removed by Cdc25) and CDK inhibitor proteins. Pairs: G1: cyclin D–CDK4/6; G1/S: cyclin E–CDK2; M: cyclin B–CDK1 (cyclin A–CDK2 acts in S).

3. Describe three different ways in which a cell can switch off a cyclin–CDK complex. Give one example of where each is used. [ELO 7]

Model answer
  1. Inhibitory phosphorylation: Wee1 adds a phosphate near the ATP site of CDK1; Cdc25 removes it. Used at the G2/M transition and in the DNA-damage checkpoint (Chk1/2 inactivate Cdc25, so CDK1 stays off).
  2. CDK inhibitor proteins (CKIs) bind the complex: p21 after DNA damage (induced by p53), p27 in G1 until SCF destroys it, p16 against CDK4/6.
  3. Destruction of the cyclin by ubiquitination and the proteasome: APC/C–Cdc20 destroys cyclin B at the end of mitosis; SCF destroys cyclin E after S phase starts.

4. Compare the two ubiquitin ligases SCF and APC/C: when are they active, how do they choose their targets, and give one target of each. [ELO 7, ELO 4]

Model answer

Both are E3 ubiquitin ligases that tag proteins with ubiquitin chains for the proteasome. SCF is active throughout the cycle but recognises only targets that a kinase has phosphorylated (a phospho-degron); so its timing is set by the kinases. Targets: the CKI p27 in late G1, cyclin E in S. APC/C is regulated by its activator subunits: with Cdc20 it is active from metaphase to anaphase (targets securin and cyclin B), with Cdh1 from late M through G1 (keeps mitotic cyclins and geminin low). It is switched off at the G1/S transition.

5. Explain how Rb and E2F control entry into S phase. Use the term “positive feedback” in your answer. [ELO 7]

Model answer

Unphosphorylated Rb binds the transcription factor E2F and keeps S-phase genes off. Mitogens raise cyclin D; cyclin D–CDK4/6 starts to phosphorylate Rb, which releases some E2F. Free E2F switches on the genes for cyclin E (and cyclin A, DNA polymerase and other replication enzymes). Cyclin E–CDK2 phosphorylates Rb further, releasing more E2F, which makes more cyclin E: a positive feedback loop. The loop turns a gradual mitogen signal into an all-or-none switch, and once it runs by itself the cell no longer needs mitogens (the restriction point). Cyclin E–CDK2 then triggers origin firing and S phase starts.

6. How does a cell make sure that each part of its genome is replicated only once per cycle? [ELO 7]

Model answer

Through licensing. In G1, when CDK activity is low, helicase rings (MCM) are loaded onto each replication origin with the help of Cdt1 (and other proteins): the origin is licensed. When CDK activity rises at the start of S, licensed origins fire and the rings move away with the replication forks. High CDK activity (and the protein geminin, which binds Cdt1) prevents new loading during S, G2 and M. Only when CDK activity falls at the end of mitosis can origins be licensed again. So each origin can fire at most once per cycle. Cell-fusion experiments showed this: a G2 nucleus cannot be made to replicate again by S-phase cytoplasm.

7. Describe the DNA-damage checkpoint. Include the roles of ATM/ATR, p53, MDM2 and p21, and say what can happen to the cell afterwards. [ELO 7]

Model answer

DNA damage (for example a double-strand break) is detected by the kinases ATM and ATR, which activate Chk1/Chk2. Fast route: Chk1/2 inactivate Cdc25, so CDK1 keeps its inhibitory phosphate and the cell stops in G2. Slow route: p53 is normally destroyed within minutes because the E3 ligase MDM2 tags it; after damage p53 is phosphorylated, escapes MDM2 and accumulates. p53 is a transcription factor; it switches on p21, a CDK inhibitor that blocks CDK2 and CDK4/6, so Rb stays unphosphorylated and the cell stops in G1. The arrest gives time for repair. If repair succeeds, p53 falls and the cycle restarts; if the damage is too severe, p53 drives permanent arrest (senescence) or apoptosis.

8. How were the genes that control the cell cycle discovered in yeast? Explain what a temperature-sensitive cdc mutant is and what Hartwell observed when he shifted such mutants to the high temperature. [ELO 7, ELO 1]

Model answer

A temperature-sensitive mutant makes a protein that works at the low (permissive) temperature, for example 25 °C, but fails at the high (restrictive) temperature, for example 36 °C. This makes it possible to grow mutants in essential genes and then switch the gene off. Hartwell collected budding-yeast cdc (cell division cycle) mutants. After the shift, all cells of one mutant stopped at the same stage, which he could see from the bud size: each gene is needed for one specific step. Nurse used fission yeast in the same way and found cdc2, the master kinase (human CDK1 can replace it), and wee1, whose mutants divide at a small size. Hunt discovered cyclins in sea urchin eggs. They shared the Nobel Prize in 2001.

9. Explain how a DNA-content histogram is measured by flow cytometry and how it can show where a drug stops the cell cycle. Why can it not separate G2 from M? [ELO 7, ELO 1]

Model answer

Cells are fixed, permeabilised and treated with RNase, then stained with a dye whose fluorescence is proportional to the amount of DNA (propidium iodide, DAPI). The flow cytometer measures each cell’s fluorescence; thousands of cells give a histogram with a 2N peak (G1), a 4N peak (G2 and M) and S-phase cells in between. A drug that stops the cycle at one point makes cells pile up there: a CDK4/6 inhibitor raises the 2N peak and empties S, a spindle poison raises the 4N peak. G2 and M cells have the same DNA content (4N), so a second marker is needed, for example an antibody against a mitosis-specific phosphorylated histone (or looking at the cells: mitotic cells are round with condensed chromosomes).

VG-level questions

10. [VG] A patient with oestrogen-receptor-positive breast cancer responds well to palbociclib plus endocrine therapy for two years, then the tumour grows again. A biopsy shows that the tumour cells have lost both copies of RB1. (a) Explain why palbociclib worked at first. (b) Explain why loss of RB1 makes the drug useless. (c) Predict what a DNA-content histogram of the resistant cells would look like with and without palbociclib, compared with the original tumour cells. [ELO 7, ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. ER-positive breast cancer cells depend on oestrogen-driven cyclin D1–CDK4/6 to phosphorylate Rb and release E2F. Palbociclib blocks the ATP pocket of CDK4/6, so Rb stays unphosphorylated and holds E2F; the cells stop in G1 before the restriction point (a cytostatic effect). Endocrine therapy removes the oestrogen signal upstream.
  2. The only job of CDK4/6 in this pathway is to inactivate Rb. Without Rb, E2F is free anyway: cyclin E, cyclin A and replication genes are on, and cyclin E–CDK2 drives S phase without CDK4/6. The drug still blocks its target but the target no longer matters.
  3. Original cells: without drug a normal histogram (2N peak, S plateau, 4N peak); with drug, after a day, nearly all cells at 2N (G1 pile-up). Resistant (Rb-null) cells: the same histogram with and without the drug, with a relatively short G1 (a smaller 2N peak and more S-phase cells than normal cells). This is also why tumours are tested for Rb before or during treatment.

11. [VG] Design an experiment to find the restriction point in a culture of fibroblasts, using live-cell imaging. Say which cells you would use, what you would do to them, what you would measure and what result would define the restriction point. Name one control. [ELO 7, ELO 1]

Model answer

Use fibroblasts expressing FUCCI (or another G1/S marker, for example a CDK2 activity sensor), growing in medium with serum (mitogens). Film them by time-lapse so that the time of birth (end of mitosis) of each cell is known. Then remove the serum (change to serum-free medium) at one time point and keep filming. For each cell that was in G1 at the moment of removal, note how long after its birth the serum was removed, and whether it entered S phase (red → yellow → green) in the next ~12 h or stayed red. Expected result: cells that were less than a certain time after birth (about 3–4 h in fibroblasts) do not enter S; cells beyond that time enter S and divide anyway. That time is the restriction point. Controls: the same cells with a mock medium change that keeps serum (to show that handling alone does not stop them); and a check that the reporter itself does not change cycle length (compare with unlabelled cells by DNA flow cytometry). A molecular version: stain the same cells for phosphorylated Rb at the time of removal.

12. [VG] Cells from a tumour with a TP53 mutation are irradiated. Predict, and explain, how the DNA-content histogram 12 hours later differs from that of irradiated normal cells. Why might losing p53 be an advantage for a tumour but a weakness under treatment? [ELO 7, ELO 9]

Model answer

Normal cells: p53 accumulates, induces p21, and cells in G1 stay in G1; cells in G2 stop through Chk1/2 → Cdc25. 12 h later: a large 2N peak, few S-phase cells (no new cells entered S), and an increased 4N peak. TP53-mutant cells: no p21, so cells in G1 enter S anyway with damaged DNA, and the G1 peak shrinks while S and G2 fill up; the G2 arrest still works, because it does not need p53, so the 4N peak rises. For the tumour, losing p53 means damaged cells keep dividing instead of dying or senescing: mutations accumulate and the tumour evolves. Under treatment, such cells rely entirely on the G2 checkpoint to repair damage before mitosis; drugs that block Chk1 or Wee1 can push them into mitosis with broken DNA, where they die (an approach being tested in clinical trials).

Each nucleus shows the FUCCI colours (red G1, yellow G1/S, green S/G2/M, dark just after division). On the right: the DNA-content histogram of every cell in the dish, built as a flow cytometer would build it, with gates for G1, S and G2/M; and the cyclins of one followed cell (tap any cell to follow it). The model is a simplified caricature with illustrative numbers: it is qualitatively right, not a quantitative prediction. The demo opens in real time, where a cycle takes about a day; choose 1 s = 1 h for the tasks. For each task press Reset all first, then choose the speed and make the settings. The start is the same after every reset, but the times at which you click differ a little, so your numbers may differ from the solutions by a few percent.

E1. Choose 1 s = 1 h and watch the followed cell (white ring) for about 30 h. In which order does its nucleus change colour? Which cyclin peaks when the nucleus turns yellow, and which cyclins vanish when the cell divides? How long is one cycle, and which phase is longest? What fractions does the histogram show for G1, S and G2/M? [ELO 7]

E2. Predict first. Set the CDK4/6 inhibitor to 100 % (Reset all, speed 1 s = 1 h, then set). What will happen to the histogram and to the colours? After 24 h, set the inhibitor back to 0 and watch for another 24 h. What do you see, and why is this a way to make all cells in a dish go through the cycle together? [ELO 7]

E3. The restriction point. Reset, choose 1 s = 1 h, then set mitogens to 0 %. Follow a red cell in early G1 (just after division) and a cell that is red–yellow (late G1). What happens to each? On the cyclin graph, where is Rb phosphorylation in each case compared with the dashed “restriction point” line? Why do some G1 cells still enter S? [ELO 7]

E4. [VG] Predict first. Irradiate the dish once (Reset, speed 1 s = 1 h, then click DNA damage). Predict the histogram after 12 h. Then repeat with p53 lost ticked before you irradiate. Compare the two histograms at 12 h and explain the differences with the two routes of the DNA-damage checkpoint. Which arrest works without p53, and why? [ELO 7]

E5. [VG] A breast cancer has lost RB1. Tick RB1 lost and run for 24 h, then add the CDK4/6 inhibitor at 100 % for another 24 h. Compare with E2. Explain the result, and why loss of Rb shortens G1. What does this mean for choosing patients for palbociclib? Bonus: tick the spindle poison in a fresh dish and compare its histogram with the CDK4/6 inhibitor. [ELO 7, ELO 9]

Solutions

E1. Red → yellow (briefly) → green → division → dark → red again. When the nucleus turns yellow, cyclin E peaks (it rises in late G1 and triggers S; SCF then destroys it). Cyclin A rises through S and G2; cyclin B rises in G2. Both are destroyed by APC/C in mitosis: cyclin A first, then cyclin B at anaphase, just before the cell divides. Rb phosphorylation (dashed) rises through G1, stays high and drops at division. One cycle takes about 21–22 h (the dish doubles in about 22 h); G1 is longest and most variable (about 8 h on average, from 4 to more than 20 h), S about 8 h, G2 about 4 h, M about 1 h. The histogram shows about 50 % G1, 25 % S and 20–25 % G2/M. (The gates are fixed like in flow analysis, so early-S cells near 2N are counted as G1; the true phases are in the grey box.)

E2. Cells in G1 before the restriction point stop at once; cells already in S, G2 or M finish their cycle, divide, and their daughters stop in the next G1. After 12 h about 80 % are at 2N and after 24 h about 99 %: one tall 2N peak, S and G2/M empty, almost all nuclei red. The cell number stops rising (about 500 cells). After removing the inhibitor nothing happens for about 5 h (cyclin D–CDK4/6 must first phosphorylate Rb up to the restriction point), then the cells enter S together: yellow, then green, with S at about 70–80 % about 10–12 h after release, a wave through G2/M at 16–18 h and many divisions at 18–22 h. Because all cells were held at the same point and released at the same moment, they move through the next cycle in step (they slowly drift apart again). Releasing cells from a G1 block (by serum starvation or a CDK4/6 inhibitor) is a standard way to synchronise a culture.

E3. At the moment of withdrawal about 40 % of the G1 cells (about 60 of 140) are already past the restriction point. A cell in early G1 has little Rb phosphorylation; without mitogens cyclin D disappears within an hour, Rb phosphorylation falls back and the cell stays red in G1 (G0). A cell in late G1 has Rb phosphorylation above the dashed line: cyclin E–CDK2 keeps Rb phosphorylated without cyclin D (positive feedback), so it enters S, turns green and divides, and only its daughters stop. In the histogram, S falls from about 25 % to about 6 % at 9 h and 2 % at 12 h, and after about 24 h almost all cells (99 %) are at 2N. (Give mitogens back and the cells return to S after a delay of about 5–6 h, together.)

E4. Normal p53: 12 h after the damage, about 54 % G1, 10 % S, 38 % G2/M. The G1 cells stay in G1 (p53 → p21 blocks CDK2 and CDK4/6, so no new cells enter S); cells in S slow down and finish; cells in G2 stop (Chk1/2 → Cdc25 off → CDK1 inhibited), so the 4N peak grows. Follow a G1 cell: p21 (dotted orange) rises within an hour or two and Rb phosphorylation stays low. After about 18–24 h most damage is repaired, p21 falls and the cells continue in a wave. With p53 lost: about 14 % G1, 54 % S, 32 % G2/M at 12 h. G1 cells do not stop: no p53, no p21, so they enter S with damaged DNA, which is what lets mutations accumulate in a tumour. The G2 arrest still works, because the fast route (ATM/ATR → Chk1/2 → Cdc25 → inhibitory phosphate on CDK1) needs no new protein and no p53. (In the model nobody dies; real cells with too much damage go into senescence or apoptosis, and p53-mutant cells are worse at doing so.)

E5. With RB1 lost the cells cycle with a shorter G1 (about 4 h instead of about 8 h): after 24 h the gates read about 30 % G1, 45 % S, 25 % G2/M, with few red and many green nuclei. E2F is free from the start of G1, so a cell does not have to wait for cyclin D–CDK4/6 to phosphorylate Rb up to the restriction point; cyclin E builds up at once. With the CDK4/6 inhibitor nothing changes: the G1 fraction swings between about 35 and 50 % as before (the short G1 makes the cells partly synchronous), and the cells keep dividing (the status line shows the dish being split; about 1900 cells if never split after 48 h, against about 500 cells that stop in E2). In this pathway the drug’s only job is to keep Rb active, and there is no Rb. So palbociclib needs tumours with an intact Rb protein (ER-positive breast cancers nearly always have one), and loss of RB1 is one way that tumours become resistant during treatment. Mitogen withdrawal also has no effect on these cells: they have no restriction point. Bonus: the spindle poison gives the opposite histogram: within 12–18 h nearly all cells are at 4N and green (stuck in mitosis with cyclin B high, because the spindle checkpoint keeps APC/C off), while the CDK4/6 inhibitor piles cells up at 2N and red.
