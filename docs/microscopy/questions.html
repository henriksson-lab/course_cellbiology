Study questions: seeing cells (microscopy)

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Explain the difference between magnification and resolution. Why can a light microscope not separate two objects that are 50 nm apart, however much it magnifies? [ELO 1]

Model answer

Magnification is how much bigger the image is than the object. Resolution is the smallest distance at which two points can still be seen as two. Light is a wave, so a lens turns each point of the sample into a small blur spot. When two points are closer than about \(0.61\,\lambda/\mathrm{NA}\), their blur spots merge into one. With visible light (about 500 nm) and the best objectives (NA about 1.4) this limit is about 200 nm. Two objects 50 nm apart give one blob, and magnifying the blob more adds no detail.

2. Describe how a phase-contrast microscope and a DIC microscope make living, unstained cells visible. Why is staining with dyes usually not an option for living cells? [ELO 1]

Model answer

Unstained cells absorb almost no light, but dense structures (nucleus, membranes) have a higher refractive index and slow light down (phase shift). Phase contrast converts these phase shifts into differences in brightness. DIC uses polarised light to turn gradients in optical path into light and shadow, so edges look three-dimensional. Most dyes for bright-field microscopy need the cells to be fixed and often sectioned, and many dyes are toxic, so stained cells are dead.

3. Draw and label the light path in an epifluorescence microscope with a filter cube for a green fluorophore. State what each filter and the dichroic mirror does. [ELO 1]

Model answer

Lamp → excitation filter (passes only blue light that excites the fluorophore) → dichroic mirror (reflects blue light down through the objective onto the sample) → fluorophore absorbs blue and emits green → green light goes back up through the objective → passes through the dichroic mirror (it transmits longer wavelengths) → emission filter (passes green, blocks stray blue) → camera or eye. The design works because of the Stokes shift: emitted light has a longer wavelength than the excitation light.

4. Describe the steps of indirect immunofluorescence to show where tubulin is in cultured cells. Why do we use a secondary antibody? [ELO 1] [ELO 6]

Model answer
  1. Fix the cells (e.g. formaldehyde) so proteins are cross-linked and stay in place. 2. Permeabilise the membranes with a mild detergent so antibodies can enter. 3. Block free binding sites (e.g. with serum proteins) to reduce background. 4. Add the primary antibody against tubulin (e.g. made in mouse), wash. 5. Add a fluorescent secondary antibody against mouse antibodies, wash. 6. Often add a DNA dye (DAPI), mount and image. The secondary antibody amplifies the signal (several secondaries bind each primary), and one labelled secondary can be used with many different primaries from the same species.

5. What is GFP, why was it so useful for cell biology, and how is a GFP fusion protein made? [ELO 1] [ELO 6]

Model answer

GFP is the green fluorescent protein from the jellyfish Aequorea victoria. Its chain of 238 amino acids folds into a barrel and forms its fluorescent group by itself (it needs only oxygen), so the gene works in any cell. To make a fusion protein you join the coding sequence of the protein of interest to the GFP coding sequence in the same reading frame, without a stop codon between them, and introduce the DNA into cells. The cell makes one chain, protein–GFP, so you can see where the protein is in a living cell and follow it over time. Mutants of GFP and proteins from corals give other colours, so several proteins can be followed at once.

6. Explain how a confocal microscope gives sharper images of thick samples than a wide-field fluorescence microscope. What is the cost? [ELO 1]

Model answer

In wide-field fluorescence the whole depth of the sample is lit, so out-of-focus light adds haze. A confocal microscope focuses a laser spot in the sample and places a pinhole in front of the detector at the point where light from the focal plane is focused. Light from other depths is out of focus at the pinhole and mostly blocked. The spot is scanned to build the image. This gives a thin optical section (about 0.5–1 µm), and a stack of sections gives a 3D image. Costs: it is slower (point by point), and the sample receives more light, so there is more photobleaching and phototoxicity.

7. Name two problems you meet when you film living fluorescent cells for many hours, and one way to reduce each. [ELO 1]

Model answer

Photobleaching: fluorophores are destroyed by repeated excitation, so the signal fades. Phototoxicity: excited fluorophores make reactive oxygen species that damage the cell, so it may behave abnormally or die. Both are reduced by using less light (lower intensity, shorter exposures, fewer time points, a more sensitive camera) and gentler methods such as spinning-disk confocal or light-sheet microscopy, which light less of the sample. The cells must also be kept at 37 °C with the right CO₂ and medium.

8. Compare TEM and SEM: how is the image made, what does each show, and why can neither be used on living cells? [ELO 1]

Model answer

TEM: electrons pass through an ultrathin section (about 50–100 nm) stained with heavy metals; areas that scatter electrons look dark. It shows internal structure (membranes, organelles, ribosomes) at about 1–2 nm. SEM: a fine beam scans a metal-coated sample, and electrons knocked out of the surface are detected. It shows the 3D surface at a few nm. Both work in a vacuum, so samples are fixed and dried (and for TEM embedded and cut), which kills the cells.

9. What is single-particle cryo-EM? Explain why averaging many images is needed and what the result is. [ELO 1]

Model answer

A purified protein or complex is frozen so quickly that water forms glass-like ice, keeping the molecules in their native, hydrated shape without stain. Only a low electron dose can be used, so each image of a single molecule is very noisy. A computer finds tens of thousands to millions of particle images, sorts them by viewing angle and averages them, so noise cancels and the signal remains. The 2D views are combined into a 3D density map, often at better than 0.3 nm, into which an atomic model is built.

VG-style questions

10. [VG] A research group makes a GFP fusion of a newly discovered protein, P. In living cells, P–GFP is spread evenly through the cytosol. But immunofluorescence with an antibody against P in fixed cells shows P on mitochondria. Give two possible explanations, and propose an experiment to decide between them. [ELO 1]

Model answer

Possible explanations:

  1. The GFP tag disturbs targeting. If P has a mitochondrial targeting signal at its N-terminus and GFP was fused there, the signal may be hidden; or the fusion is overexpressed so much that the import machinery is saturated and the excess stays in the cytosol.
  2. The antibody is not specific. It may bind another, mitochondrial protein, so the immunofluorescence result is wrong.
  3. (Also acceptable) Fixation artefact: soluble proteins can be extracted or moved during fixation and permeabilisation.

Experiments: put GFP at the other end of P and express it at low level (e.g. from the gene’s own promoter, or by tagging the endogenous gene with CRISPR); check if it then goes to mitochondria (co-stain with a mitochondrial marker). Test the antibody on cells lacking P (knockout or knockdown): if mitochondria are still stained, the antibody is not specific. Biochemistry can add independent evidence: fractionate cells and test which fraction contains P on a western blot (next lecture).

11. [VG] You want to know (a) how often vesicles carrying a GFP-tagged cargo move along microtubules in living cells, and (b) whether the cargo sits inside the vesicle or on its outer membrane. Design an imaging approach for each part, and explain why one method cannot answer both. [ELO 1] [ELO 6]

Model answer

  1. Live-cell fluorescence imaging of the GFP-cargo, ideally with microtubules labelled in a second colour (e.g. a red fluorescent tubulin fusion), using a fast, gentle method (spinning-disk confocal or light-sheet) and short exposures to limit bleaching and phototoxicity. Track vesicles over time and count runs along microtubules. Control: a drug that depolymerises microtubules should stop the directed runs.

  2. A vesicle is about 50–100 nm across, below the 200 nm light limit, so inside versus outside cannot be decided with ordinary light microscopy. Use immuno-electron microscopy: fix cells, label the cargo (anti-GFP antibody with gold particles) on thin sections, and see whether the gold is in the lumen or on the cytosolic face of the vesicle membrane. Cryo-electron tomography or super-resolution would also help.

One method cannot do both because live imaging needs light microscopy (low resolution, living cells), while the nanometre question needs EM (high resolution, but dead or frozen cells). Combining them on the same cells is called correlative light and electron microscopy (CLEM).

12. [VG] Explain why the SARS-CoV-2 spike in the mRNA COVID-19 vaccines carries two extra prolines (the “2P” spike), and why cryo-EM was a good method to check this design quickly. [ELO 1]

Model answer

The spike is a fusion protein: it starts in a compact prefusion shape and, when it fuses the viral and cell membranes, S1 is lost and S2 refolds into a long postfusion rod. Many protective (neutralising) antibodies recognise the prefusion shape, but a spike made on its own can flip to the postfusion form. Two prolines, which are stiff, placed at a hinge in S2 block this refolding and keep the prefusion shape, so the immune system sees the right target. The idea came from structures of related coronavirus spikes (MERS, SARS). Cryo-EM was ideal because the spike is a large, flexible, glycosylated membrane-protein trimer that is hard to crystallise; cryo-EM needs only a small amount of purified protein, no crystals, and keeps it in a near-native state. In early 2020 a cryo-EM structure confirmed within weeks that the SARS-CoV-2 2P spike holds the prefusion shape.

Explore

Two glowing points (for example two fluorescent molecules) seen through a microscope. The lens turns each point into a blur spot with faint rings (an Airy pattern); the camera sees the sum of the two spots. The graph below the image shows the brightness along the line through both points: the dashed curves are the two single spots, the solid curve is what the camera records. Change the distance \(d\) between the points (slider, or drag on the image), the wavelength \(\lambda\) and the numerical aperture NA, or switch to electrons. The readout gives the Rayleigh limit \(0.61\,\lambda/\mathrm{NA}\) and the depth of the dip between the two peaks.

E1. Start with green light (520 nm) and the oil objective (NA 1.4), \(d = 300\) nm. Move the points slowly together. At what distance does the readout say you reach the Rayleigh limit? Check it with the formula. How deep is the dip there, and at about what distance does the dip disappear completely? [ELO 1]

E2. Set \(d = 250\) nm. Try the three objectives: dry 10× (NA 0.25), dry 40× (NA 0.65) and oil 100× (NA 1.4). Which of them separate the two points? What is the limit for each? [ELO 1]

E3. [VG] Predict first, then check. Keep \(d = 250\) nm and NA 1.4. Will the two points be resolved in red light (650 nm)? In blue light (450 nm)? Then explain why a light microscope cannot be improved much further by choosing a shorter wavelength or a better lens. [ELO 1]

E4. [VG] Switch to Electrons (TEM). The wavelength is about 100 000 times shorter than green light. What is the limit with a 10 mrad aperture, and why is it much larger than the wavelength? Why do electron micrographs of cells still show only about 1–2 nm detail? [ELO 1]

Solutions

E1. The limit is \(0.61 \times 520 / 1.4 \approx 227\) nm. At that distance the first dark ring of one spot falls on the centre of the other, and the solid curve has a dip of about 26 % between the two peaks: just enough to see two spots. Closer, the dip becomes shallower and disappears at about 175 nm (about \(0.47\,\lambda/\mathrm{NA}\), the Sparrow limit); below that the camera records one blob, and no magnification can split it again.

E2. The limits are about 1270 nm (NA 0.25), 490 nm (NA 0.65) and 227 nm (NA 1.4). Only the oil objective separates points 250 nm apart. A lens with a higher NA collects light from a wider cone, which makes the blur spot smaller.

E3. Red light: limit \(0.61 \times 650 / 1.4 \approx 283\) nm, larger than 250 nm, so not resolved (a faint dip only). Blue light: \(0.61 \times 450 / 1.4 \approx 196\) nm, so resolved. Shorter wavelengths help, but visible light stops at about 400 nm (shorter UV light damages cells and is absorbed by glass). NA \(= n \sin\theta\) cannot exceed the refractive index of the oil and glass (about 1.5), because \(\sin\theta \le 1\). So the limit for a light microscope stays near 200 nm. Super-resolution methods get around it by making neighbouring molecules glow at different times, not by a better lens.

E4. \(0.61 \times 0.0037 / 0.01 \approx 0.23\) nm. The limit is about 60 times the wavelength because electron lenses have large errors (aberrations), so only electrons travelling at a very small angle can be used: the NA is about 0.01 instead of 1.4. In cells, the sample sets the limit: the section is fixed, stained with heavy metals and about 50–100 nm thick, and the stain grains and the overlapping material in the section blur the detail to about 1–2 nm. Purified proteins imaged frozen (cryo-EM) get much closer to the lens limit.
