Quiz: seeing cells (microscopy)

Short questions to check your understanding of the lecture Seeing cells: microscopy. Try each on paper first, then open the hint or the answer. The tag after each question shows the learning outcome (ELO) it practises.

Q1. A student says: “If I use a 100× objective and a 20× eyepiece instead of a 10× eyepiece, I will be able to see ribosomes in a light microscope.” Is the student right? [ELO 1]

Hint

What does the eyepiece change: the size of the image or the amount of detail in it? How big is a ribosome compared with the resolution limit?

Answer

No. A stronger eyepiece only magnifies the image more; it does not improve resolution. Resolution is set by the wavelength of light and the numerical aperture of the objective, and is about 200 nm for the best light microscope. A ribosome is about 25 nm, so it stays below the limit. Extra magnification just makes the blur bigger (“empty magnification”).

Q2. Using \(d \approx 0.61\,\lambda / \mathrm{NA}\), which change gives better (smaller) resolution: (a) blue light (450 nm) instead of red light (650 nm), or (b) a dry objective with NA 0.75 instead of an oil objective with NA 1.4? [ELO 1]

Hint

Smaller \(\lambda\) and larger NA both make \(d\) smaller.

Answer
  1. improves resolution: shorter wavelength gives smaller \(d\) (about 196 nm with NA 1.4 instead of about 283 nm). (b) makes it worse: a lower NA collects light from a narrower cone, so \(d\) grows (for 500 nm light, about 407 nm with NA 0.75 instead of about 218 nm with NA 1.4).

Q3. Why can unstained living cells be seen clearly in a phase-contrast microscope but hardly at all in a bright-field microscope? [ELO 1]

Hint

Cells absorb very little light. What else happens to light that passes through a nucleus or a membrane?

Answer

Cells are mostly water and absorb little visible light, so in bright field the light passes through almost unchanged. But dense parts of the cell (higher refractive index) slow the light down, giving a phase shift that the eye cannot see. Phase-contrast optics turn these phase differences into differences in brightness, so the nucleus and the cell edges become visible without stain.

Q4. A fluorophore is excited at 490 nm and emits at 520 nm. What is the Stokes shift, and why is it essential for fluorescence microscopy? [ELO 1]

Hint

Think of the filter cube: what would happen if the emitted light had the same colour as the excitation light?

Answer

The Stokes shift is the difference between the excitation and emission peaks, here 30 nm. Emitted light has a longer wavelength because some energy is lost as heat before emission. Because the colours differ, the dichroic mirror and emission filter can block the very bright excitation light and pass only the weak emitted light. Without the shift, the glow would be lost in the excitation light.

Q5. You want to follow how fast a vesicle protein moves in a living neuron. Should you use immunofluorescence or a GFP fusion protein? Give one reason and one thing you must check. [ELO 1] [ELO 6]

Hint

What must you do to cells before antibodies can get inside?

Answer

A GFP fusion protein. Immunofluorescence needs the cells to be fixed and permeabilised, so they are dead and nothing moves. A GFP fusion is made by the living cell itself. You must check that the GFP tag does not change the protein: it should still work and be in the same place as the untagged protein (for example, compare with antibody staining in fixed cells).

Q6. What does the pinhole in a confocal microscope do, and what do you gain from it? [ELO 1]

Hint

Where is light from the focal plane focused, and where is light from other depths focused?

Answer

The pinhole sits where light from the focal plane comes to a sharp point, so that light passes. Light from planes above and below is spread out at the pinhole and mostly blocked. You gain a sharp optical section (about 0.5–1 µm thick) without out-of-focus haze, and by refocusing step by step you can build a 3D stack without cutting the sample.

Q7. Match each question to the best method: (i) the shape of microvilli on the surface of an intestinal cell; (ii) the atomic structure of a purified membrane pump; (iii) how the ER membrane is arranged next to a mitochondrion inside a cell, at nanometre detail. Methods: TEM, SEM, single-particle cryo-EM. [ELO 1]

Hint

Which method shows surfaces, which shows thin slices through a cell, and which averages many copies of one purified molecule?

Answer
  1. SEM: a scanned beam shows the 3D surface. (ii) Single-particle cryo-EM: many images of the purified protein in thin ice are averaged into a map, often better than 0.3 nm. (iii) TEM of thin sections (or cryo-electron tomography of a frozen cell) shows membranes inside the cell at about 1–2 nm.

Q8. In a figure, protein A (green) and protein B (red) give yellow in the merged confocal image. The authors conclude “A binds B”. What is wrong with this conclusion? [ELO 1]

Hint

How close must two proteins be to look like one spot in a confocal image? How big is a protein?

Answer

Yellow only means the two signals are in the same spot within the resolution limit, about 200 nm, which is about 40 protein diameters. A and B could be in the same organelle without touching. To claim binding you need other evidence, for example co-immunoprecipitation (next lecture), or a method with much better resolution. You should also check the controls (for example cells lacking A) and that the signal does not leak from one channel into the other.
