Quiz: a tour of the cell

Short questions to check that you can use the map of the cell from the lecture. Try each on paper first, then open the hint, and only then the answer.

Q1. [ELO 1] Name three structures that a eukaryotic animal cell has and a bacterium does not.

Hint

Think of everything that is surrounded by its own membrane inside the cell.

Answer

Any three of: a nucleus with a double membrane (nuclear envelope), endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, endosomes and lysosomes, mitochondria, peroxisomes, and a cytoskeleton with microtubules organised by a centrosome. A bacterium has a plasma membrane, ribosomes and DNA in a nucleoid, but almost no internal membranes.

Q2. [ELO 1] Which of these spaces are on the same side of a membrane as the outside of the cell: cytosol, ER lumen, nucleoplasm, lysosome lumen, Golgi lumen?

Hint

Which compartments exchange material with the outside by vesicles that bud and fuse? Which are joined by open pores?

Answer

The ER lumen, the Golgi lumen and the lysosome lumen are topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell: vesicles carry material between them and the outside without crossing a membrane. The cytosol and the nucleoplasm form the other side; they are joined through nuclear pores.

Q3. [ELO 1] Put these in order from smallest to largest, and give a typical size for each: ribosome, mitochondrion (width), protein, nucleus, potassium ion, virus such as influenza.

Hint

Each step on the scale ladder was roughly a factor of 3–10.

Answer

Potassium ion (~0.3 nm) < protein (~5 nm) < ribosome (~25 nm) < influenza virus (~100 nm) < mitochondrion (~1 µm wide) < nucleus (~5–10 µm).

Q4. [ELO 1] A protein diffuses across a 10 µm cell in a few seconds. About how long would it take to diffuse 100 µm? What does this tell you about very long cells such as nerve cells?

Hint

Diffusion time grows with the square of the distance: \(t \approx x^2 / 6D\).

Answer

Ten times the distance means \(10^2 = 100\) times the time: a few hundred seconds, that is several minutes. Over a metre, the time becomes hundreds of years. So long cells such as nerve cells cannot rely on diffusion; they carry cargo with motor proteins along the cytoskeleton.

Q5. [ELO 5] A cell type is packed with rough ER and Golgi. What does the cell probably spend most of its effort on? Give one example of such a cell.

Hint

What does the rough ER make, and where do those proteins go?

Answer

It probably secretes large amounts of protein. The rough ER makes proteins for secretion and the Golgi modifies and sorts them. Examples: a pancreatic acinar cell (digestive enzymes) or a plasma cell (antibodies).

Q6. [ELO 1] A membrane receptor has a sugar chain on one domain. On which side of the plasma membrane do you expect to find that domain, and why?

Hint

Sugar chains are added in the lumen of the ER and Golgi. Follow the protein from the ER to the plasma membrane.

Answer

On the outside of the cell. The sugar chain is added while that domain faces the ER and Golgi lumen. Vesicles bud and fuse without the protein flipping, so the lumen-facing side ends up facing the outside of the cell.

Q7. [ELO 1] Give one advantage and one limitation of using HeLa cells to study a human protein.

Hint

Think of what a cell line is, and where HeLa came from.

Answer

Advantage: human cells that divide without limit, are cheap and easy to grow, image and perturb, and are used in many labs, so results can be compared. Limitation: HeLa cells are cancer cells with an abnormal genome (around 76–80 chromosomes) and an HPV infection that disables normal cell-cycle brakes; they grow flat on plastic, so they may not behave like a normal cell in a tissue. (Also acceptable: contamination of other cell lines, or the ethical questions about consent.)

Q8. [ELO 1] Electron microscopy of thin sections first showed the ER and ribosomes. Why was another method also needed to understand what lysosomes do?

Hint

What does a picture tell you, and what does it not tell you?

Answer

A micrograph shows where a structure is and what it looks like, but not what it does. Cell fractionation (breaking cells and separating their parts by centrifugation, then measuring enzyme activities in each fraction) showed that one fraction held digestive enzymes that work at acidic pH: the lysosomes.
