Study questions: the journey of a drug through the cell

Use the two journeys to connect mechanisms from the lecture. Draw the compartments and label the molecule at each step before opening a model answer. The quiz gives a shorter self-check.

G-level questions

1. Follow an mRNA–LNP from a particle outside a cell to productive translation. Name the two membrane barriers, explain endocytosis and escape, and distinguish the paths of the lipid carrier, mRNA and encoded protein. [ELO 2, ELO 4, ELO 5]

Model answer

The plasma membrane separates extracellular space from cytosol. Endocytosis encloses the particle in an intracellular vesicle, so the cargo crosses the cell boundary without being released into cytosol. The endosomal membrane is a second barrier: intact mRNA must become accessible to cytosolic ribosomes before it is degraded. Ionisable carrier lipids can favour membrane interactions during acidification; the exact escape route depends on formulation and cell type. The lipids can traffic or be metabolised separately, the mRNA is translated in the cytoplasm and eventually degraded, and the protein follows its own sequence-dependent targeting route. Neither carrier uptake nor persistent lipid fluorescence demonstrates cytosolic delivery of functional mRNA.

2. A delivered mRNA encodes a protein with a cleavable ER signal peptide, a large antigen domain and a membrane anchor. Explain how the antigen domain reaches the ER lumen and what can happen if it fails to fold. Why must you inspect the remaining sequence before predicting the effect of deleting the signal peptide? [ELO 4]

Model answer

Translation begins on a cytosolic ribosome. SRP recognises the emerging signal, directs the ribosome to the ER, and translation feeds the chain through a translocon. The signal is cleaved; the antigen domain enters the lumen, where chaperones help it fold and N-glycans can be added. Its membrane anchor retains it in the bilayer. Failed folding causes retention and can lead to ER-associated degradation: export to cytosol, ubiquitination and proteasomal degradation. Deleting a cleavable signal does not necessarily prevent all ER targeting: a remaining hydrophobic segment may act as an internal signal-anchor, possibly with altered topology. A simple “cytosolic protein” prediction requires the assumption that no functional alternative targeting signal remains.

3. Trace a correctly folded membrane antigen from ER to cell surface. Mark which side contains its glycosylated antigen domain and which contains its cytosolic tail at each stage. Give a way to test the final orientation. [ELO 1, ELO 2, ELO 4, ELO 5]

Model answer

ER → transport carriers → Golgi → carriers from the trans-Golgi network → plasma membrane. The antigen domain faces the lumen of each compartment; the tail faces the cytosol. Fusion with the plasma membrane exposes the carrier lumen to the outside, placing antigen and glycans extracellularly. Test with antibodies against the external domain and cytosolic tail: in intact viable, non-permeabilised cells only the external epitope is accessible. Permeabilisation allows both to be detected. Include antibody-specificity and membrane-integrity controls. A total-protein measurement alone cannot establish surface orientation.

4. Explain how cytoskeletal transport and organelle homeostasis support the journey. Give two reasons a global ATP-depletion experiment cannot isolate a single failed step. [ELO 5, ELO 6]

Model answer

Microtubules and motors position organelles and move many carriers; actin and myosins contribute to local traffic. Motor transport works with docking and fusion machinery rather than replacing it. Energy supports translation, chaperone cycles and motor activity. ATP-driven pumps establish organelle pH and calcium conditions, so mitochondria and glycolysis support several steps indirectly. ATP depletion can therefore impair both protein production and trafficking, while also disrupting compartment chemistry or damaging cells. A reduced surface signal does not distinguish these effects. Use a time course, viability controls and more focused perturbations, measuring the earliest affected event.

5. Compare whole-antigen display with classical peptide–MHC-I display. Which route uses secretory transport, and which uses the proteasome? Why does neither measurement alone establish a complete immune response? [ELO 3, ELO 4]

Model answer

A whole membrane antigen folds and reaches the surface through ER and Golgi traffic. In the classical MHC-I route, the proteasome generates peptides; peptides are transported into the ER, loaded onto MHC-I, and peptide–MHC-I complexes also pass through the secretory pathway to the surface. Thus secretory transport contributes to both, while proteolysis is central to peptide generation. Folded antigen and peptide–MHC engage different immune receptors. Immune responses also depend on the presenting cell, receptor specificity, costimulation, cytokines and other interactions. Antigen production in one cell is evidence of expression, not proof of immune protection. Other presentation routes are covered in Immunologi.

6. Follow imatinib from outside a leukaemia cell to inhibition of BCR–ABL1. Explain why total cellular drug, target engagement and downstream phosphorylation are different measurements. [ELO 1, ELO 2, ELO 3, ELO 9]

Model answer

Available extracellular drug enters the cell; entry, export, binding and sequestration determine the amount accessible at the target. Imatinib binds the BCR–ABL1 kinase domain and reduces its signalling. Total cellular drug includes bound or sequestered material and does not automatically give free target-site concentration. Target engagement tests interaction with the intended protein. Phosphorylation reports kinase or pathway action, which may change indirectly. A validated target-dependent readout, total-protein controls and an appropriate time course help connect the measurements. This route acts on an existing protein and does not require translation of a new therapeutic protein or Golgi export.

7. Explain how kinase inhibition can cause cell-cycle arrest or intrinsic apoptosis. Which measurements distinguish those outcomes, and why is a smaller cell population insufficient evidence of death? [ELO 1, ELO 3, ELO 7, ELO 8]

Model answer

Reduced mitogenic signalling can lower cyclin/CDK activity and prevent cell-cycle progression. Reduced survival signalling can shift BCL-2-family control towards BAX/BAK activation, mitochondrial outer-membrane permeabilisation, cytochrome c release and caspase activation. The outcome depends on cellular dependencies and the state of the death machinery. Measure DNA synthesis or cell-cycle profiles for proliferation and caspase activation with complementary viability or membrane-integrity measurements for death. Compare time courses and controls. A population can become smaller relative to control simply because it divides more slowly; this does not require cells to die.

8. Compare three resistance mechanisms: increased drug efflux, a resistant kinase and a downstream survival bypass. Predict whether each can preserve growth and whether it must prevent inhibition of the intended kinase. [ELO 2, ELO 3, ELO 9]

Model answer

Increased efflux can lower target-site exposure so kinase activity remains sufficient. Target resistance, such as a mutation reducing imatinib sensitivity, can preserve kinase activity despite adequate exposure. A downstream bypass can sustain growth or survival even when the intended kinase is inhibited. All can reduce treatment response, but only the third explicitly allows intact target inhibition with continued downstream function. Test exposure, target sequence/abundance and target action before assigning the mechanism; pathways can change together. Clinical resistance is not diagnosed from a cell count alone.

VG-level questions

9. [VG] Two mRNA formulations give the following results in equal numbers of viable cells at the same time. Values are normalised to formulation A. Interpret the difference and design two measurements to distinguish competing explanations. [ELO 1, ELO 2, ELO 4]

Readout A B
Cell-associated lipid fluorescence 100 180
Total encoded protein 100 25
Model answer

B has more cell-associated lipid label but less protein expression. It does not follow that B delivered more intact mRNA to cytosol. Possibilities include more surface-bound or endosomally trapped carrier, separation of lipid and RNA, RNA degradation, or impaired translation. Measure (1) cargo location and cytosolic access, for example using a validated access reporter with membrane-damage controls, and (2) intact RNA and/or translation competence with an independent control mRNA delivered by another method. Surface-label removal or quenching and endosomal colocalisation can distinguish some uptake artefacts. Viability normalisation does not exclude sublethal stress. The table alone cannot uniquely locate the defect or establish which formulation would work better in vivo.

10. [VG] After a treatment, total antigen remains near control but surface antigen falls sharply. RNA delivery is unchanged. Compare an ER-folding defect with an ER-to-Golgi traffic block. Design a test that distinguishes them, including a control and a limitation. [ELO 1, ELO 4, ELO 5]

Model answer

Both can lower surface delivery while leaving initial translation intact. A folding defect can retain antigen in the ER and eventually send it to ERAD; a traffic block can retain folded cargo before Golgi arrival. Use a time-resolved pulse-chase or equivalent synthesis/chase experiment, combined with ER/Golgi localisation and a folding- or conformation-sensitive assay. Include an unrelated secretory cargo: a general transport block should affect that cargo too, whereas a cargo-specific folding mutation may not. Endo-H sensitivity can support failure to acquire Golgi processing but cannot alone distinguish the two hypotheses, because both ER-retained proteins may remain sensitive. Check synthesis, membrane integrity and viability. Imaging localisation without a folding test cannot establish whether the retained cargo is correctly folded.

11. [VG] Three leukaemia cell lines receive the same extracellular inhibitor. A validated target-dependent phosphorylation readout and DNA synthesis are measured relative to vehicle controls. Propose a next test for each line. Do not assign a unique molecular mechanism from the table. [ELO 1, ELO 3, ELO 7, ELO 8, ELO 9]

Line Target-dependent phosphorylation DNA synthesis
X 15 % 20 %
Y 95 % 95 %
Z 15 % 90 %
Model answer

X: pathway action and DNA synthesis both fall, consistent with target-dependent proliferation. Measure cell-cycle distribution and apoptosis separately to distinguish arrest from death. Y: little pathway inhibition; measure cellular exposure and target engagement, then test target sequence/abundance. Low exposure and a resistant or abundant target are alternatives, not conclusions. Z: the intended pathway readout falls while DNA synthesis persists; investigate alternative growth signalling, incomplete duration of inhibition or assay timing. Test a candidate bypass by an independent perturbation rather than merely correlating its expression. Use matched time courses, replicate wells, total-protein controls and viable-cell counts. Neither DNA synthesis nor phosphorylation alone establishes long-term response or clinical efficacy.

Explore

Demo: drug journey. Choose mRNA–LNP or Imatinib and use Next and Previous, or select a numbered stop. The map follows a mechanism one event at a time; it has no automatic biological clock. The endpoint readouts are qualitative predictions, not measured data or probabilities of treatment success. Choose a What-if scenario only after making your own prediction. Switching journeys returns to its first stop and Baseline; changing a scenario keeps the current stop so you can compare it directly.

E1. Select mRNA–LNP, Baseline. Step through all seven stops. At Escape, Translation, ER/Golgi and Surface/peptides, name the molecule being followed and which side of a membrane it occupies. Does the mRNA pass through the Golgi? [ELO 2, ELO 4, ELO 5]

E2. Predict, then select Impaired endosomal escape. Which readouts could still look successful even though protein expression falls? Why would increasing particle fluorescence not resolve the problem? [ELO 1, ELO 2, ELO 4]

E3. [VG] Compare No ER-targeting signal with Secretory traffic block. Which early events can remain intact? Why must the signal scenario assume that no alternative signal-anchor redirects the protein? Does failed whole-antigen surface display necessarily eliminate every antigen-derived peptide? [ELO 4, ELO 5]

E4. Switch to Imatinib and follow the five stops. Compare Increased drug efflux and Resistant target. Which measurement helps distinguish the two assumptions, and which would show whether kinase activity actually fell? [ELO 1, ELO 2, ELO 3, ELO 9]

E5. [VG] The kinase readout falls but cells remain alive. Explain why this need not contradict the drug’s molecular mechanism. Choose separate tests for cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis and a survival bypass, then explain what the map cannot decide for you. [ELO 1, ELO 3, ELO 7, ELO 8, ELO 9]

Solutions

E1. Escape moves intact mRNA from endosomal lumen to cytosol, where ribosomes can translate it. Translation starts in cytosol. The emerging protein’s ER signal directs the growing chain into/along the ER membrane; the antigen domain faces the ER lumen. The protein travels through Golgi and secretory carriers to the surface, where that domain faces outside. Peptide–MHC display is a separate processing route. The mRNA and LNP do not need to travel through the Golgi with the antigen.

E2. Particle association and uptake can remain high, while cytosolic access and expression are low. A fluorescent lipid reports its own location, not necessarily that of intact mRNA. Measure cargo integrity and cytosolic access, with endosomal, membrane-damage and viability controls.

E3. Both scenarios can preserve uptake, escape and initial translation. In the signal scenario, ER targeting is assumed to fail; in the traffic scenario, protein can enter the ER but fail to reach its later destination. Real sequences can contain internal signal-anchors, so deleting a signal peptide is not a universal way to prevent ER entry. A protein that fails to appear intact at the surface can still provide proteasomal peptides, depending on its synthesis, degradation and the cell’s presentation machinery. The simple map cannot predict the full set of peptide–MHC complexes or an immune response.

E4. Increased efflux assumes lower drug available at the target; target resistance assumes reduced inhibitor sensitivity. Compare exposure and target engagement, sequence/abundance and a validated kinase-action readout. Similar endpoint growth patterns do not distinguish the causes. The scenario selector chooses a mechanism for illustration; it has not diagnosed one from data.

E5. Cells can arrest without immediately dying, persist through alternative survival routes, or respond on a different timescale. Assess DNA synthesis and cell-cycle profiles for arrest, caspase activation plus complementary viability measures for apoptosis, and controlled perturbation of a candidate pathway for a bypass. The map supplies a causal story and predictions; it does not model cell-state diversity, actual drug concentrations or clinical response.
