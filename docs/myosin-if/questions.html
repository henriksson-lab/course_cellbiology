Study questions: myosin, intermediate filaments and the mechanical cell

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then open the answer. Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

Useful values (frog skeletal muscle, as in the lecture): thick filament 1.6 µm (bare zone 0.2 µm), thin filament 1.0 µm on each side, Z-disc 0.05 µm.

G-level questions

1. Describe the domain structure of a myosin molecule. How do the tails of myosin II and myosin V suit their different jobs?

Answer

Every myosin has a motor domain (head) that binds actin and ATP, a lever arm (neck) stabilised by light chains that amplifies small shape changes in the head into a swing of several nanometres, and a tail. Myosin II has a long coiled-coil tail; hundreds of tails bundle into a bipolar thick filament with heads at both ends, so it pulls actin filaments of opposite polarity towards the middle: contraction (muscle, contractile ring, cell rear). Myosin V has long lever arms (36 nm steps) and a tail that binds cargo (vesicles, pigment granules); it walks processively, hand over hand, along actin.

2. Draw and explain the cross-bridge cycle of myosin II. For each step, say what happens to the nucleotide and to the head.

Answer
  1. Rigor: no nucleotide, head bound tightly to actin. 2. ATP binds → the actin-binding cleft opens and the head releases actin. 3. ATP is hydrolysed to ADP + Pi (both stay bound) → the lever is cocked (re-primed). 4. The head binds actin weakly at a new site; Pi is released → the power stroke: the lever swings and moves the actin filament 5–10 nm towards the M-line (towards the actin minus end). ADP is released → back to rigor. One ATP is used per cycle. A sketch should show the actin filament, the thick filament, the head attached or detached, the lever position and the nucleotide in each state.

3. Draw a sarcomere and label: Z-disc, M-line, thin filament, thick filament, A band, I band, H zone. Which bands change length during contraction, and why?

Answer

The sketch: Z-discs at both ends; thin (actin) filaments anchored in each Z-disc with their plus ends, pointing to the middle; thick (myosin II) filaments in the middle, centred on the M-line, overlapping the thin filaments. A band = the thick-filament length (dark); I band = the part with only thin filaments (light), across the Z-disc; H zone = the middle part of the A band without thin filaments. During contraction the A band stays constant and the I band and H zone shrink, because the filaments keep their length and slide past each other (sliding-filament model).

4. Explain the shape of the length–tension curve of skeletal muscle (Gordon, Huxley and Julian, 1966): the descending limb, the plateau and the ascending limb.

Answer

Descending limb (above 2.25 µm): force falls in a straight line with the overlap between thin filaments and the head-bearing part of the thick filaments, reaching zero at 3.65 µm (1.6 + 2 × 1.0 + 0.05 µm), where the filaments no longer overlap. This shows that every cross-bridge contributes force independently. Plateau (2.0–2.25 µm): every head faces a thin filament; extra overlap in the bare zone, which has no heads, adds nothing. Ascending limb (below 2.0 µm): thin filaments from the two halves cross the M-line and get in each other’s way (and wrong-polarity filaments face the heads); below about 1.65 µm the thick filaments are pressed against the Z-discs, so force falls steeply.

5. Explain how Ca²⁺ controls contraction in skeletal muscle. Compare with how non-muscle myosin II is switched on.

Answer

Skeletal muscle: at rest (Ca²⁺ ≈ 0.1 µM) tropomyosin lies on the actin filament and covers the myosin-binding sites. An action potential opens Ca²⁺ channels in the sarcoplasmic reticulum; Ca²⁺ rises to about 10 µM and binds troponin C; troponin moves tropomyosin aside and heads can bind. SERCA pumps return Ca²⁺ to the sarcoplasmic reticulum to relax the muscle. The switch is on the thin filament. Non-muscle myosin II (and smooth muscle) has no troponin. The switch is on myosin itself: phosphorylation of the regulatory light chain by myosin light-chain kinase (activated by Ca²⁺–calmodulin) or by Rho kinase turns the motor on and lets it form minifilaments; a phosphatase turns it off.

6. Give two jobs of non-muscle myosin II in cells and explain how it does them.

Answer
  1. Cytokinesis: at the end of mitosis, a contractile ring of actin filaments and myosin II minifilaments forms under the plasma membrane at the cell equator. Myosin pulls the antiparallel actin filaments past each other, the ring tightens like a purse string and pinches the cell in two. (2) Cell migration: at the rear of a migrating cell, myosin II contracts the actin network, pulls the cell body and tail forward and helps detach rear adhesions, while actin polymerisation pushes the front. (Also: tension in stress fibres, which traction-force microscopy measures.)

7. Describe how intermediate filaments are assembled, and explain why they have no polarity and no motor proteins.

Answer

Each protein has a long α-helical rod with head and tail domains. Two rods wind into a parallel coiled-coil dimer. Two dimers associate side by side, antiparallel and staggered, into a tetramer. Eight tetramers pack side by side into a unit-length filament, and these join end to end and compact into a 10 nm filament. Because the tetramer is antiparallel, both ends of the filament are the same: no polarity. Motor proteins need a polar track to know which way to walk, so no motors move on intermediate filaments. They also bind no ATP or GTP; they are regulated mainly by phosphorylation.

8. Name four families of intermediate filaments and where each is found. How do intermediate filaments give an epithelium its mechanical strength?

Answer

Keratins (epithelia, e.g. skin, gut, liver), vimentin (fibroblasts, endothelium, white blood cells), desmin (muscle), neurofilaments (axons), nuclear lamins (nuclear lamina in all nucleated cells). In an epithelium, keratin filaments are anchored at desmosomes (to the next cell) and hemidesmosomes (to the basal lamina), so the networks of all cells form one continuous network that spreads force over the whole sheet. The filaments themselves can be stretched to two or three times their length before breaking and become stiffer as they stretch.

9. Choose a method to answer each question and explain briefly how it works: (a) How much force does a fibroblast exert on its surroundings? (b) How far does one myosin V molecule move per step? (c) Is the keratin network rebuilt as fast as the actin network?

Answer
  1. Traction-force microscopy: grow the cell on a soft gel of known stiffness containing fluorescent beads; measure bead displacements with and without the cell; calculate the force map (nanonewtons). (b) Optical tweezers (or single-molecule TIRF tracking of a fluorescent myosin V on surface-bound actin): a laser trap holds a bead attached to the motor or the actin filament; the bead jumps by one step (36 nm) per ATP, and the trap also measures the force (piconewtons). (c) FRAP: express GFP-keratin or GFP-actin, bleach a spot, measure how fast fluorescence recovers. Actin recovers within seconds; keratin much more slowly (minutes or longer): a more stable network.

VG-style questions

10. [VG] A heart muscle cell normally works at sarcomere lengths between about 1.8 and 2.3 µm. (a) Using the length–tension curve, predict what happens to the force of contraction when the heart fills with more blood and its sarcomeres are stretched from 1.9 to 2.2 µm. (b) A patient has a mutation in cardiac myosin that releases heads from the parked (super-relaxed) state. Predict the effect on force and on ATP use, and explain why mavacamten helps. (c) Why must the dose of mavacamten be adjusted carefully?

Answer

  1. At 1.9 µm the sarcomere is on the ascending limb: thin filaments from the two halves overlap each other and force is below maximum. Stretching to 2.2 µm brings it to the plateau, where every head faces a thin filament, so force increases. (This is the cellular basis of the Frank–Starling law: a fuller heart pumps more strongly; more about it in the organ course. In real heart muscle, stretch also increases Ca²⁺ sensitivity.)

  2. More heads are available to form cross-bridges, so at the same Ca²⁺ the muscle makes more force and uses more ATP (more cycling heads, and fewer heads in the energy-saving parked state); relaxation is slower. Over time the heart wall thickens (hypertrophic cardiomyopathy). Mavacamten binds the myosin motor domain, slows its ATPase (phosphate release) and stabilises the parked state, so fewer heads form cross-bridges and force and energy use fall back towards normal.

  3. Mavacamten reduces force for every head it parks. Too much drug parks too many heads and the heart pumps too weakly (heart failure). Patients are therefore checked by echocardiography (how much blood the ventricle ejects), and the dose is adjusted for each person.

11. [VG] A researcher adds a drug to permeabilised muscle fibres (the membrane is made leaky, so the solution reaches the filaments directly). In the bath, Ca²⁺ is 10 µM and ATP is present, and the fibres contract normally. Predict and explain the result in each case: (a) ATP is removed from the bath; (b) ATP is replaced by a non-hydrolysable ATP analogue that binds myosin like ATP but cannot be split; (c) Ca²⁺ is lowered to 0.1 µM while ATP is present.

Answer

  1. No ATP: heads finish their stroke and remain bound (rigor). The fibre becomes stiff and cannot relax or shorten further; it stays at whatever tension it had. This shows that ATP is needed to release heads.

  2. Non-hydrolysable analogue: binding still makes heads let go of actin, but they cannot hydrolyse it, so the lever is not re-cocked and no power stroke follows. The fibre relaxes (becomes floppy, like a resting muscle) and produces no force, even though Ca²⁺ is high. This separates the two roles: binding releases, hydrolysis cocks.

  3. Low Ca²⁺ with ATP: troponin loses Ca²⁺, tropomyosin covers the binding sites, heads (cocked with ADP·Pi) cannot bind: the fibre relaxes normally. Compare with (a): low Ca²⁺ relaxes a muscle only when ATP is present to detach the heads.

12. [VG] Transgenic mice that make a mutant keratin 14 in their skin, in addition to their own two normal keratin 14 genes, develop blisters after mild friction, like patients with epidermolysis bullosa simplex. (a) Explain how a mutant keratin can cause disease when plenty of normal keratin 14 is also made. (b) Design an experiment with cultured keratinocytes to test whether cells with the mutant keratin are mechanically weaker, and name the controls.

Answer

  1. Keratin 14 pairs with keratin 5, and many keratin molecules are packed side by side and end to end in each filament. The mutant keratin 14 is still built into the same dimers, tetramers and filaments as the normal protein, but it disrupts filament assembly (often it changes the conserved ends of the rod that are needed for elongation). Because each filament contains many subunits, a minority of bad subunits is enough to spoil most filaments: the network collapses into clumps. This is a dominant-negative effect, which is why the human disease is usually dominant (one mutant copy is enough). Without a network anchored to desmosomes and hemidesmosomes, basal cells tear under friction.

  2. Grow keratinocytes with normal or mutant keratin 14 (patient cells, or cells edited with CRISPR) as a sheet. Mechanical test: stretch the cells on a flexible membrane (or apply shear stress) and count broken cells, e.g. with a dye that enters only damaged cells; or detach the sheet with an enzyme, shake it and count the fragments (a weaker sheet breaks into more pieces). Imaging: immunofluorescence for keratin 14 to see network versus clumps; FRAP of GFP-keratin to compare network stability. Controls: wild-type cells; cells expressing extra normal keratin 14 at the same level (to rule out over-expression effects); unstretched cells; ideally a rescue by correcting the mutation. Repeat with several independent cell lines and test the difference statistically.

Explore

A sarcomere drawn to scale (frog filament lengths, as in the lecture), with its Z-discs, thin filaments (actin with tropomyosin and troponin), and thick filaments with their myosin heads. Set the sarcomere length with the slider or by dragging the orange point on the length–tension curve; the band labels (A, I, H) update. The bars show the three factors that multiply to give the active force: the filament overlap (how many heads face a thin filament), the binding sites exposed by Ca²⁺ on troponin, and the heads available (not parked). The muscle is held at fixed length, so the filaments do not slide; the heads go through the cross-bridge cycle at 1× real time (about one cycle per second per head, like a slow heart myosin). The geometry of the length–tension curve is accurate in shape; the Ca²⁺ and mavacamten effects are qualitative (right direction, illustrative numbers).

E1. Choose Full activation, optimal length. Read the A band, I band and H zone. Now set the sarcomere length to 3.0 µm, then to 1.7 µm. Which bands change and which do not? Read the force at each length and explain it from the drawing.

E2. Choose Relaxed (rest Ca²⁺) and watch the heads for a few seconds. Then raise Ca²⁺ slowly. At which Ca²⁺ is the force half of maximum? How much does Ca²⁺ have to rise to go from about 10 % to about 90 % of the force? Why is a steep switch useful for a muscle?

E3. Choose No ATP (rigor). What do the heads do, and why can the muscle not relax even though Ca²⁺ is low? Tick ATP again: what happens? Which step of the cross-bridge cycle does this show?

E4. [VG] Predict first. Set the length to 3.0 µm with full Ca²⁺. Will raising Ca²⁺ from 10 to 20 µM restore the force? Will the HCM mutation? Test both, and explain which factor limits the force here.

E5. [VG] Choose HCM mutation at optimal length. What is the force? Increase mavacamten until the force is back at about 100 %. What happens if you give the full dose? Explain what this means for treating patients, and why the shape of the length–tension curve does not change with the drug.

Solutions

E1. At 2.1 µm: A band 1.60 µm, I band 0.25 µm on each side, H zone 0.05 µm; force 100 % (plateau: every head faces a thin filament; the counter shows all 88 heads facing a thin filament). At 3.0 µm: A band still 1.60 µm, I band 0.70 µm on each side, H zone 0.95 µm; overlap 0.325 of 0.70 µm per half, so force 46 %, and only 40 of 88 heads face a thin filament. At 1.7 µm: A band 1.60 µm, I band 0.05 µm, no H zone; force 86 %: the thin filaments from the two halves overlap each other in the middle and get in the way (the demo uses the measured shape of the ascending limb). The A band never changes because the thick filaments keep their length; I and H change because the filaments slide.

E2. At rest (0.1 µM) almost no sites are exposed (0.1 %) and the heads stay cocked, waiting. Force is 50 % at 1 µM. Force goes from about 10 % (0.5 µM: 11 %) to about 90 % (2 µM: 89 %) over only a fourfold rise in Ca²⁺. The switch is steep (cooperative: several Ca²⁺ ions and neighbouring troponin–tropomyosin units act together). A steep switch makes the muscle either relaxed or fully on with a small change in Ca²⁺, and makes it insensitive to small fluctuations in resting Ca²⁺. (The demo’s curve is qualitative: K = 1 µM, Hill coefficient 3.)

E3. Without ATP, every head that faces a thin filament binds, makes its stroke and stays bound: after a few seconds all 88 heads are attached. Ca²⁺ is low, but the bound heads push tropomyosin aside, and without ATP they cannot let go, so the muscle is locked and stiff: rigor. When ATP returns, ATP binds the heads, they release, re-cock, and (at low Ca²⁺) stay detached: the muscle relaxes. This shows step 2 of the cycle: ATP binding releases the head.

E4. No to both. At 3.0 µm the overlap limits force: only 40 of 88 heads face a thin filament. Raising Ca²⁺ from 10 to 20 µM changes nothing (46 % at both), because the sites were already fully exposed (99.9 % at 10 µM). The HCM mutation raises force to 62 %, because more of the heads that face a thin filament are free to bind, but it cannot create new overlap. Force is the product of the three factors, so the smallest one (here the overlap) sets the limit.

E5. HCM mutation: force 133 % of normal (80 % of heads available instead of 60 %). Mavacamten at 30 % of the maximal effect brings force back to about 101 % (35 %: 95 %). At the full dose only 15 % of heads are available and force falls to 25 %: the heart would pump far too weakly. This is why patients get their dose adjusted step by step, with ultrasound checks of heart function. The drug acts on the heads, not on the filament geometry, so it scales the whole curve down by the same factor at every length: the shape of the length–tension curve stays the same.
