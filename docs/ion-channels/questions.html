Study questions: ion channels and membrane potential

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

Useful values (37 °C): \(RT/F \approx 26.7\) mV, or \(61.5\) mV per tenfold gradient for a singly charged ion.

G-level questions

1. Compare ion channels and transporters with respect to (a) rate, (b) how the solute crosses, (c) whether they can move a solute against its gradient. [ELO 2]

Model answer
  1. Rate: channels pass up to about 10⁸ ions per second; transporters move about 10²–10⁴ molecules per second. (b) A channel is a water-filled pore; when open, ions stream through without the protein changing shape for each ion. A transporter binds the solute, changes shape (alternating access) and releases it on the other side; it is never open to both sides at once. (c) Channels only allow passive flow down the electrochemical gradient. Transporters can be active: coupled to ATP hydrolysis (pumps) or to the downhill flow of another solute, they can move a solute uphill.

2. Describe the structure of the K⁺ channel selectivity filter and explain how it lets K⁺ through but keeps Na⁺ out. [ELO 2]

Model answer

Four identical subunits surround a central pore. Near the outer end the pore narrows into the selectivity filter, about 1.2 nm long, lined by the backbone carbonyl oxygens of a conserved sequence (TVGYG). In water, each ion is surrounded by water oxygens; to enter the narrow filter it must lose this water, which costs energy. The carbonyl oxygens are held rigidly at the distance at which they replace the water around a K⁺ ion, so the energy is paid back. Na⁺ is smaller; the oxygens are too far away to replace its water, so dehydration is not compensated and Na⁺ does not enter. The structure was solved by X-ray crystallography (MacKinnon, KcsA, 1998).

3. Name the three main ways a channel can be gated. For each, give one example and say what opens it. [ELO 2]

Model answer

Voltage-gated: e.g. the voltage-gated Na⁺ channel of nerve and muscle; a helix with positive charges (the voltage sensor) moves outwards when the membrane depolarises and opens the gate. Ligand-gated: e.g. the nicotinic acetylcholine receptor at the nerve–muscle junction; binding of two acetylcholine molecules opens a cation pore. Mechanically gated: e.g. Piezo channels; tension in the membrane flattens the channel’s blades and opens the pore (Piezo2 in touch, Piezo1 in sensing blood flow).

4. Derive the Nernst equation from the Gibbs energy for moving one mole of an ion with charge \(z\) from outside to inside a cell. [ELO 2]

Model answer

Chemical part: \(\Delta G = \Delta G^\circ + RT\ln Q\) with \(\Delta G^\circ = 0\) (same ion on both sides) and \(Q = c_\text{in}/c_\text{out}\), so \(RT\ln(c_\text{in}/c_\text{out})\). Electrical part: moving \(z\) moles of charge through the membrane potential \(V = V_\text{in} - V_\text{out}\) costs \(zFV\). Total: \(\Delta G = RT\ln(c_\text{in}/c_\text{out}) + zFV\). At equilibrium there is no net flow, so \(\Delta G = 0\), which gives \[E = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{c_\text{out}}{c_\text{in}}.\]

5. Calculate the equilibrium potentials for K⁺ (inside 140 mM, outside 5 mM) and Cl⁻ (inside 10 mM, outside 110 mM) at 37 °C. Explain the sign of each. [ELO 2]

Model answer

\(E_\text{K} = 26.7 \times \ln(5/140) = 26.7 \times (-3.33) \approx\) −89 mV. K⁺ is higher inside and tends to leave; the inside must be negative to hold the positive ion back.

\(E_\text{Cl} = \frac{26.7}{-1} \times \ln(110/10) = -26.7 \times 2.40 \approx\) −64 mV. Cl⁻ is higher outside and tends to enter; because it is negative, a negative inside repels it, so equilibrium is reached at a negative potential.

6. Explain how the resting membrane potential arises. Include the roles of the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, K⁺ leak channels and large intracellular anions. [ELO 2]

Model answer

The Na⁺/K⁺ pump uses ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, building high K⁺ inside and high Na⁺ outside (it also contributes a few mV directly, because it is electrogenic). The resting membrane has many open K⁺ leak channels and few open Na⁺ channels. K⁺ flows out down its concentration gradient; the large anions inside (proteins, phosphates, organic molecules) cannot follow. Each K⁺ that leaves adds a positive charge outside and leaves a negative charge inside, building a thin layer of charge at the membrane. The voltage grows until the electrical pull balances the concentration push, close to \(E_\text{K}\). The small Na⁺ leak makes it a bit less negative (about −70 mV): the resting potential is a permeability-weighted average (Goldman). Very few ions need to move (fewer than 1 in 10 000 of the K⁺ ions), so concentrations hardly change.

7. Describe the four phases of an action potential and which channels cause each. [ELO 2]

Model answer
  1. A stimulus depolarises the membrane to threshold (about −55 mV). 2. Voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open; Na⁺ rushes in, depolarising the membrane further and opening more Na⁺ channels (positive feedback); the voltage rises towards \(E_\text{Na}\) (peak about +35 mV). 3. Na⁺ channels inactivate and slower voltage-gated K⁺ channels open; K⁺ flows out and the voltage falls. 4. Undershoot towards \(E_\text{K}\) while K⁺ channels are still open; inactivated Na⁺ channels cannot yet reopen (refractory period), so the spike travels in one direction. The whole spike lasts about 1–2 ms and moves very few ions; the pump restores the gradients over time.

8. Describe the patch-clamp method. What is a gigaseal, and what does a single-channel recording show? [ELO 1] [ELO 2]

Model answer

A glass pipette with a tip about 1 µm wide, filled with salt solution and containing an electrode, is pressed against a cell, and gentle suction is applied. The membrane seals to the glass with a resistance above 1 GΩ (gigaseal), so no current leaks around the tip and the only current measured flows through channels in the membrane patch under the pipette. An amplifier holds the voltage constant (clamp) and measures the current. In the cell-attached configuration the cell stays intact. A single-channel recording shows the current jumping in square steps of a few pA between closed and fully open; open and closed times are random, and gating signals change the open probability. Developed by Neher and Sakmann (Nobel Prize 1991).

9. Explain how CFTR in airway epithelial cells keeps the airway surface wet, and why mucus becomes thick in cystic fibrosis. [ELO 2] [ELO 9]

Model answer

CFTR is a Cl⁻ channel in the apical membrane, opened by PKA phosphorylation and ATP binding. When open, Cl⁻ flows out into the airway lumen. Na⁺ follows between the cells (attracted by the negative charge), and water follows the salt by osmosis. This keeps a liquid layer under the mucus deep enough for the cilia to beat and move the mucus out of the lungs. In cystic fibrosis CFTR is missing or does not open, so little Cl⁻ and water are secreted; the liquid layer becomes thin, mucus becomes thick and sticky, cilia cannot clear it, and bacteria cause chronic infections.

VG-style questions

10. [VG] A patient’s blood K⁺ rises from 5 mM to 10 mM (intracellular K⁺ stays at 140 mM). (a) Calculate the new \(E_\text{K}\). (b) Predict what happens to the resting potential of heart muscle cells and explain why. (c) Explain why this can be dangerous, using what you know about voltage-gated Na⁺ channels. [ELO 2]

Model answer

  1. \(E_\text{K} = 26.7 \times \ln(10/140) = 26.7 \times (-2.64) \approx\) −70 mV (instead of −89 mV).

  2. The resting membrane is mostly permeable to K⁺, so the resting potential follows \(E_\text{K}\): it becomes less negative (depolarised), roughly 15–20 mV.

  3. A sustained depolarisation brings the membrane closer to threshold, so cells can fire too easily at first, but it also inactivates voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, which cannot recover while the membrane stays depolarised. With fewer available Na⁺ channels, action potentials become smaller and conduct more slowly, and the heart’s rhythm can fail (arrhythmia, in severe cases cardiac arrest). This is why blood K⁺ is monitored closely in kidney failure and when K⁺ is given intravenously.

11. [VG] In the sweat gland duct, CFTR reabsorbs Cl⁻ from sweat into the cell, while in the airways it secretes Cl⁻ out of the cell. (a) How can the same channel move Cl⁻ in opposite directions? (b) Explain why people with cystic fibrosis have salty sweat. [ELO 2] [ELO 9]

Model answer

  1. A channel only lets ions flow down their electrochemical gradient; it has no direction of its own. The direction depends on the Cl⁻ concentrations and the membrane potential in each tissue. In airway cells, transporters load Cl⁻ into the cell above its equilibrium, so when CFTR opens, Cl⁻ flows out into the lumen. In the sweat duct, the sweat starts out with a high NaCl concentration (similar to plasma), so Cl⁻ flows from the lumen into the duct cells (with Na⁺ through Na⁺ channels) and is returned to the blood.

  2. Without working CFTR in the duct, Cl⁻ (and therefore Na⁺) cannot be reabsorbed, so the sweat reaching the skin stays salty. The sweat test measures this: Cl⁻ ≥ 60 mmol/L indicates cystic fibrosis.

12. [VG] A new compound blocks pain in an animal model. You suspect it blocks voltage-gated Na⁺ channels, like lidocaine. Design patch-clamp experiments to test (a) whether it blocks Na⁺ channels, (b) whether it acts from the inside or the outside of the membrane, and (c) whether, like lidocaine, it blocks more strongly when channels open often. [ELO 1] [ELO 2]

Model answer

  1. Whole-cell recording from cells expressing the Na⁺ channel (e.g. cultured sensory neurons or a cell line expressing it). Depolarise with voltage steps and measure the inward Na⁺ current before and after adding the compound; a dose–response curve gives the IC₅₀. Controls: vehicle alone; a known blocker (tetrodotoxin or lidocaine); test other channels (e.g. K⁺ currents) to check specificity.

  2. Use excised patches: in an inside-out patch, add the compound to the bath, which now faces the cytosolic side; in an outside-out patch, the bath faces the extracellular side. Block from one side only shows the site of action. A permanently charged version of the compound, which cannot cross the membrane, is a useful extra test.

  3. Apply trains of depolarising pulses at low (e.g. 0.1 Hz) and high frequency (e.g. 10 Hz). If block builds up from pulse to pulse at high frequency (use-dependent block), the compound binds preferentially to open or inactivated channels, as lidocaine does. This property would let it block busy pain fibres more than normal nerves.

E5. Tick the particle view and choose Resting cell. Compare how many K⁺ ions cross out and in per second, and how many Na⁺ ions come in (average over 10 s; the counts are random). How do the numbers show that the net charge flow is zero at \(V_m\)? Then choose Na⁺ channels open: what changes? [ELO 2]

Solutions

E1. \(E_\text{K} = -89\) mV, \(E_\text{Na} = +67\) mV, \(E_\text{Cl} = -64\) mV and \(V_m = -70\) mV, as in the lecture. Driving forces: K⁺ \(+19\) mV (net flow out), Na⁺ \(-136\) mV (net flow in), Cl⁻ \(-6\) mV (almost at equilibrium; a tiny net flow out). Na⁺ is pushed hardest, but few Na⁺ channels are open, so its flow is small. At \(V_m\) the positive charge that leaves as K⁺ equals the positive charge that enters as Na⁺ (plus the little Cl⁻ that leaves): the net current is zero, so the voltage stays put. This is exactly how the Goldman equation is defined. The ions themselves keep leaking, and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump keeps the gradients.

E2. With 8 mM outside, \(E_\text{K}\) rises from −89 to −76.5 mV and \(V_m\) from −70 to −63.5 mV: the cell is depolarised by about 6 mV. With 10 mM: \(E_\text{K}\) −70.5 mV, \(V_m\) −59.9 mV. With 3 mM (hypokalaemia): \(E_\text{K}\) −102.7 mV, \(V_m\) −75.2 mV (hyperpolarised). \(V_m\) moves less than \(E_\text{K}\) because it is a weighted average: the Na⁺ and Cl⁻ channels, whose \(E\) did not change, still pull on it. In heart muscle, a depolarised resting potential inactivates part of the Na⁺ channels and disturbs the rhythm, which is why high blood K⁺ is dangerous.

E3. \(V_m\) is about +36 mV (the lecture’s action-potential peak), well above 0 but not at \(E_\text{Na}\) (+67 mV), because the K⁺ (and Cl⁻) channels are still open and pull towards their own \(E\). Na⁺ channels must outnumber K⁺ channels much more: at 30 (relative units) \(V_m\) is +57 mV, at 100 it is +63 mV. With lidocaine the voltage stays at −70 mV: the Na⁺ channels are told to open, but their pores are blocked, so only the resting leak remains and there is no action potential. A pain fibre then sends no signal to the brain. (In real cells lidocaine blocks part of the channels and binds best to channels that open often; the demo blocks all of them.)

E4. CFTR closed: \(E_\text{Cl} = -21\) mV, \(V_m = -61.6\) mV. CFTR open: $V_m = $ −28.8 mV, close to \(E_\text{Cl}\); Cl⁻ flows out of the cell (the table: net flow out), and K⁺ out flow increases too. In an airway cell, this Cl⁻ leaves through the apical membrane into the airway, Na⁺ follows between the cells and water follows by osmosis. With only 10 mM Cl⁻ inside, \(E_\text{Cl} = -64\) mV, and opening the same channels moves \(V_m\) only from −70 to −65.8 mV. The rule: opening channels for an ion moves \(V_m\) towards that ion’s \(E\); how far and in which direction depends on where \(E\) lies, which is set by the concentrations. Without CFTR (cystic fibrosis) the Cl⁻ secretion and the water that follows it are lost.

E5. At rest about 6 K⁺ ions cross out and 3 cross in per second (net about 3 out), about 3 Na⁺ come in and almost none go out, and Cl⁻ crosses about equally in both directions (the rates are scaled to be watchable). The net K⁺ outflow equals the net Na⁺ inflow (plus a small Cl⁻ outflow): no net charge flows. Note that K⁺ crosses in both directions: equilibrium is not “no movement” but “equal movement both ways”. With Na⁺ channels open, many Na⁺ ions flood in, and the K⁺ outflow rises to match them, because \(V_m\) is now far above \(E_\text{K}\); the counts are scaled down so that you can still follow them.
