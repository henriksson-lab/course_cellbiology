Study questions: when cell biology goes wrong

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Name the five categories of the disease framework from the lecture. Explain why a recessive disease is usually a loss of function, while a dominant disease is often a gain of function or a toxic aggregate. [ELO 9]

Model answer

Loss of function, gain of function, toxic aggregate, wrong place, wrong amount (or wrong time). In a recessive disease one normal copy is enough for health: half the normal amount of protein still does the job, so disease appears only when both copies are lost, which means the problem is a missing function. In a dominant disease one mutant copy causes disease even though a normal copy is present. That is easiest to explain if the mutant protein does something harmful: it is overactive (HCM myosin, PCSK9), it sticks in the wrong place (progerin in the lamina), or it aggregates (TTR, α-synuclein in rare families). (Some dominant diseases are caused by a lost copy when half the amount is not enough, but these are less common.)

2. Cystic fibrosis is caused by more than 2000 different mutations in CFTR. Describe three kinds of molecular defect, and for each say which kind of drug can help (if any). [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. No protein: e.g. a premature stop codon; the mRNA is degraded and nothing is made. CFTR modulators cannot help, because there is no protein to act on; gene or mRNA therapy would be needed. (2) Misfolding: F508del; the protein is recognised by ER quality control and degraded by ERAD, so it never reaches the apical membrane. A corrector (elexacaftor, tezacaftor) helps it fold and leave the ER; it is combined with a potentiator because rescued F508del opens poorly. (3) Gating defect: G551D; the protein reaches the apical membrane but the gate rarely opens. A potentiator (ivacaftor) holds the gate open.

3. In α1-antitrypsin deficiency (Z variant), patients can get both emphysema and liver cirrhosis. Explain both from one molecular defect, and place each in the disease framework. [ELO 9]

Model answer

The Z mutation makes α1-antitrypsin fold slowly in the ER of liver cells, and the partly folded molecules link into polymers that stay in the ER. Lung: too little α1-antitrypsin reaches the blood, so neutrophil elastase in the lungs is not inhibited and digests the walls of the air sacs (emphysema). This is a loss of function caused by the protein being in the wrong place. Liver: the polymers accumulate in the ER and damage the liver cells (cirrhosis). This is a toxic aggregate (gain of toxic function). Infusions of purified α1-antitrypsin help the lungs but not the liver.

4. Describe three different molecular defects that all cause familial hypercholesterolaemia. Why do they all raise blood LDL? [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. No LDL receptor is made (or it misfolds and stays in the ER). (2) A mutation in the receptor’s cytoplasmic tail: the receptor binds LDL at the surface but is not collected into clathrin-coated pits, so LDL is not taken in. (3) A gain-of-function PCSK9: PCSK9 binds the receptor and sends it to the lysosome instead of recycling to the surface. In all three, fewer working receptors remove LDL from the blood in the liver, so LDL stays high. Blood LDL is set by the number of receptors that cycle through the liver cell surface.

5. Compare Gaucher disease and I-cell disease. What is missing, where do the lysosomal enzymes end up, and how many enzymes are affected? [ELO 9]

Model answer

Gaucher disease: one lysosomal enzyme, glucocerebrosidase, is defective. All other enzymes reach the lysosome normally. Its substrate, glucosylceramide, piles up in the lysosomes of macrophages (Gaucher cells). I-cell disease: the enzyme in the cis-Golgi that adds the mannose-6-phosphate tag is missing. All lysosomal enzymes are made and active, but without the tag the M6P receptor in the trans-Golgi network cannot sort them, so they are secreted: high in blood, low inside cells. Dozens of enzymes are missing from the lysosome at once, and many substrates pile up. Gaucher is a loss of function; I-cell disease is a wrong-place disease.

6. What is amyloid, and why do we think that the aggregating protein causes Alzheimer’s and Parkinson’s disease rather than being just a marker of dying neurons? [ELO 9]

Model answer

Amyloid is a very stable, ordered aggregate in which each protein chain forms a β-strand lying across a long fibril, with strands from many chains stacked together. It is hard for chaperones and the proteasome to clear. Evidence that the aggregating protein is a cause: rare inherited forms of the diseases are caused by mutations in the aggregating protein or its processing (APP and presenilin in Alzheimer’s, α-synuclein in Parkinson’s), and these families get the disease early. Also, small aggregates are toxic to cells in culture, and aggregates can seed misfolding of normal protein. Antibodies that remove Aβ slow the decline a little.

7. Explain how one silent base change in the lamin A gene causes progeria, and how lonafarnib acts. [ELO 9]

Model answer

Lamin A is made as prelamin A, which gets a farnesyl lipid at its C-terminus; a protease then cuts off the end with the lipid, and mature lamin A joins the nuclear lamina. In progeria, the silent base change creates a new splice site, so 50 amino acids, including the cut site, are lost from the protein. The farnesyl stays on, and the protein (progerin) remains stuck to the inner nuclear membrane. It disturbs the lamina, and nuclei become misshapen. One copy is enough: a dominant gain of function. Lonafarnib inhibits farnesyltransferase, so less progerin carries the lipid; it extends life by a few years.

8. A woman with a mitochondrial DNA mutation has muscle weakness and hearing loss; her mother has only mild diabetes. Explain (a) why the disease comes from the mother, (b) why mother and daughter differ, (c) why muscle and ear are affected. [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. Mitochondria, and their DNA, are inherited only from the egg; sperm mitochondria are lost. (b) Each cell has hundreds to thousands of mtDNA copies, and the mutation is in only some of them (heteroplasmy). The fraction passed to each egg varies, and it differs between tissues. Disease appears when the mutant fraction in a tissue passes a threshold. (c) Muscle, brain, the inner ear and the heart need the most ATP from oxidative phosphorylation; mtDNA encodes 13 respiratory-chain proteins plus the tRNAs and rRNAs to make them, so these tissues fail first. Blood lactate rises because cells rely on glycolysis.

9. Match each therapy type to the disease it is used for, and say where in the cell (or body) it acts: (a) enzyme replacement, (b) antibody, (c) antisense oligonucleotide, (d) siRNA, (e) gene therapy, (f) small-molecule inhibitor. Diseases: SMA, Gaucher disease, familial hypercholesterolaemia, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, TTR amyloidosis. [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. Gaucher: imiglucerase, taken up by endocytosis, acts in the lysosome. (b) FH: anti-PCSK9 antibodies bind PCSK9 in the blood. (c) SMA: nusinersen changes splicing of SMN2 pre-mRNA in the nucleus (also TTR amyloidosis: inotersen, which destroys TTR mRNA). (d) TTR amyloidosis: patisiran destroys TTR mRNA in liver cells (also FH: inclisiran against PCSK9 mRNA). (e) SMA: onasemnogene delivers SMN1 DNA to the nucleus of motor neurons. (f) HCM: mavacamten inhibits the myosin ATPase in the heart muscle cell.

VG-style questions

10. [VG] A child has very high LDL cholesterol. Sequencing finds no mutation in the LDL receptor gene. Design an experiment with the patient’s cells to decide between (a) a receptor that is made but not taken in, and (b) an overactive PCSK9. Predict the result for each. [ELO 9] [VG]

Model answer

A normal receptor gene already argues against (a), which needs a tail mutation in the receptor itself; but the test is useful in general. Grow the patient’s skin fibroblasts (or liver-like cells made from stem cells) and measure labelled LDL binding at 4 °C (surface receptors only, no endocytosis) and uptake and degradation at 37 °C. (a) Tail mutation: binding normal, uptake very low; receptors are spread over the surface instead of in coated pits (immunostaining or EM). (b) PCSK9 gain of function: PCSK9 comes from outside, so add the patient’s PCSK9 (or medium from cells expressing the mutant PCSK9) to normal cells. Surface receptor number falls, so both binding and uptake fall, and receptor protein disappears faster (pulse–chase or Western blot over time); a lysosome inhibitor (e.g. raising lysosomal pH) or an anti-PCSK9 antibody prevents the loss. Controls: normal cells with normal PCSK9, and no PCSK9.

11. [VG] A new dominant disease is found: a mutant K⁺ channel that stays open too long in heart muscle cells. A colleague suggests gene therapy that adds a normal copy of the gene. Evaluate the idea and propose two better therapy types, with reasons. [ELO 9] [VG]

Model answer

A dominant disease with a channel that is too active is a gain of function. The patient already has one normal copy; adding another normal copy does not remove the harmful mutant channel, so gene therapy that adds a gene is unlikely to help (it might dilute the mutant subunits a little if channels are made of several subunits, but not remove them). Better: (1) a small-molecule channel blocker (inhibitor) that reduces the current through the overactive channels, as mavacamten turns down overactive myosin; (2) an allele-specific siRNA or ASO that destroys only the mutant mRNA, so only normal channels are made. Gene editing that corrects or switches off the mutant allele would also fit. Any therapy must reach heart muscle cells and be tested for effects on the heart rhythm.

12. [VG] Children with spinal muscular atrophy who have four copies of SMN2 usually have a much milder disease than children with two copies. (a) Explain why. (b) Predict what an siRNA against SMN2 mRNA would do in a patient. (c) Why is nusinersen injected into the spinal fluid rather than into a vein? [ELO 9] [VG]

Model answer
  1. Each SMN2 copy makes a little full-length SMN (about 10 % of the normal output of SMN1, because exon 7 is kept in about 1 of 10 transcripts). Four copies make about twice as much as two, which is enough to keep more motor neurons alive. This is a wrong amount disease: the amount of protein decides severity. (b) An siRNA would destroy SMN2 mRNA and remove the little SMN that is left: the disease would get worse. The right RNA tool is one that changes splicing (an ASO such as nusinersen), not one that destroys the mRNA. (c) The target cells are motor neurons in the spinal cord, behind the blood–brain barrier. An oligonucleotide is a large, charged molecule that does not cross it, so it is injected into the spinal fluid, from which it reaches the neurons.

Explore

Diagnose the defect. Ten patients, from the clinic to the molecule. Each case starts with one finding; ask for more findings at any time (they cost nothing). Then pick the process in the cell that is broken. A wrong or half-right choice explains why and lets you try again. When you find the process, the disease, its category in the framework and the lecture where you learned the cell biology are shown. Then pick a therapy type that fits; you can click the other therapy types afterwards to read why they fit or not. Points: 3 for the right process at the first try (2 at the second, 1 later), plus 2 for a fitting therapy at the first try (1 later); 50 in total. Each case is a typical textbook patient, and “fits” means the therapy type that acts on the broken step or the best one available today, not a full treatment plan.

E1. Solve cases 1 and 2. Both patients have cystic fibrosis and nearly the same sweat chloride. Which finding tells the two cases apart? Which process and which therapy type fit each case, and why does the potentiator only half fit case 1? [ELO 9]

E2. Predict first. Open case 3 and reveal findings up to and including the Cell finding, but not the Molecule finding. Which process is broken? Now try Lysosomal digestion and Sorting: why are they only partly right? Then choose a therapy type. How many therapy types fit, and why is a statin only partly right? [ELO 9]

E3. Solve cases 4 and 5. Both patients have swollen lysosomes. Which single finding best tells the two diseases apart? Why does enzyme replacement fit case 4 but not case 5? [ELO 9]

E4. [VG] Solve all ten cases and note the category chips. Sort the cases into loss of function, gain of function or toxic aggregate, wrong place and wrong amount. Which therapy types fit the loss-of-function cases, and which fit the gain-of-function and aggregate cases? Find the one loss-of-function case where an inhibitor also fits, and explain why that is not a contradiction. [ELO 9] [VG]

E5. [VG] A new patient has had a wet cough and chest infections since birth (compare cases 1 and 7). Predict which one finding you would ask for first to tell cystic fibrosis from primary ciliary dyskinesia, and what result you expect in each disease. Then explain why gene therapy is only “partly” right for case 7, although it is a loss-of-function disease. [ELO 9] [VG]

Solutions

E1. The Cell finding (the Western blot) separates them: case 1 has only band B (the ER form), case 2 has normal band C and CFTR in the apical membrane; patch clamp in case 2 shows that the channel opens about 100 times less often. Case 1: protein folding and quality control (F508del misfolds and is degraded by ERAD); therapy: corrector (fits). Case 2: ion channel (G551D, gate shut); therapy: potentiator (fits; ivacaftor). In case 1 the potentiator is only half right because almost no F508del CFTR reaches the surface, so a potentiator alone has little to act on; correctors and a potentiator are given together. (In case 1, Ion channel and Sorting are “partly”: Cl⁻ transport is lost and the protein does not reach the surface, but the cause is misfolding.)

E2. The Cell finding says the receptor is made normally and reaches the surface, but after endocytosis it goes to the lysosome instead of back to the surface: endocytosis and receptor recycling is broken. Lysosomal digestion is only partly right because the lysosome works normally; it digests what it is sent. Sorting is only partly right because the receptor is mis-sorted after uptake, in the endosome, not on the way out of the Golgi. Two therapy types fit: an antibody against PCSK9 (evolocumab, alirocumab) and an siRNA against PCSK9 mRNA (inclisiran). A statin (small-molecule inhibitor) is “partly”: it is the first drug given and raises receptor numbers, but it does not act on the defect, and it even raises PCSK9.

E3. The best single finding is the enzyme levels: in case 4 one enzyme (glucocerebrosidase) is low, the others normal; in case 5 many lysosomal enzymes are low inside cells and high in blood plasma. Case 4 is a defect of lysosomal digestion; case 5 is a sorting defect (no M6P tag). Enzyme replacement fits case 4 because one soluble enzyme is missing and macrophages take up the infused enzyme by endocytosis and deliver it to the lysosome. In case 5, dozens of enzymes are missing at once, and the cells’ own enzymes keep being lost to the blood, so replacing them all is not practical.

E4. Loss of function: cases 1 (also wrong place), 2, 4, 7, 9 and 10 (also wrong amount). Gain of function: cases 3 (PCSK9; also wrong amount) and 8 (myosin). Toxic aggregate: case 6 (also gain of function). Wrong place: cases 1 and 5. Wrong amount: cases 3 and 10. Fitting therapies for loss of function add something back: corrector (1), potentiator (2), enzyme replacement (4), ASO that fixes splicing and gene therapy (10), or, where nothing reaches the cause yet, supportive care (5, 7, 9). For gain of function and aggregates they take something away: antibody and siRNA (3), inhibitor (8), siRNA, ASO and a stabiliser (6). The exception is case 4: the inhibitor eliglustat blocks glucosylceramide synthesis, so less substrate is made and the weak enzyme can keep up (substrate reduction). It does not inhibit the broken protein; it lowers the load on it. (Case 6 has the opposite twist: a small molecule that stabilises a protein is used against a toxic protein, because it keeps TTR from falling apart.)

E5. Ask for the sweat test (a body-fluid finding). Cystic fibrosis: sweat chloride ≥ 60 mmol/L (98 and 105 mmol/L in cases 1 and 2). Primary ciliary dyskinesia: normal (25 mmol/L in case 7), because CFTR works. A chest X-ray with the heart on the right, or high-speed video of nasal cilia, also points to PCD. Gene therapy is the right idea for a missing protein, but it is only partly right in practice: the DNAH5 coding sequence is about 14 kb, too large for the AAV vector (about 4.7 kb), and the gene would have to reach every ciliated cell in the airways. So care today treats the consequences.
