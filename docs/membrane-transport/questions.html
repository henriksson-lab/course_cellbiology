Study questions: moving molecules across membranes

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Which kinds of molecules cross a pure lipid bilayer, and which do not? Explain the rule with the structure of the bilayer, and give one example of each kind. [ELO 2]

Model answer

The middle of the bilayer is a hydrophobic layer of fatty acid tails. A molecule crosses only if it can leave water and dissolve in this layer. Small hydrophobic molecules cross fast (O₂, CO₂, steroid hormones). Small uncharged polar molecules cross more slowly (water, urea). Larger polar molecules cross very slowly (glucose). Ions practically do not cross (Na⁺, K⁺), because their charge and their shell of water make it very costly to enter the oily core; a pure bilayer is about 10⁹ times more permeable to water than to Na⁺ or K⁺. Large and charged molecules (ATP, proteins) do not cross. Everything that does not cross fast enough needs a transport protein.

2. Give the typical concentrations of Na⁺, K⁺ and Ca²⁺ inside and outside a mammalian cell. Explain why these differences are called a steady state and not an equilibrium. [ELO 2]

Model answer

Na⁺: about 12 mM inside, 145 mM outside. K⁺: about 140 mM inside, 5 mM outside. Ca²⁺: about 100 nM (0.0001 mM) free in the cytosol, about 2 mM outside. At equilibrium there would be no net movement and no energy needed. Here ions leak down their gradients all the time, and pumps (Na⁺/K⁺ pump, Ca²⁺ pumps) spend ATP all the time to move them back. The concentrations stay constant only because leak and pumping balance: a steady state. If ATP runs out, the gradients disappear.

3. Compare a channel and a transporter. Give at least four differences. [ELO 2]

Model answer
Channel Transporter
How it works water-filled pore, open to both sides at once when open binds the solute; alternating access, open to one side at a time
Speed up to about 10⁸ ions/s about 10²–10⁴ molecules/s
Direction only down the (electro)chemical gradient passive, or active when coupled to ATP or another gradient
Kinetics rate rises with the gradient, practically no saturation saturates (Vmax, Km) like an enzyme
Control gated (opens and closes) regulated, e.g. by number of transporters in the membrane

Examples: K⁺ channel; GLUT1, SGLT1, Na⁺/K⁺ pump.

4. Describe the alternating-access mechanism of a uniporter such as GLUT1. Why can GLUT1 not move glucose against its gradient? [ELO 2]

Model answer

The transporter has a glucose-binding site in the middle of the membrane. (1) It is open to the outside; glucose binds. (2) The outer gate closes, so glucose is trapped (occluded). (3) The protein opens to the inside and glucose is released. (4) The empty transporter returns to the outward-open shape. The binding site is never open to both sides at once. Each step is reversible and no energy is put in, so the cycle runs equally well in both directions. The net flow is therefore from the side with more glucose to the side with less: always downhill.

5. Explain the difference between primary and secondary active transport. Use the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and SGLT1 as examples. [ELO 2]

Model answer

In primary active transport the transport protein uses energy directly, usually by hydrolysing ATP. The Na⁺/K⁺ pump hydrolyses one ATP per cycle to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in, against both gradients. In secondary active transport the protein uses the energy stored in another ion gradient, which a primary pump made. SGLT1 lets 2 Na⁺ flow into the cell, down their electrochemical gradient, and couples this to moving 1 glucose into the cell, even against the glucose gradient. SGLT1 uses no ATP itself, but if the Na⁺/K⁺ pump stops, the Na⁺ gradient runs down and SGLT1 stops too.

6. Describe the reaction cycle of the Na⁺/K⁺ pump in four steps. Why is it called electrogenic? [ELO 2]

Model answer
  1. Open to the cytosol, high affinity for Na⁺: 3 Na⁺ bind. (2) ATP phosphorylates an aspartate in the pump; the pump switches to the outward-open shape with low Na⁺ affinity and releases 3 Na⁺ outside. (3) 2 K⁺ bind from the outside; this triggers removal of the phosphate. (4) The pump returns to the inward-open shape and releases 2 K⁺ into the cytosol. Each cycle moves 3 positive charges out and 2 in, so one net positive charge leaves the cell: the pump produces a small electric current and is electrogenic.

7. Name three families of ATP-driven pumps and give one example and one function for each. Which related machine runs “backwards”, and what does it make? [ELO 2] [ELO 5]

Model answer

P-type pumps (phosphorylate themselves): the Na⁺/K⁺ pump, which keeps Na⁺ low and K⁺ high in the cell; the Ca²⁺ pumps (in the plasma membrane and SERCA in the ER), which keep cytosolic Ca²⁺ at about 100 nM. V-type pumps: the H⁺ pump that acidifies lysosomes (pH 4.5–5) and endosomes. ABC transporters: P-glycoprotein, which exports drugs and other hydrophobic molecules from cells. The F-type ATP synthase in the inner mitochondrial membrane is built like a V-type pump but runs in reverse: H⁺ flowing down its gradient into the matrix drives the synthesis of ATP.

8. Draw a gut epithelial cell and show how glucose is moved from the gut lumen to the blood. Label the apical and basolateral membranes, the transporters, the direction of each flow, and the tight junctions. [ELO 2]

Model answer

Your sketch should show: apical membrane (with microvilli) facing the lumen, carrying SGLT1: 2 Na⁺ + 1 glucose into the cell. Basolateral membrane facing the blood, carrying GLUT2: glucose out of the cell, down its gradient; and the Na⁺/K⁺ pump: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in, using ATP. Tight junctions near the apical end between neighbouring cells, sealing the gap so glucose cannot leak back, and keeping apical and basolateral proteins in their own membrane. Glucose concentration in the cell is high enough to drive it out through GLUT2.

9. What are aquaporins, and how do cells move water if no pump for water exists? [ELO 2]

Model answer

Aquaporins are water channels: four subunits, each with a narrow pore through which water passes in single file, up to about 10⁹ molecules per second. The pore excludes ions, including H⁺, so gradients are not lost. Water always moves passively by osmosis, towards the side with the higher concentration of dissolved particles. Cells therefore move water by moving solutes first (for example Na⁺ with pumps and transporters); water follows through aquaporins. In the kidney collecting duct, vasopressin causes AQP2 to be inserted into the membrane, so more water is taken back.

VG-style questions

10. [VG] Researchers treat isolated intestinal epithelium with ouabain on the blood side. Predict what happens over the next hour to (a) Na⁺ inside the cells, (b) glucose uptake from the lumen, and (c) glucose transport into the blood. Explain each step. What result would you expect if ouabain were added only on the lumen side? [ELO 2]

Model answer

Ouabain blocks the Na⁺/K⁺ pump from the extracellular side; in epithelial cells the pump is in the basolateral membrane, so ouabain on the blood side reaches it. (a) Na⁺ leaks in (including through SGLT1) but is no longer pumped out: Na⁺ inside rises and K⁺ falls; the membrane potential also becomes less negative. (b) SGLT1 depends on the inward Na⁺ gradient (and the negative inside). As the gradient runs down, glucose uptake from the lumen falls, and SGLT1 can no longer concentrate glucose. (c) Less glucose reaches the cell, so less glucose crosses to the blood through GLUT2, even though GLUT2 itself is not blocked. On the lumen side only, ouabain would have little or no effect at first, because there are no pumps in the apical membrane and tight junctions stop the drug from reaching the basolateral side. This experiment shows both the coupling of secondary to primary active transport and the polarity of the epithelium.

11. [VG] A new protein, X, is suspected to be a transporter for the amino acid leucine. Design experiments to show (a) that leucine uptake depends on X, (b) that X itself is enough to transport leucine, and (c) whether X is a uniporter or a Na⁺-coupled symporter. [ELO 2] [ELO 1]

Model answer
  1. Measure the initial rate of uptake of radiolabelled (or fluorescent) leucine at many concentrations in cells with X and in cells where X is knocked out (CRISPR) or knocked down, and in cells that overexpress X. If X is the transporter, uptake saturates (Vmax, Km), is lower without X and higher with more X, and is competed by unlabelled leucine but not by an unrelated molecule. (b) Purify X and reconstitute it in liposomes. Leucine should enter liposomes that contain X, but not empty liposomes; since nothing else is present, X is sufficient. (c) Repeat uptake with Na⁺ replaced by another cation (e.g. choline⁺ or K⁺) outside. A Na⁺-coupled symporter stops working (or slows strongly) without external Na⁺, and it can accumulate leucine above the outside concentration; in liposomes, uptake should depend on an imposed Na⁺ gradient. A uniporter works without Na⁺ and only brings the inside up to the outside concentration. Controls: same cell number, short time points (initial rates), cold or no-transporter background.

12. [VG] Explain how (a) digoxin makes the heart beat more strongly, and (b) P-glycoprotein can make a tumour resistant to several unrelated anticancer drugs at the same time. In each case, name the transport protein and say what it normally does. [ELO 2] [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. Digoxin partly blocks the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (normally: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP) in heart muscle cells. Na⁺ inside rises a little, so the Na⁺ gradient is smaller. The Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger, which normally uses Na⁺ flowing in to push Ca²⁺ out, therefore removes less Ca²⁺. More Ca²⁺ stays in the cell and is stored in the sarcoplasmic reticulum, so more Ca²⁺ is released at each beat and contraction is stronger. Because the pump is in every cell, too much digoxin is toxic, and the safe dose range is narrow. (b) P-glycoprotein (ABCB1) is an ABC transporter that uses ATP to export hydrophobic molecules from cells; it normally protects tissues such as the gut, liver and the blood–brain barrier from toxins. Its binding cavity is large and accepts many different hydrophobic molecules. A cancer cell that makes a lot of P-glycoprotein pumps out several unrelated chemotherapy drugs, so their concentration inside the cell stays too low to kill it: multidrug resistance.

E1. Choose Bilayer only and press Run assay series. Then choose GLUT (12 transporters) and run it again. Describe the two curves. Read \(V_\text{max}\) and \(K_m\) for GLUT. What limits the rate at 20 mM in each case? [ELO 2]

E2. Predict first. What will happen to \(V_\text{max}\) and to \(K_m\) if the cell has twice as many GLUT transporters (24)? Run the assay to check. [ELO 2]

E3. Live: GLUT, outside glucose 5 mM. Watch the inside glucose for 2–3 minutes (use 10× faster). Where does it level off, and why does uptake stop? Then switch to SGLT with outside glucose 1 mM (Pause, Reset, set, Run). What is the inside concentration after 2 minutes? Where does the energy for this come from? Compare “highest possible ratio” for GLUT and SGLT. [ELO 2]

E4. [VG] Predict first. Let SGLT (outside glucose 1 mM) run for 2 minutes, then untick the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (as ouabain would). Predict what happens to the Na⁺ inside, the voltage, the highest possible ratio and the glucose inside. Then watch. Why does SGLT stop working although ouabain does not bind to SGLT? What happens if you set outside Na⁺ to 0 instead (Na⁺ replaced by another cation)? [ELO 2]

E5. [VG] Run the assay for SGLT (12 transporters). Then tick gliflozin and run it again. Then untick gliflozin, set outside Na⁺ to 20 mM and run it a third time. Compare \(V_\text{max}\) and \(K_m\) in the three runs and explain each change. How does the gliflozin result explain why glucose appears in the urine of patients who take the drug? [ELO 2]

Solutions

E1. Bilayer only: the points lie on a straight line through 0 (slope about 0.09 mM/min per mM; only about 1.6 mM/min at 20 mM): Fick’s law, rate proportional to the concentration difference, no maximum. GLUT: the curve rises steeply at first and then flattens: the fit gives \(V_\text{max} \approx\) 16 mM/min and \(K_m \approx\) 2.6 mM (the equations: 16 mM/min and 2.7 mM). At 20 mM the bilayer rate is limited only by how many molecules hit the membrane per second, so more glucose gives proportionally more uptake. GLUT is limited by the transporters themselves: almost every transporter is loaded and busy flipping, so more glucose outside cannot make them faster. Watch the molecules view at 20 mM: nearly every transporter holds a glucose.

E2. \(V_\text{max}\) should double and \(K_m\) stay the same. The assay gives \(V_\text{max} \approx\) 31 mM/min and \(K_m \approx\) 2.8 mM. \(V_\text{max}\) is (number of transporters) × (turnover of one transporter), so it scales with the number of transporters. \(K_m\) is a property of one transporter (how readily it binds glucose compared with how fast it cycles), so it does not depend on how many there are. Cells use this: insulin raises glucose uptake into muscle and fat by moving more GLUT4 transporters to the surface, a change in \(V_\text{max}\).

E3. With GLUT the inside rises quickly at first (about 2.6 mM after 30 s) and then more slowly (about 4.5 mM after 2 min), approaching 5 mM, the outside concentration (ratio 1). Uptake stops because GLUT is passive: it carries glucose in both directions, and when inside and outside are equal, as many molecules leave as enter. With SGLT the inside passes 1 mM within seconds and is about 20 mM after 2 minutes (ratio about 20), still rising: glucose moves uphill. The energy comes from the Na⁺ that enters with each glucose (2 Na⁺ per glucose), flowing down its steep gradient (145 mM outside, about 12–14 mM inside) and pulled in by the −70 mV inside. The highest possible ratio is 1 for GLUT but about 20 000 for SGLT, \((145/14)^2 \times e^{140/26.7}\), as in the lecture (“more than 10 000-fold”). In practice the climb slows long before that, because inside glucose starts to occupy the inward-facing transporters.

E4. Without the pump, Na⁺ leaks in and is no longer removed: inside Na⁺ rises from about 14 mM to about 90 mM in 30 s and to nearly 145 mM after 2 min, and the voltage falls from −70 mV towards 0. The highest possible ratio falls from about 20 000 to about 20 within 30 s and then to 1. The inside glucose (about 20 mM) is now far above what the weak gradient can hold, so the uptake becomes negative (about −2 mM/min): SGLT runs backwards and glucose leaks out (about 14 mM after 3 more minutes). SGLT uses no ATP itself; it is driven by the Na⁺ gradient that the pump makes (secondary active transport), so blocking the pump stops it indirectly. With outside Na⁺ at 0, SGLT cannot bind Na⁺ and stops completely: only diffusion through the bilayer is left (about 0.1 mM/min).

E5. SGLT alone: \(V_\text{max} \approx\) 26 mM/min, \(K_m \approx\) 0.7 mM (a high affinity, like SGLT1 in the gut). With gliflozin, 90 % of the transporters are blocked (in the demo 11 of 12): \(V_\text{max}\) falls to about 4 mM/min (one working transporter plus a little diffusion), while \(K_m\) changes less (the fit, 1.9 mM, is noisy because the rates are small). Blocking transporters removes binding sites, like a non-competitive inhibitor in enzyme kinetics. With outside Na⁺ at 20 mM, \(V_\text{max}\) stays about the same (29 mM/min) but \(K_m\) rises about six-fold, to about 4 mM: with less Na⁺, fewer transporters have their two Na⁺ bound when a glucose arrives, so more glucose is needed to keep them busy; at high glucose they still saturate. In the kidney, SGLT2 normally takes back about 90 % of the filtered glucose. With most SGLT2 blocked, the tubule’s capacity (\(V_\text{max}\)) is far below the glucose load, so much of the filtered glucose stays in the urine, and blood glucose falls.
