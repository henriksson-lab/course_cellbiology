Study questions: G-protein-coupled receptors and second messengers

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Describe the structure of a G-protein-coupled receptor and explain how binding of a hormone outside the cell activates a G protein inside. [ELO 3]

Model answer

A GPCR is one polypeptide that crosses the membrane as seven α-helices (N-terminus outside, C-terminus inside); the helices form a bundle. Small ligands bind in a pocket between the helices, reached from outside. Agonist binding stabilises an active arrangement in which helix 6 moves outwards and a cavity opens on the cytosolic face. The C-terminal helix of Gα inserts there, which opens Gα’s nucleotide site: GDP leaves and GTP binds. The receptor is thus a GEF for the G protein. Gα–GTP separates from Gβγ and from the receptor, which can then activate another G protein.

2. Explain why the trimeric G protein is called a switch with a built-in timer. What do RGS proteins do? [ELO 3]

Model answer

Gα is on with GTP and off with GDP. With GTP it leaves βγ, and both halves bind and regulate target proteins. Gα is itself a slow GTPase: after seconds to minutes (for Gs, on average 10–20 s) it hydrolyses GTP to GDP + Pᵢ, switches off and rebinds βγ. The time until hydrolysis is the timer, and it sets how long each activation lasts. RGS proteins (regulators of G-protein signalling) are GTPase-activating proteins: they speed up hydrolysis, making the signal shorter.

3. Compare Gs, Gi and Gq: the enzyme each acts on, the second messenger(s) that change, and one example receptor. [ELO 3]

Model answer

Gs stimulates adenylyl cyclase → cAMP rises (e.g. β-adrenergic receptor for adrenaline, GLP-1 receptor). Gi inhibits adenylyl cyclase → cAMP falls; its βγ also opens K⁺ channels (e.g. μ-opioid receptor, α₂-adrenergic receptor). Gq activates phospholipase C-β → IP₃ and DAG, and IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from the ER (e.g. α₁-adrenergic receptor, M3 muscarinic acetylcholine receptor).

4. Describe the cAMP pathway from adenylyl cyclase to a change in gene expression. [ELO 3]

Model answer

Active Gαs binds and activates adenylyl cyclase in the plasma membrane, which converts ATP to cAMP. cAMP binds the regulatory subunits of PKA (two cAMP per R, four in total); the R subunits release the two catalytic subunits, which are active Ser/Thr kinases. They phosphorylate cytosolic targets within seconds (enzymes of glycogen metabolism, ion channels such as CFTR). Some C subunits enter the nucleus and phosphorylate the transcription factor CREB, which then activates transcription of target genes over minutes to hours. Phosphodiesterases turn cAMP into AMP and end the signal.

5. Explain how a Gq-coupled receptor raises cytosolic Ca²⁺ and activates protein kinase C. [ELO 3]

Model answer

Gαq–GTP activates phospholipase C-β, which cleaves the membrane lipid PIP₂ into IP₃ and DAG. IP₃ is water-soluble, diffuses to the ER and opens the IP₃ receptor, a Ca²⁺ channel; Ca²⁺ flows down its large gradient from the ER lumen into the cytosol, which rises from about 100 nM to about 1 µM. PKC binds Ca²⁺, moves to the plasma membrane and is fully activated when it also binds DAG there. Ca²⁺ also binds calmodulin, which activates other targets (CaM-kinases, myosin light-chain kinase).

6. Explain, with an example, how a GPCR cascade amplifies a signal. Which step does not amplify, and why? [ELO 3]

Model answer

Gain comes from steps where one active molecule catalyses the activation or production of many. One hormone-bound receptor activates G proteins one after another; each Gαs activates an adenylyl cyclase, which makes many cAMP per second; each PKA catalytic subunit phosphorylates many target proteins; in the liver, PKA → phosphorylase kinase → glycogen phosphorylase, each an enzyme, so a few hormone molecules release millions of glucose molecules. The step from cAMP to PKA is binding, not catalysis: four cAMP release two catalytic subunits, so there is no gain at that step.

7. Describe desensitisation of a GPCR by GRK and arrestin, and what happens to the receptor afterwards. [ELO 3]

Model answer

A G-protein-coupled receptor kinase (GRK) recognises the active receptor and phosphorylates Ser/Thr on its cytosolic tail. Arrestin binds the phosphorylated receptor on its cytosolic face and blocks G-protein binding: the receptor is desensitised although the hormone is still bound. Arrestin also binds clathrin and its adaptor, so the receptor is internalised by clathrin-mediated endocytosis. In the endosome the ligand dissociates and the receptor is dephosphorylated and recycled to the surface (resensitisation), or it is sent to the lysosome and degraded, which lowers the number of receptors (down-regulation). This contributes to drug tolerance.

8. Describe one method to follow a second messenger in a living cell. What would you see when you add a Gq-activating hormone? [ELO 1] [ELO 3]

Model answer

Ca²⁺ imaging: load cells with a Ca²⁺-sensitive dye such as fura-2, or express a genetically encoded indicator such as GCaMP (GFP fused to calmodulin), and record fluorescence over time in a fluorescence microscope. Binding of Ca²⁺ changes the brightness (or the excitation ratio for fura-2). After adding a Gq-activating hormone, the fluorescence rises within seconds as IP₃ releases Ca²⁺ from the ER, often as a spike or repeated oscillations, and falls back as pumps remove Ca²⁺. (Alternative: a FRET sensor for cAMP, CFP–Epac–YFP; cAMP binding opens the sensor, FRET falls and the CFP/YFP emission ratio rises.)

9. Explain how cholera toxin and pertussis toxin both raise cAMP, although they act on different G proteins. [ELO 3] [ELO 9]

Model answer

Both toxins transfer ADP-ribose from NAD⁺ to a Gα subunit. Cholera toxin modifies Gαs so that it can no longer hydrolyse GTP: once activated, Gs stays on and adenylyl cyclase is stimulated continuously. Pertussis toxin modifies Gαi so that its receptors can no longer activate it: Gi stays off, and the inhibitory brake on adenylyl cyclase is lost. In both cases cAMP rises. In the gut, high cAMP keeps CFTR open, causing secretory diarrhoea (cholera).

VG-style questions

10. [VG] A patient takes theophylline, a phosphodiesterase inhibitor. (a) Predict how the cAMP response of a cell to a short adrenaline pulse changes in height and duration, and explain why. (b) Would theophylline have any effect in a cell with no adrenaline at all? (c) Explain why a PDE inhibitor and a β-blocker have opposite effects on the same pathway, although they act at very different steps. [ELO 3] [ELO 9]

Model answer
  1. cAMP level is set by a balance between synthesis (adenylyl cyclase) and breakdown (PDE). With less PDE activity, the same cyclase activity builds up more cAMP, so the peak is higher; and after the pulse, when Gα has switched off the cyclase, cAMP is removed more slowly, so the response lasts longer. (b) Yes: there is always some basal cyclase activity, so resting cAMP also rises (and with it basal PKA activity). (c) Both change the amount of cAMP. The β-blocker reduces the input (fewer active receptors, less Gs, less cAMP made), the PDE inhibitor reduces the output (less cAMP destroyed). Any step in a cascade can be a drug target, and the effect on the end product depends on whether the drug acts on an “on” or an “off” step.

11. [VG] You suspect that a new compound is an agonist at a Gs-coupled receptor and want to show that its effect needs the receptor, Gs and cAMP. Design experiments using a cell line, a cAMP FRET sensor and tools from this lecture. Include controls. [ELO 1] [ELO 3]

Model answer

Express a cAMP FRET sensor (CFP–Epac–YFP) in a cell line that expresses the receptor, and image the CFP/YFP ratio before and after adding the compound; a rise in the ratio means a rise in cAMP. Make a dose–response curve (EC₅₀). Receptor: add a specific competitive antagonist of the receptor: the response should be reduced and shifted to higher compound concentrations; and test a cell line without the receptor (or with the receptor gene knocked out by CRISPR, then re-expressed as a rescue). Gs: compare with a known Gs agonist; in cells lacking Gαs (knock-out) the response should disappear; cholera toxin pre-treatment maximally activates Gs and should occlude a further response. cAMP-specific: a PDE inhibitor should make the response larger and longer; forskolin, which activates adenylyl cyclase directly, is a positive control for the sensor. Controls: vehicle alone, untransfected cells, and a check that the compound does not change CFP or YFP fluorescence by itself.

This is a simple model (a few differential equations) with illustrative numbers: qualitatively right, not a fit to data. Binding is treated as fast, there is only one receptor type and one PDE, and the steps from PKA to glucose are lumped.

E1. Choose Normal cell, 1 min pulse. Read the peak values of active receptors, active Gαs, cAMP and active PKA. In the table, find the gain per step. Which step amplifies most, and which step has a gain below 1? Explain both with the mechanism. How many extra glucose molecules are released per hormone molecule bound? [ELO 3]

E2. Still in the normal cell, find (by dragging the cursor) how long after the end of the pulse cAMP is half-way back to rest. Now predict, then test: (a) Slow GTPase; (b) the PDE inhibitor; (c) Fast GTPase. For each, how do the height and the duration of the cAMP signal change, and why? [ELO 3]

E3. Predict first. Choose Cholera toxin. What happens to Gαs, cAMP and PKA after the hormone has gone? Then set the dose to 0.1 × K_d, and then the duration to 5 s. Does it matter how much hormone the cell got? Explain using the G-protein cycle. [ELO 3] [ELO 9]

E4. Choose β-blocker, then β-blocker + 10 × more hormone. Compare the peaks with the normal cell. Now switch the β-blocker off and find the hormone dose that, without blocker, gives the same response as the blocker preset. What does this tell you about how a competitive antagonist works? [ELO 3] [ELO 9]

E5. [VG] Choose Long exposure (5 min). Read active receptors, cAMP and PKA at the start (about 60 s) and at the end of the pulse (320 s). Then set the arrestin slider to 0 and to 0.030 /s. (a) Explain the shapes with GRK and arrestin. (b) Active receptors fall much more than PKA activity does. Why? (c) Use the result to explain why patients who take an opioid every day need higher doses over time. [ELO 3] [ELO 9]

Solutions

E1. Peaks: active receptors about 82 % (8 200 of 10 000), active Gαs about 69 % (69 000), cAMP about 4.6 µM (5.6 million molecules), active PKA about 84 % (about 810 000 free C subunits). Gains: receptor → Gαs × 8, Gαs → cAMP × 81 (the largest: adenylyl cyclase is an enzyme that keeps making cAMP while switched on, and PDE removes it at a finite rate), cAMP → PKA × 0.15 (below 1: binding, not catalysis; four cAMP free two C subunits, and most cAMP is free in the cytosol), PKA → glucose × 30 per second (enzymes again). The receptor step amplifies because an active receptor is a GEF that activates one G protein after another. Over the 10 minutes the cell releases about 2 billion extra glucose molecules, about 220 000 per hormone molecule bound.

E2. Normal cell: the pulse ends at 80 s; cAMP is half-way back after about 24 s (t ≈ 104 s). (a) Slow GTPase (0.01 /s): each Gα stays active longer, so more Gα is on at the same time: cAMP peaks higher (6.2 µM) and is half-way back only after about 73 s; even resting cAMP is higher (0.74 µM instead of 0.26 µM), because spontaneously activated Gα also lasts longer. (b) PDE inhibitor: cAMP made at the same rate is removed more slowly: the peak is much higher (12.8 µM) and the return slower (about 38 s); resting cAMP also rises (0.79 µM). PKA, which is already almost saturated, rises less (98 %). (c) Fast GTPase (1 /s): each Gα is on for under a second, so few are active at once (11 %); cAMP peaks at only 0.8 µM, PKA at 14 %, and the signal ends within about 8 s of the pulse. Fast off-switches give short, weak signals; slow ones give large, long signals that no longer follow the hormone closely.

E3. With cholera toxin, Gαs that is switched on cannot switch off. During the pulse Gαs goes to about 100 %; after the pulse it stays at 100 %, cAMP stays at about 6.8 µM and PKA at about 92 % to the end of the run. With dose 0.1 × K_d the cell still ends at Gαs 76 %, cAMP 5.2 µM, PKA 87 % after 10 minutes; with a 5-second pulse Gαs reaches 72 %. Even with the dose at its lowest the end result is similar; and with no hormone at all, spontaneous activation alone would bring Gαs to about half in 10 minutes. The reason: the off-step (GTP hydrolysis) is gone, so every activation, by the hormone or by the slow spontaneous GDP–GTP exchange, adds up and is never undone. The amount of hormone only changes how fast the cell gets there. In the gut this means CFTR stays open for as long as the toxin-modified Gαs lasts.

E4. With the β-blocker (100 × K_B) and the normal dose, only about 9 % of receptors are active, cAMP peaks at 1.5 µM and PKA at 37 %. With 10 × more hormone the response recovers to 47 % receptors, cAMP 3.9 µM, PKA 79 %. Without the blocker, a dose of 0.1 × K_d gives the same response as the blocker preset (and 1 × K_d the same as blocker + 10 × hormone). A competitive antagonist does not lower the maximum; it competes for the same site, so it shifts the dose–response curve to the right by the factor (1 + [B]/K_B), here about 100. Enough agonist can overcome it. (In a patient on β-blockers, the same adrenaline surge has a smaller effect on the heart.)

E5. (a) Default arrestin (0.010 /s): at 60 s about 66 % of receptors are active (28 % already desensitised) and cAMP is at its peak (4.6 µM, PKA 84 %); by the end of the pulse (320 s) 79 % of the receptors are desensitised, only 19 % are active, cAMP has fallen to 2.7 µM and PKA to 64 %, although the hormone is still there. GRK phosphorylates only active receptors, and arrestin then blocks their coupling to Gs, so the more a receptor is used, the more it is switched off. With arrestin at 0, cAMP stays at a plateau (5.0 µM, PKA 86 %) for the whole pulse; with 0.030 /s it falls further (cAMP 1.3 µM, PKA 29 % at 320 s). After the pulse, receptors recover only slowly (still 45 % desensitised at 600 s with the default), as they are dephosphorylated and recycled from endosomes. (b) Amplification and saturation: with the default settings there are more active G proteins and more cAMP than PKA needs, so PKA is near its maximum. Losing receptors first removes this reserve; PKA falls only when cAMP drops below the range where PKA is saturated. (c) With daily opioid use, μ-opioid receptors are repeatedly activated, phosphorylated by GRK, bound by arrestin and internalised (some degraded). Fewer working receptors remain, so the same dose gives a smaller Gi signal: tolerance. A higher dose is needed for the same effect. (Other adaptations in the cell also contribute.)
