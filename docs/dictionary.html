Glossary

Key terms used in the course. Definitions are short; follow the link for the full explanation. Add terms here as lectures are written.

The cell and how we study it

A tour of the cell

See A tour of the cell.

Cytosol: the fluid part of the cell between the organelles, where free ribosomes make proteins and much of metabolism takes place.

Cytoplasm: everything inside the plasma membrane except the nucleus: the cytosol plus the organelles.

Organelle: a membrane-bound compartment inside a eukaryotic cell with its own job, such as the ER or a mitochondrion.

Lumen: the space inside a membrane-bound organelle, on the other side of its membrane from the cytosol.

Topology (membrane): which side of a membrane a space or a part of a protein lies on; the lumen of the ER, Golgi, vesicles and lysosomes is topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell.

Nuclear envelope: the two membranes around the nucleus, crossed by nuclear pores; the outer membrane continues into the ER.

Nuclear pore: a large protein channel in the nuclear envelope that joins the nucleoplasm to the cytosol.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane sheets and tubes that makes membrane and secreted proteins (rough ER) and lipids (smooth ER), and stores Ca²⁺.

Golgi apparatus: a stack of flattened membrane compartments that modifies proteins from the ER, for example by adding sugar chains, and sorts them to their destinations.

Lysosome: an acidic organelle (pH about 5) full of digestive enzymes that breaks down material taken up by the cell or from the cell itself.

Model system: a simpler organism or cell type (yeast, a cell line, an organoid) chosen because it answers a question more easily than a human body can.

Informed consent: a person’s voluntary agreement, after being told the purpose and risks, to let their tissue or data be used in research.

Cell fractionation: breaking cells open and separating their parts, usually by centrifugation, to find out what each part does.

Seeing cells: microscopy

See Seeing cells: microscopy.

Resolution: the smallest distance at which two points can still be seen as two separate points.

Magnification: how much larger the image is than the object; it adds no detail beyond the resolution limit.

Numerical aperture, NA: how wide a cone of light an objective collects; a higher NA gives better resolution.

Phase-contrast microscopy: turns small delays of light in dense cell parts into differences in brightness, so living unstained cells become visible.

Differential interference contrast, DIC: shows steep changes in optical path as light and shadow, so cells look three-dimensional.

Fluorophore: a molecule that absorbs light of one wavelength and emits light of a longer wavelength.

Stokes shift: the difference in wavelength between a fluorophore’s excitation peak and emission peak.

Dichroic mirror: a mirror that reflects light below a certain wavelength and lets longer wavelengths pass; it separates excitation light from emitted light.

Immunofluorescence: labelling a protein in fixed, permeabilised cells with antibodies coupled, directly or through a secondary antibody, to a fluorophore.

Green fluorescent protein, GFP: a jellyfish protein that forms its own fluorescent group, used as a genetic tag to see proteins in living cells.

Fusion protein: one protein chain made from two genes joined in the same reading frame, for example a protein of interest joined to GFP.

Confocal microscopy: fluorescence microscopy in which a pinhole blocks out-of-focus light, giving a sharp optical section of a thick sample.

Photobleaching: the permanent loss of fluorescence when a fluorophore is destroyed by repeated excitation.

Super-resolution microscopy: light-microscopy methods, such as STED and PALM/STORM, that resolve details below the ~200 nm limit.

Transmission electron microscopy, TEM: electrons pass through an ultrathin, heavy-metal-stained section, showing structures inside the cell at about 1–2 nm.

Scanning electron microscopy, SEM: images the 3D surface of a metal-coated sample by scanning it with a fine electron beam.

Cryo-electron microscopy, cryo-EM: electron microscopy of rapidly frozen samples in glass-like ice; averaging many images of one protein gives its 3D structure.

Taking cells apart and perturbing them

Cell line: a population of cells that has been made immortal and can be grown in culture without limit.

Primary cells: cells taken directly from a tissue, which divide only a limited number of times in culture.

Organoid: a small 3D structure grown from stem cells that contains several cell types of an organ, arranged roughly as in the tissue.

Mycoplasma: very small bacteria without a cell wall that often contaminate cell cultures unnoticed and change how the cells behave.

Differential centrifugation: separating cell components by spinning at increasing speeds, so larger and denser particles form a pellet first.

Density-gradient centrifugation: separating particles by spinning them through a solution that gets denser towards the bottom, so each stops at its own density.

Marker protein: a protein known to be found in only one organelle, used to identify that organelle in a fraction.

Epitope: the small part of a target molecule that an antibody binds.

Western blot: separates proteins by size, transfers them to a membrane and detects one protein with antibodies.

Immunoprecipitation: using an antibody on beads to pull a protein, and the proteins bound to it, out of a cell lysate.

Flow cytometry: measuring light scatter and fluorescence of single cells as they pass a laser one at a time.

Gating: choosing a region in a flow cytometry plot so that only one population of cells is analysed further.

RNA interference, RNAi: lowering the amount of a protein with a short double-stranded RNA that leads to the matching mRNA being cut.

Knockout: disabling a gene completely, for example by a CRISPR-Cas9 cut that causes a frameshift.
