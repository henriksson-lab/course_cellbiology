Glossary

Key terms used in the course. Definitions are short; follow the link for the full explanation. Add terms here as lectures are written.

The cell and how we study it

A tour of the cell

See A tour of the cell.

Cytosol: the fluid part of the cell between the organelles, where free ribosomes make proteins and much of metabolism takes place.

Cytoplasm: everything inside the plasma membrane except the nucleus: the cytosol plus the organelles.

Organelle: a membrane-bound compartment inside a eukaryotic cell with its own job, such as the ER or a mitochondrion.

Lumen: the space inside a membrane-bound organelle, on the other side of its membrane from the cytosol.

Topology (membrane): which side of a membrane a space or a part of a protein lies on; the lumen of the ER, Golgi, vesicles and lysosomes is topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell.

Nuclear envelope: the two membranes around the nucleus, crossed by nuclear pores; the outer membrane continues into the ER.

Nuclear pore: a large protein channel in the nuclear envelope that joins the nucleoplasm to the cytosol.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane sheets and tubes that makes membrane and secreted proteins (rough ER) and lipids (smooth ER), and stores Ca²⁺.

Golgi apparatus: a stack of flattened membrane compartments that modifies proteins from the ER, for example by adding sugar chains, and sorts them to their destinations.

Lysosome: an acidic organelle (pH about 5) full of digestive enzymes that breaks down material taken up by the cell or from the cell itself.

Model system: a simpler organism or cell type (yeast, a cell line, an organoid) chosen because it answers a question more easily than a human body can.

Informed consent: a person’s voluntary agreement, after being told the purpose and risks, to let their tissue or data be used in research.

Cell fractionation: breaking cells open and separating their parts, usually by centrifugation, to find out what each part does.

Seeing cells: microscopy

See Seeing cells: microscopy.

Resolution: the smallest distance at which two points can still be seen as two separate points.

Magnification: how much larger the image is than the object; it adds no detail beyond the resolution limit.

Numerical aperture, NA: how wide a cone of light an objective collects; a higher NA gives better resolution.

Phase-contrast microscopy: turns small delays of light in dense cell parts into differences in brightness, so living unstained cells become visible.

Differential interference contrast, DIC: shows steep changes in optical path as light and shadow, so cells look three-dimensional.

Fluorophore: a molecule that absorbs light of one wavelength and emits light of a longer wavelength.

Stokes shift: the difference in wavelength between a fluorophore’s excitation peak and emission peak.

Dichroic mirror: a mirror that reflects light below a certain wavelength and lets longer wavelengths pass; it separates excitation light from emitted light.

Immunofluorescence: labelling a protein in fixed, permeabilised cells with antibodies coupled, directly or through a secondary antibody, to a fluorophore.

Green fluorescent protein, GFP: a jellyfish protein that forms its own fluorescent group, used as a genetic tag to see proteins in living cells.

Fusion protein: one protein chain made from two genes joined in the same reading frame, for example a protein of interest joined to GFP.

Confocal microscopy: fluorescence microscopy in which a pinhole blocks out-of-focus light, giving a sharp optical section of a thick sample.

Photobleaching: the permanent loss of fluorescence when a fluorophore is destroyed by repeated excitation.

Super-resolution microscopy: light-microscopy methods, such as STED and PALM/STORM, that resolve details below the ~200 nm limit.

Transmission electron microscopy, TEM: electrons pass through an ultrathin, heavy-metal-stained section, showing structures inside the cell at about 1–2 nm.

Scanning electron microscopy, SEM: images the 3D surface of a metal-coated sample by scanning it with a fine electron beam.

Cryo-electron microscopy, cryo-EM: electron microscopy of rapidly frozen samples in glass-like ice; averaging many images of one protein gives its 3D structure.

Taking cells apart and perturbing them

Cell line: a population of cells that has been made immortal and can be grown in culture without limit.

Primary cells: cells taken directly from a tissue, which divide only a limited number of times in culture.

Organoid: a small 3D structure grown from stem cells that contains several cell types of an organ, arranged roughly as in the tissue.

Mycoplasma: very small bacteria without a cell wall that often contaminate cell cultures unnoticed and change how the cells behave.

Differential centrifugation: separating cell components by spinning at increasing speeds, so larger and denser particles form a pellet first.

Density-gradient centrifugation: separating particles by spinning them through a solution that gets denser towards the bottom, so each stops at its own density.

Marker protein: a protein known to be found in only one organelle, used to identify that organelle in a fraction.

Epitope: the small part of a target molecule that an antibody binds.

Western blot: separates proteins by size, transfers them to a membrane and detects one protein with antibodies.

Immunoprecipitation: using an antibody on beads to pull a protein, and the proteins bound to it, out of a cell lysate.

Flow cytometry: measuring light scatter and fluorescence of single cells as they pass a laser one at a time.

Gating: choosing a region in a flow cytometry plot so that only one population of cells is analysed further.

RNA interference, RNAi: lowering the amount of a protein with a short double-stranded RNA that leads to the matching mRNA being cut.

Knockout: disabling a gene completely, for example by a CRISPR-Cas9 cut that causes a frameshift.

Membranes and transport

Membrane structure

See Membrane structure.

Amphipathic: describes a molecule with one part that likes water (polar) and one part that avoids water (hydrophobic).

Hydrophobic effect: the tendency of non-polar groups to gather together in water, driven mainly by the rise in entropy of the water molecules they release.

Lipid bilayer: two layers of lipid molecules with their tails facing inwards, forming the basic sealed structure of every cell membrane.

Membrane fluidity: how easily lipids and proteins move within the plane of a membrane, set by tail length, double bonds, cholesterol and temperature.

Flip-flop: movement of a lipid from one leaflet of the bilayer to the other, which is very rare without the help of enzymes.

Flippase: an ATP-driven enzyme that moves specific lipids, such as phosphatidylserine, from the outer to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane.

Scramblase: a membrane protein that, when switched on, lets lipids move in both directions between leaflets without using energy, so their asymmetry is lost.

Lipid raft: a proposed small, short-lived membrane domain rich in sphingolipids and cholesterol that may gather certain proteins.

Integral membrane protein: a protein with parts embedded in the hydrophobic core of the bilayer, which can be released only with a detergent.

GPI anchor: a glycolipid attached to the end of a protein that holds the protein on the outer surface of the plasma membrane.

Hydropathy plot: a graph of average hydrophobicity along a protein sequence, used to predict transmembrane α-helices.

Detergent: a small amphipathic molecule with one tail that forms micelles and can dissolve membranes to extract membrane proteins.

FRAP: fluorescence recovery after photobleaching; a laser bleaches a small spot and you measure how fast fluorescent molecules diffuse back in.

Freeze-fracture electron microscopy: splitting a frozen membrane between its two leaflets, which shows the integral proteins as particles in a metal replica.

Glycocalyx: the coat of sugar chains on glycoproteins, glycolipids and proteoglycans that covers the outside of the cell.

Lipid nanoparticle (LNP): a small particle of ionisable lipid, helper phospholipid, cholesterol and PEG-lipid that carries RNA into cells.

Transporters and pumps

See Transporters and pumps.

Simple diffusion: movement of a molecule straight through the lipid bilayer, down its concentration gradient, without help from a protein.

Passive transport: movement of a solute down its (electro)chemical gradient (ΔG < 0); it needs no energy input but may need a transport protein as a path.

Active transport: movement of a solute against its (electro)chemical gradient (ΔG > 0), driven by ATP hydrolysis or by another gradient.

Electrochemical gradient: the combined driving force on an ion from its concentration difference and from the voltage across the membrane.

Transporter (carrier): a membrane protein that binds a specific solute and changes shape to move it across the membrane, one or a few molecules per cycle.

Alternating access: the mechanism by which a transporter’s binding site faces first one side of the membrane and then the other, never both at once.

Uniport: transport of a single kind of solute by a transporter, always down its gradient (e.g. GLUT1).

Symport: coupled transport of two different solutes in the same direction by one transporter (e.g. SGLT1: 2 Na⁺ + 1 glucose).

Antiport: coupled transport of two different solutes in opposite directions by one transporter (e.g. the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger).

Secondary active transport: uphill transport of a solute driven by the downhill flow of another ion, usually Na⁺ or H⁺, whose gradient was made by a pump.

Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase): a P-type pump in all animal cells that uses one ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ into the cell.

P-type pump: an ATP-driven pump that phosphorylates itself on an aspartate during each transport cycle (e.g. the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and the Ca²⁺ pumps).

ABC transporter: a pump with two ATP-binding cassettes that uses ATP to move substrates across a membrane; in human cells mostly exporters of lipids, toxins and drugs.

P-glycoprotein (ABCB1): an ABC exporter that pumps many hydrophobic drugs out of cells, causing multidrug resistance in cancer and limiting drug uptake into the gut and brain.

Aquaporin: a water channel that lets water, but not ions or H⁺, cross the membrane rapidly by osmosis.

SGLT2 inhibitor (gliflozin): a drug that blocks the kidney Na⁺–glucose symporter SGLT2, so glucose leaves in the urine; used in type 2 diabetes, heart failure and kidney disease.

Ion channels and membrane potential

See Ion channels and membrane potential.

Ion channel: a membrane protein that forms a water-filled pore through which specific ions flow rapidly down their electrochemical gradient when the channel is open.

Selectivity filter: the narrowest part of a channel pore, whose lining atoms decide which ion can pass; in K⁺ channels it is lined by backbone carbonyl oxygens.

Gating: the switching of a channel between closed and open states in response to a signal such as voltage, a bound ligand or mechanical force.

Voltage sensor: a transmembrane helix carrying positive charges that moves when the membrane potential changes, and so opens or closes a voltage-gated channel.

Equilibrium (Nernst) potential: the membrane potential at which an ion’s concentration and electrical forces balance, so its net flow through open channels is zero.

Nernst equation: \(E = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{c_\text{out}}{c_\text{in}}\), which gives the equilibrium potential of an ion from its concentrations and charge.

Resting membrane potential: the steady voltage across the plasma membrane of an unstimulated cell, about −70 mV, set mainly by open K⁺ leak channels.

Depolarisation: a change of the membrane potential towards less negative values.

Action potential: a brief, all-or-none electrical signal in which voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and then voltage-gated K⁺ channels restore the resting potential.

Refractory period: the short time after an action potential when inactivated Na⁺ channels cannot reopen, so a new action potential cannot start.

Patch clamp: a glass pipette sealed tightly onto a membrane records the tiny currents through single ion channels or a whole cell.

CFTR: an ATP- and phosphorylation-gated Cl⁻ channel in epithelial cells whose loss of function causes cystic fibrosis.

CFTR potentiator: a drug, such as ivacaftor, that increases the time a CFTR channel at the cell surface spends open.

CFTR corrector: a drug, such as elexacaftor or tezacaftor, that helps misfolded CFTR fold and leave the ER so that it reaches the plasma membrane.
