Glossary

Key terms used in the course. Definitions are short; follow the link for the full explanation. Add terms here as lectures are written.

The cell and how we study it

A tour of the cell

See A tour of the cell.

Cytosol: the fluid part of the cell between the organelles, where free ribosomes make proteins and much of metabolism takes place.

Cytoplasm: everything inside the plasma membrane except the nucleus: the cytosol plus the organelles.

Organelle: a membrane-bound compartment inside a eukaryotic cell with its own job, such as the ER or a mitochondrion.

Lumen: the space inside a membrane-bound organelle, on the other side of its membrane from the cytosol.

Topology (membrane): which side of a membrane a space or a part of a protein lies on; the lumen of the ER, Golgi, vesicles and lysosomes is topologically equivalent to the outside of the cell.

Nuclear envelope: the two membranes around the nucleus, crossed by nuclear pores; the outer membrane continues into the ER.

Nuclear pore: a large protein channel in the nuclear envelope that joins the nucleoplasm to the cytosol.

Endoplasmic reticulum (ER): a network of membrane sheets and tubes that makes membrane and secreted proteins (rough ER) and lipids (smooth ER), and stores Ca²⁺.

Golgi apparatus: a stack of flattened membrane compartments that modifies proteins from the ER, for example by adding sugar chains, and sorts them to their destinations.

Lysosome: an acidic organelle (pH about 5) full of digestive enzymes that breaks down material taken up by the cell or from the cell itself.

Model system: a simpler organism or cell type (yeast, a cell line, an organoid) chosen because it answers a question more easily than a human body can.

Informed consent: a person’s voluntary agreement, after being told the purpose and risks, to let their tissue or data be used in research.

Cell fractionation: breaking cells open and separating their parts, usually by centrifugation, to find out what each part does.

Seeing cells: microscopy

See Seeing cells: microscopy.

Resolution: the smallest distance at which two points can still be seen as two separate points.

Magnification: how much larger the image is than the object; it adds no detail beyond the resolution limit.

Numerical aperture, NA: how wide a cone of light an objective collects; a higher NA gives better resolution.

Phase-contrast microscopy: turns small delays of light in dense cell parts into differences in brightness, so living unstained cells become visible.

Differential interference contrast, DIC: shows steep changes in optical path as light and shadow, so cells look three-dimensional.

Fluorophore: a molecule that absorbs light of one wavelength and emits light of a longer wavelength.

Stokes shift: the difference in wavelength between a fluorophore’s excitation peak and emission peak.

Dichroic mirror: a mirror that reflects light below a certain wavelength and lets longer wavelengths pass; it separates excitation light from emitted light.

Immunofluorescence: labelling a protein in fixed, permeabilised cells with antibodies coupled, directly or through a secondary antibody, to a fluorophore.

Green fluorescent protein, GFP: a jellyfish protein that forms its own fluorescent group, used as a genetic tag to see proteins in living cells.

Fusion protein: one protein chain made from two genes joined in the same reading frame, for example a protein of interest joined to GFP.

Confocal microscopy: fluorescence microscopy in which a pinhole blocks out-of-focus light, giving a sharp optical section of a thick sample.

Photobleaching: the permanent loss of fluorescence when a fluorophore is destroyed by repeated excitation.

Super-resolution microscopy: light-microscopy methods, such as STED and PALM/STORM, that resolve details below the ~200 nm limit.

Transmission electron microscopy, TEM: electrons pass through an ultrathin, heavy-metal-stained section, showing structures inside the cell at about 1–2 nm.

Scanning electron microscopy, SEM: images the 3D surface of a metal-coated sample by scanning it with a fine electron beam.

Cryo-electron microscopy, cryo-EM: electron microscopy of rapidly frozen samples in glass-like ice; averaging many images of one protein gives its 3D structure.

Taking cells apart and perturbing them

Cell line: a population of cells that has been made immortal and can be grown in culture without limit.

Primary cells: cells taken directly from a tissue, which divide only a limited number of times in culture.

Organoid: a small 3D structure grown from stem cells that contains several cell types of an organ, arranged roughly as in the tissue.

Mycoplasma: very small bacteria without a cell wall that often contaminate cell cultures unnoticed and change how the cells behave.

Differential centrifugation: separating cell components by spinning at increasing speeds, so larger and denser particles form a pellet first.

Density-gradient centrifugation: separating particles by spinning them through a solution that gets denser towards the bottom, so each stops at its own density.

Marker protein: a protein known to be found in only one organelle, used to identify that organelle in a fraction.

Epitope: the small part of a target molecule that an antibody binds.

Western blot: separates proteins by size, transfers them to a membrane and detects one protein with antibodies.

Immunoprecipitation: using an antibody on beads to pull a protein, and the proteins bound to it, out of a cell lysate.

Flow cytometry: measuring light scatter and fluorescence of single cells as they pass a laser one at a time.

Gating: choosing a region in a flow cytometry plot so that only one population of cells is analysed further.

RNA interference, RNAi: lowering the amount of a protein with a short double-stranded RNA that leads to the matching mRNA being cut.

Knockout: disabling a gene completely, for example by a CRISPR-Cas9 cut that causes a frameshift.

Membranes and transport

Membrane structure

See Membrane structure.

Amphipathic: describes a molecule with one part that likes water (polar) and one part that avoids water (hydrophobic).

Hydrophobic effect: the tendency of non-polar groups to gather together in water, driven mainly by the rise in entropy of the water molecules they release.

Lipid bilayer: two layers of lipid molecules with their tails facing inwards, forming the basic sealed structure of every cell membrane.

Membrane fluidity: how easily lipids and proteins move within the plane of a membrane, set by tail length, double bonds, cholesterol and temperature.

Flip-flop: movement of a lipid from one leaflet of the bilayer to the other, which is very rare without the help of enzymes.

Flippase: an ATP-driven enzyme that moves specific lipids, such as phosphatidylserine, from the outer to the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane.

Scramblase: a membrane protein that, when switched on, lets lipids move in both directions between leaflets without using energy, so their asymmetry is lost.

Lipid raft: a proposed small, short-lived membrane domain rich in sphingolipids and cholesterol that may gather certain proteins.

Integral membrane protein: a protein with parts embedded in the hydrophobic core of the bilayer, which can be released only with a detergent.

GPI anchor: a glycolipid attached to the end of a protein that holds the protein on the outer surface of the plasma membrane.

Hydropathy plot: a graph of average hydrophobicity along a protein sequence, used to predict transmembrane α-helices.

Detergent: a small amphipathic molecule with one tail that forms micelles and can dissolve membranes to extract membrane proteins.

FRAP: fluorescence recovery after photobleaching; a laser bleaches a small spot and you measure how fast fluorescent molecules diffuse back in.

Freeze-fracture electron microscopy: splitting a frozen membrane between its two leaflets, which shows the integral proteins as particles in a metal replica.

Glycocalyx: the coat of sugar chains on glycoproteins, glycolipids and proteoglycans that covers the outside of the cell.

Lipid nanoparticle (LNP): a small particle of ionisable lipid, helper phospholipid, cholesterol and PEG-lipid that carries RNA into cells.

Transporters and pumps

See Transporters and pumps.

Simple diffusion: movement of a molecule straight through the lipid bilayer, down its concentration gradient, without help from a protein.

Passive transport: movement of a solute down its (electro)chemical gradient (ΔG < 0); it needs no energy input but may need a transport protein as a path.

Active transport: movement of a solute against its (electro)chemical gradient (ΔG > 0), driven by ATP hydrolysis or by another gradient.

Electrochemical gradient: the combined driving force on an ion from its concentration difference and from the voltage across the membrane.

Transporter (carrier): a membrane protein that binds a specific solute and changes shape to move it across the membrane, one or a few molecules per cycle.

Alternating access: the mechanism by which a transporter’s binding site faces first one side of the membrane and then the other, never both at once.

Uniport: transport of a single kind of solute by a transporter, always down its gradient (e.g. GLUT1).

Symport: coupled transport of two different solutes in the same direction by one transporter (e.g. SGLT1: 2 Na⁺ + 1 glucose).

Antiport: coupled transport of two different solutes in opposite directions by one transporter (e.g. the Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger).

Secondary active transport: uphill transport of a solute driven by the downhill flow of another ion, usually Na⁺ or H⁺, whose gradient was made by a pump.

Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase): a P-type pump in all animal cells that uses one ATP to move 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ into the cell.

P-type pump: an ATP-driven pump that phosphorylates itself on an aspartate during each transport cycle (e.g. the Na⁺/K⁺ pump and the Ca²⁺ pumps).

ABC transporter: a pump with two ATP-binding cassettes that uses ATP to move substrates across a membrane; in human cells mostly exporters of lipids, toxins and drugs.

P-glycoprotein (ABCB1): an ABC exporter that pumps many hydrophobic drugs out of cells, causing multidrug resistance in cancer and limiting drug uptake into the gut and brain.

Aquaporin: a water channel that lets water, but not ions or H⁺, cross the membrane rapidly by osmosis.

SGLT2 inhibitor (gliflozin): a drug that blocks the kidney Na⁺–glucose symporter SGLT2, so glucose leaves in the urine; used in type 2 diabetes, heart failure and kidney disease.

Ion channels and membrane potential

See Ion channels and membrane potential.

Ion channel: a membrane protein that forms a water-filled pore through which specific ions flow rapidly down their electrochemical gradient when the channel is open.

Selectivity filter: the narrowest part of a channel pore, whose lining atoms decide which ion can pass; in K⁺ channels it is lined by backbone carbonyl oxygens.

Gating: the switching of a channel between closed and open states in response to a signal such as voltage, a bound ligand or mechanical force.

Voltage sensor: a transmembrane helix carrying positive charges that moves when the membrane potential changes, and so opens or closes a voltage-gated channel.

Equilibrium (Nernst) potential: the membrane potential at which an ion’s concentration and electrical forces balance, so its net flow through open channels is zero.

Nernst equation: \(E = \frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{c_\text{out}}{c_\text{in}}\), which gives the equilibrium potential of an ion from its concentrations and charge.

Resting membrane potential: the steady voltage across the plasma membrane of an unstimulated cell, about −70 mV, set mainly by open K⁺ leak channels.

Depolarisation: a change of the membrane potential towards less negative values.

Action potential: a brief, all-or-none electrical signal in which voltage-gated Na⁺ channels open and then voltage-gated K⁺ channels restore the resting potential.

Refractory period: the short time after an action potential when inactivated Na⁺ channels cannot reopen, so a new action potential cannot start.

Patch clamp: a glass pipette sealed tightly onto a membrane records the tiny currents through single ion channels or a whole cell.

CFTR: an ATP- and phosphorylation-gated Cl⁻ channel in epithelial cells whose loss of function causes cystic fibrosis.

CFTR potentiator: a drug, such as ivacaftor, that increases the time a CFTR channel at the cell surface spends open.

CFTR corrector: a drug, such as elexacaftor or tezacaftor, that helps misfolded CFTR fold and leave the ER so that it reaches the plasma membrane.

Protein handling and organelles

Sorting proteins to the right compartment

See Sorting proteins to the right compartment.

Sorting signal: a part of a protein (a short amino-acid pattern or a surface patch) that a receptor reads to decide where the protein goes.

Signal peptide (ER): an N-terminal stretch of 15–30 amino acids with a hydrophobic core that sends a protein into the ER during translation and is then cut off.

Nuclear localisation signal (NLS): a short basic stretch of amino acids (e.g. PKKKRKV) that importins recognise to carry a protein into the nucleus.

Nuclear export signal (NES): a short leucine-rich stretch that exportins such as XPO1 recognise to carry a protein out of the nucleus.

Nuclear pore complex: a large protein channel (about 120 MDa) through the nuclear envelope, filled with FG repeats, that lets small molecules diffuse and larger ones pass with a carrier.

FG repeats: disordered, phenylalanine–glycine-rich chains of nucleoporins that fill the nuclear pore and act as a selective sieve.

Importin: a transport receptor that binds NLS cargo in the cytosol, carries it through the nuclear pore and releases it when Ran-GTP binds.

Ran: a small GTPase whose GTP form is made in the nucleus and destroyed in the cytosol; the resulting gradient makes nuclear transport one-way.

Gated transport: movement between the cytosol and the nucleus through nuclear pores, without crossing a membrane.

Mitochondrial presequence: an N-terminal, positively charged amphipathic helix that targets a protein to the mitochondrial matrix and is removed there.

TOM and TIM complexes: the protein translocators of the outer (TOM) and inner (TIM) mitochondrial membranes, through which proteins made in the cytosol are imported.

PTS1: a C-terminal tripeptide (typically -SKL) that the receptor PEX5 reads to import a folded protein into peroxisomes.

KDEL retrieval: return of ER-resident proteins with a C-terminal KDEL from the Golgi to the ER by the KDEL receptor.

Selinexor: a drug that blocks the exportin XPO1, trapping tumour-suppressor proteins in the nucleus; used in multiple myeloma.

The endoplasmic reticulum

See The endoplasmic reticulum.

Signal sequence: a stretch of about 15–30 amino acids at the N-terminus, with a hydrophobic core, that sends a protein into the ER and is then cut off.

Signal recognition particle (SRP): a protein–RNA complex that binds a signal sequence as it leaves the ribosome, pauses translation and brings the ribosome to the ER membrane.

Translocon (Sec61): the protein channel in the ER membrane through which a new chain enters the lumen; a lateral gate lets membrane segments out into the lipid.

Co-translational translocation: moving a protein into the ER while the ribosome is still making it.

Stop-transfer sequence: a hydrophobic segment that stops translocation of a chain already entering the ER and stays in the membrane.

Signal-anchor sequence: an uncleaved hydrophobic segment that starts translocation and stays as a membrane anchor; the positive charges next to it set its orientation.

Positive-inside rule: positively charged amino acids next to a membrane segment stay on the cytosolic side.

Protein disulfide isomerase (PDI): an ER enzyme that forms disulfide bonds in new proteins and rearranges wrong ones.

BiP: the main Hsp70 chaperone of the ER lumen; it binds unfolded chains and holds the UPR sensors inactive.

N-linked glycosylation: transfer of a 14-sugar block from dolichol to an asparagine in the sequence N-X-S/T, by oligosaccharyltransferase in the ER.

Calnexin cycle: repeated binding of a glycoprotein with one glucose to the lectin chaperone calnexin until it folds; UGGT re-adds the glucose to unfolded proteins.

ERAD (ER-associated degradation): return of misfolded ER proteins to the cytosol, where they are ubiquitinated and degraded by the proteasome.

Unfolded protein response (UPR): signalling from the ER sensors IRE1, PERK and ATF6 that raises folding capacity and lowers translation when unfolded proteins pile up, or triggers apoptosis.

Microsomes: small sealed vesicles of ER membrane formed when cells are broken; used to study translocation and topology.

Russell body: a swollen ER compartment filled with retained immunoglobulin in a plasma cell.

The Golgi and the secretory pathway

See The Golgi and the secretory pathway.

Secretory pathway: the route of proteins from the ER through the Golgi to the plasma membrane, secretory granules or lysosomes, always inside membrane compartments.

ERGIC: the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment, a set of tubules and vesicle clusters between the ER exit sites and the cis Golgi.

COPII: the coat that makes vesicles leaving the ER, switched on by the small GTPase Sar1.

COPI: the coat that makes vesicles carrying proteins back from the Golgi to the ER and between Golgi cisternae, switched on by ARF1.

Clathrin: a three-legged protein that forms basket-like coats on vesicles at the trans-Golgi network and the plasma membrane, linked to cargo by adaptors such as AP1 and AP2.

Rab GTPase: a small GTPase that marks the identity of a membrane and, in its GTP form, recruits tethers and other effectors.

Tether: a long coiled-coil protein or multi-subunit complex that catches an arriving vesicle before fusion.

SNARE: a membrane protein whose helices zip with partner SNAREs on the other membrane into a four-helix bundle that fuses the two bilayers.

NSF: an ATPase that pulls SNARE complexes apart after fusion so the SNAREs can be reused.

Trans-Golgi network (TGN): the tubular exit face of the Golgi, where cargo is sorted to the plasma membrane, secretory granules or endosomes.

Cisternal maturation: the model in which Golgi cisternae themselves move and change from cis to trans, carrying cargo with them.

Mannose-6-phosphate (M6P): a sugar tag added to lysosomal enzymes in the Golgi, which the M6P receptor uses to send them to the lysosomes.

Constitutive secretion: continuous, unregulated delivery of proteins and membrane to the cell surface, which happens in every cell.

Regulated secretion: storage of products in secretory granules that fuse with the plasma membrane only when a signal raises cytosolic Ca²⁺.

Brefeldin A: a fungal compound that blocks activation of ARF1, so COPI cannot form and the Golgi merges into the ER.

Endocytosis, lysosomes and autophagy

See Endocytosis, lysosomes and autophagy.

Endocytosis: uptake of material by folding in the plasma membrane and pinching off a vesicle.

Dynamin: a GTPase that forms a ring around the neck of a budding vesicle and pinches it off.

Receptor-mediated endocytosis: uptake of specific molecules bound to receptors that collect in clathrin-coated pits.

LDL (low-density lipoprotein): a particle of about 22 nm that carries cholesterol esters in the blood and is recognised through its apoB protein.

Endosome: a membrane compartment that receives endocytosed material and sorts it for recycling or degradation; it becomes more acidic as it matures.

Macropinocytosis: unselective, actin-driven uptake of large gulps of extracellular fluid.

Phagocytosis: receptor-triggered, actin-driven engulfment of large particles such as bacteria or dead cells by specialised cells.

Autophagy: delivery of parts of the cell’s own cytosol and organelles to lysosomes inside a double-membrane autophagosome.

mTORC1: a kinase on the lysosome surface that senses nutrients, drives growth and blocks autophagy when nutrients are plentiful.

PCSK9: a liver protein that binds the LDL receptor and sends it to the lysosome instead of recycling; the target of cholesterol-lowering antibodies.

Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH): an inherited disease with high blood LDL from birth, usually caused by faulty LDL receptors.

Endosomal escape: release of a drug, such as RNA from a lipid nanoparticle, from the endosome into the cytosol before it reaches the lysosome.

Protein life and death

See Protein life and death.

Chaperone: a protein that binds exposed hydrophobic parts of unfolded chains to prevent wrong contacts and help them fold, without carrying information about the final shape.

Hsp70: the main cytosolic chaperone, which binds and releases short hydrophobic stretches of a chain in an ATP-driven cycle.

Chaperonin: a barrel-shaped chaperone (GroEL/GroES in bacteria, TRiC in the human cytosol) that encloses a protein so it can fold alone.

Amyloid: an ordered, very stable protein aggregate in which β-strands from many chains stack across a long fibril (cross-β) and seed further growth.

Protein kinase: an enzyme that moves a phosphate from ATP onto a serine, threonine or tyrosine of a protein; a protein phosphatase removes it.

Acetylation: addition of an acetyl group to a lysine, which removes its positive charge (for example on histones).

Ubiquitin: a small protein of 76 amino acids attached to lysines of other proteins as a signal; Lys48-linked chains mark proteins for the proteasome.

E3 ubiquitin ligase: the enzyme that recognises a specific substrate and transfers ubiquitin from an E2 onto it; about 600 kinds in humans give the system its specificity.

Degron: a feature of a protein, such as a phosphorylated motif or an exposed hydrophobic stretch, that an E3 ligase recognises as a signal for destruction.

Proteasome: a large protease complex in the cytosol and nucleus; its 19S cap unfolds ubiquitinated proteins and its 20S core cuts them into short peptides.

Protein half-life: the time for half of a protein’s molecules to be destroyed (t½ = ln 2 / k_d).

Cycloheximide chase: blocking protein synthesis with cycloheximide and following the decay of a protein over time to measure its half-life.

Pulse-chase: labelling newly made proteins briefly, then following the labelled group over time.

PROTAC: a two-headed drug that links a target protein to an E3 ligase so that the target is ubiquitinated and destroyed.

Molecular glue: a small molecule, such as lenalidomide bound to cereblon, that makes an E3 ligase recognise and destroy new substrate proteins.

Organelles compared: keeping the cell in balance

See Organelles compared: keeping the cell in balance.

Nuclear lamina: a mesh of lamin filaments under the inner nuclear membrane that gives the nucleus its shape and anchors chromatin.

Lamin: an intermediate-filament protein (A- or B-type) that forms the nuclear lamina.

Peroxisome: a single-membrane organelle that imports folded enzymes, carries out oxidations that make H₂O₂, and shortens very-long-chain fatty acids.

Catalase: a peroxisomal enzyme that breaks down hydrogen peroxide to water and oxygen.

Lipid droplet: an organelle that stores neutral fat in a core covered by a single layer of phospholipids.

Contact site: a place where two organelle membranes are held 10–30 nm apart by tether proteins without fusing, so lipids and ions can pass between them.

Store-operated Ca²⁺ entry: refilling of the ER Ca²⁺ store, in which STIM in the ER opens ORAI channels in the plasma membrane.

SERCA: the ATP-driven pump that moves Ca²⁺ from the cytosol into the ER.

V-type H⁺-ATPase: the proton pump that acidifies the lumen of the Golgi, endosomes and lysosomes.

AMPK: a kinase that senses a low energy charge (high AMP) and switches on fuel use and switches off synthesis.

Homeostasis: keeping a variable near a set point by negative feedback.

Stereology: estimating volume fractions from sections by counting how many grid points fall on each structure.

Progerin: a shortened lamin A that keeps its farnesyl lipid and causes Hutchinson–Gilford progeria.

Lonafarnib: a farnesyltransferase inhibitor, the first approved drug for progeria.

Zellweger syndrome: an inherited disease in which a defect in a PEX gene blocks protein import into peroxisomes.

Cell signalling

Principles of cell communication

See Principles of cell communication.

Endocrine signalling: a hormone travels in the blood to target cells throughout the body.

Paracrine signalling: a local mediator acts on nearby cells and is quickly removed.

Autocrine signalling: a cell responds to a signal that it releases itself.

Contact-dependent signalling: a membrane-bound signal acts only on cells that touch the sending cell.

Nuclear receptor: an intracellular receptor for a small fat-soluble hormone that acts as a transcription factor.

Dissociation constant (K_d): the free ligand concentration at which half the receptors are occupied; a low K_d means high affinity.

Dose–response curve: a plot of response against log drug concentration, giving E_max and EC₅₀.

EC₅₀: the concentration of a drug that gives half of its maximal response; a measure of potency.

IC₅₀: the inhibitor concentration that removes half of a response, at a stated agonist concentration.

Efficacy: how strongly a drug activates a receptor once bound.

Agonist: a drug that binds a receptor and activates it.

Partial agonist: a drug that binds a receptor but activates it only part-way, even at full occupancy.

Antagonist: a drug that binds a receptor without activating it and blocks the agonist; competitive antagonists shift the curve right, non-competitive ones lower the maximum.

Spare receptors: receptors beyond those needed for a maximal response, which make EC₅₀ lower than K_d.

Desensitisation: a fall in response to a signal that stays on, for example by receptor phosphorylation, arrestin binding or receptor endocytosis.

G-protein-coupled receptors and second messengers

See G-protein-coupled receptors and second messengers.

G-protein-coupled receptor (GPCR): a cell-surface receptor made of one protein chain that crosses the membrane seven times and activates a trimeric G protein.

Trimeric G protein: a membrane-bound switch of three subunits (Gα, Gβ, Gγ) that is on when Gα holds GTP and off when it holds GDP.

GEF (guanine nucleotide exchange factor): a protein that makes a GTPase release GDP so that GTP can bind; an active GPCR is the GEF for its G protein.

Gs / Gi / Gq: the main Gα families: Gs stimulates and Gi inhibits adenylyl cyclase; Gq activates phospholipase C-β.

Second messenger: a small molecule or ion made or released inside the cell in response to a signal, such as cAMP, IP₃, DAG or Ca²⁺.

Adenylyl cyclase: a plasma-membrane enzyme that turns ATP into cyclic AMP (cAMP).

Phosphodiesterase (PDE): an enzyme that breaks down cAMP (or cGMP) and so ends the signal.

Protein kinase A (PKA): a kinase whose catalytic subunits are released when cAMP binds its regulatory subunits.

Phospholipase C-β: a Gq-activated enzyme that cuts the membrane lipid PIP₂ into IP₃ and DAG.

IP₃ receptor: a Ca²⁺ channel in the ER membrane that opens when IP₃ binds.

Protein kinase C (PKC): a kinase activated at the membrane by DAG together with Ca²⁺.

Calmodulin: a small Ca²⁺-binding protein that, with four Ca²⁺ bound, wraps around and activates target proteins.

Arrestin: a protein that binds phosphorylated active GPCRs, blocks G-protein coupling and links the receptor to clathrin for endocytosis.

Cholera toxin: a bacterial enzyme that ADP-ribosylates Gαs so it cannot hydrolyse GTP, keeping cAMP high in gut cells.

Enzyme-coupled receptors: kinase cascades

See Enzyme-coupled receptors: kinase cascades.

Receptor tyrosine kinase (RTK): a single-pass receptor whose cytosolic kinase domain phosphorylates tyrosines when ligand binding brings two receptor chains together.

Autophosphorylation: phosphorylation of a kinase by a second molecule of the same kinase, as in an RTK dimer where each chain phosphorylates the other.

SH2 domain: a protein domain of about 100 amino acids that binds a phosphorylated tyrosine together with the next few amino acids.

Adaptor protein: a protein without enzyme activity that links two other proteins, such as Grb2 linking an active RTK to SOS.

Ras: a small membrane-anchored GTPase that is active with GTP and links RTKs to the MAP kinase cascade.

GAP (GTPase-activating protein): a protein that switches a GTPase off by speeding up hydrolysis of its bound GTP.

MAP kinase cascade: a chain of three kinases (here RAF → MEK → ERK), each phosphorylating and activating the next.

Ultrasensitivity: a switch-like response in which the output goes from off to on over a small change in input (Hill coefficient above 1).

PI3K: a lipid kinase that turns PIP₂ into PIP₃ in the plasma membrane, recruiting proteins such as Akt.

Akt (protein kinase B): a kinase recruited to PIP₃ by its PH domain that promotes cell survival and growth.

JAK–STAT pathway: a pathway in which receptor-bound JAK kinases phosphorylate STATs, which pair up and act directly as transcription factors.

Phospho-specific antibody: an antibody that binds a protein only when a particular site is phosphorylated, so it reports the active form.

Gatekeeper mutation: a mutation that puts a larger amino acid at the entrance of a kinase’s ATP pocket, keeping an inhibitor out but letting ATP in.

Imatinib: the first targeted cancer drug; it blocks the ATP pocket of the BCR-ABL kinase in chronic myeloid leukaemia.

Signalling that shapes tissues and decides fates

See Signalling that shapes tissues and decides fates.

β-catenin: transcriptional coactivator that is destroyed continually unless Wnt signalling blocks the destruction complex.

Destruction complex: protein complex (APC, Axin, GSK3) that phosphorylates β-catenin and marks it for destruction by the proteasome.

Primary cilium: single non-moving cell projection that acts as a signalling antenna, where Hedgehog signalling takes place.

Patched: Hedgehog receptor that keeps Smoothened inactive until Hedgehog binds it.

Smoothened: membrane protein that activates Gli when it moves into the primary cilium; the target of vismodegib.

SMAD: transcription factor phosphorylated by TGF-β-family receptor kinases, which then enters the nucleus with SMAD4.

Notch: receptor for the membrane-bound signal Delta; it is cut, and its released intracellular domain activates genes with CSL.

γ-secretase: protease that cuts proteins inside the membrane, including Notch and the amyloid precursor protein.

Lateral inhibition: feedback in which a cell signalling via Delta stops its neighbours from taking the same fate, giving a salt-and-pepper pattern.

Morphogen: a signal that forms a concentration gradient and gives different cell fates at different concentrations.

NF-κB: inflammatory transcription factor held in the cytosol by IκB until IκB is phosphorylated and destroyed by the proteasome.

Reporter gene: a gene for luciferase or GFP placed after binding sites for a transcription factor, so that light measures pathway activity.

Tamoxifen: drug that binds the oestrogen receptor but blocks coactivator binding, used in ER-positive breast cancer.

Competence: a cell’s readiness to respond to a signal in a particular way, set by its open chromatin and partner transcription factors.

Cytoskeleton

Actin: cell shape and migration

See Actin: cell shape and migration.

G-actin: the free, globular actin monomer, which binds ATP or ADP.

F-actin: the filament made of G-actin subunits, a polar two-stranded helix about 7 nm wide.

Barbed end (+ end): the fast-growing end of an actin filament, with the lower critical concentration.

Pointed end (− end): the slow-growing end of an actin filament, where old ADP subunits leave.

Critical concentration: the free monomer concentration at which subunits join and leave a filament end equally fast.

Treadmilling: a steady state in which a filament gains subunits at its + end as fast as it loses them at its − end, so it keeps its length and moves.

Arp2/3 complex: a nucleator that binds the side of an actin filament and starts a branch at about 70°, building branched networks.

Formin: a nucleator that stays on the growing barbed end and makes long, unbranched actin filaments.

Profilin: a protein that binds actin monomers, recharges them with ATP and lets them join only free barbed ends.

Cofilin: a protein that binds old ADP-actin filaments, cuts them and speeds their disassembly.

Capping protein: a protein that binds barbed ends tightly and stops growth there.

Lamellipodium: the thin sheet at the front of a crawling cell, filled with a branched actin network that pushes the membrane.

Filopodium: a thin, finger-like protrusion containing a tight bundle of parallel actin filaments.

Stress fibre: a contractile bundle of actin and myosin II that ends in focal adhesions.

Rho GTPases (Rho, Rac, Cdc42): small GTP-binding switches that turn on stress fibres, lamellipodia and filopodia respectively.

Phalloidin: a mushroom toxin that binds and stabilises F-actin; with a fluorescent dye it stains actin in fixed cells.

Latrunculin: a sponge toxin that binds G-actin and so makes actin filaments disassemble.

Microtubules and motor proteins

See Microtubules and motor proteins.

Tubulin: the αβ protein dimer, 8 nm long, that builds microtubules; both subunits bind GTP.

Protofilament: a head-to-tail row of tubulin dimers; 13 of them form a microtubule.

Plus end / minus end: the fast-growing end of a microtubule (β-tubulin exposed) and the slow, usually anchored end (α-tubulin exposed).

GTP cap: the GTP-tubulin at a growing microtubule end that keeps it straight and stable.

Dynamic instability: random switching of a microtubule end between slow growth and fast shrinkage.

Catastrophe: the switch from growth to rapid shrinkage when the GTP cap is lost.

Rescue: the switch from shrinkage back to growth.

Centrosome: the main microtubule-organising centre of animal cells: two centrioles in pericentriolar material.

γ-tubulin ring complex: a ring that templates new microtubules and caps their minus ends.

EB1: a protein that binds only growing plus ends; fused to GFP it shows them as moving comets.

Kinesin: a family of motor proteins that mostly walk towards the plus end, 8 nm and one ATP per step.

Dynein: a large motor that walks towards the minus end; it also bends cilia (axonemal dynein).

Axonal transport: motor-driven transport along an axon, out to the tip (anterograde, kinesin) or back to the cell body (retrograde, dynein).

Axoneme: the 9 + 2 microtubule core of a motile cilium or flagellum.

Myosin, intermediate filaments and the mechanical cell

See Myosin, intermediate filaments and the mechanical cell.

Myosin: a motor protein that uses ATP to walk along actin filaments; the tail decides whether it pulls (myosin II) or carries cargo (myosin V).

Cross-bridge cycle: the repeating cycle in which a myosin head binds actin, makes a power stroke, releases when ATP binds, and is re-cocked by ATP hydrolysis.

Power stroke: the swing of the myosin lever arm, triggered by phosphate release, that moves an actin filament 5–10 nm.

Rigor: the state in which myosin heads stay locked to actin because no ATP is bound; it causes rigor mortis.

Sarcomere: the repeating contractile unit of striated muscle, from one Z-disc to the next, about 2–3 µm long.

Sliding-filament model: muscle shortens because thick and thin filaments slide past each other while keeping their length.

Length–tension curve: the dependence of a muscle’s active force on sarcomere length, set by how much the thick and thin filaments overlap.

Tropomyosin: a rod-shaped protein along the actin filament that covers the myosin-binding sites in resting muscle.

Troponin: a protein complex on tropomyosin that binds Ca²⁺ and then moves tropomyosin so myosin can bind actin.

Contractile ring: a ring of actin and non-muscle myosin II that pinches a dividing cell in two.

Intermediate filament: a 10 nm rope-like, non-polar filament (keratins, vimentin, desmin, neurofilaments, lamins) that gives cells mechanical strength.

Desmosome: a cell–cell junction that anchors intermediate filaments and links the networks of neighbouring cells.

Hemidesmosome: a junction that anchors a cell’s intermediate filaments to the basal lamina.

TIRF microscopy: fluorescence microscopy that lights only a layer about 100 nm thick above the glass, for sharp images of the cell bottom and single molecules.

Traction-force microscopy: a method that calculates the forces a cell exerts from how far beads move in the soft gel the cell grows on.

Mavacamten: a cardiac myosin inhibitor that parks myosin heads, used to treat hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Cell cycle and cell death

The cell-cycle control system

See The cell-cycle control system.

Cell cycle: the ordered series of phases (G1, S, G2, M) by which a cell copies its contents and divides into two.

G0: a resting state outside the cell cycle, entered from G1, in which most cells of the adult body stay.

Cyclin: a protein whose level rises and falls during the cell cycle and that activates a cyclin-dependent kinase when bound to it.

Cyclin-dependent kinase (CDK): a protein kinase that is active only with a cyclin partner and drives the transitions of the cell cycle.

CDK inhibitor (CKI): a protein, such as p21, p27 or p16, that binds a cyclin–CDK complex and blocks its activity.

SCF: a ubiquitin ligase that tags phosphorylated cell-cycle proteins, such as p27 and cyclin E, for destruction by the proteasome.

APC/C (anaphase-promoting complex/cyclosome): a ubiquitin ligase that destroys securin and mitotic cyclins to trigger anaphase and exit from mitosis, and keeps cyclins low in G1.

Rb (retinoblastoma protein): a protein that holds the transcription factor E2F inactive until CDKs phosphorylate it in G1.

E2F: a transcription factor that switches on the genes needed for S phase once it is released from Rb.

Restriction point: the point in G1 after which a cell completes the cycle even if mitogens are removed.

Checkpoint: a control mechanism that stops the cell cycle until a condition is met, for example intact or fully replicated DNA.

p53: a transcription factor that accumulates after DNA damage and stops the cell cycle, mainly through p21, or triggers senescence or apoptosis.

Replication licensing: the loading of helicase rings onto replication origins in G1, which ensures that each origin fires only once per cycle.

FUCCI: a pair of fluorescent reporters, red in G1 and green in S/G2/M, that show the cell-cycle phase of living cells.

CDK4/6 inhibitor: a drug, such as palbociclib, that blocks cyclin D–CDK4/6, so that Rb stays active and cells stop in G1.

Mitosis and cytokinesis

See Mitosis and cytokinesis.

Cohesin: a ring-shaped protein complex that holds sister chromatids together from S phase until anaphase.

Condensin: a ring-shaped complex that uses ATP to fold DNA into loops, compacting chromosomes for mitosis.

Kinetochore: the protein machine on a centromere that attaches a chromatid to spindle microtubules and holds on to their growing and shrinking ends.

Spindle: the bipolar microtubule machine, with two poles and kinetochore, interpolar and astral microtubules, that separates the chromosomes.

Kinesin-5: a four-headed plus-end motor that slides antiparallel microtubules apart and pushes the spindle poles away from each other; the target of monastrol.

Amphitelic attachment: correct attachment, with each sister kinetochore bound to the opposite pole (biorientation).

Syntelic attachment: a wrong attachment in which both sister kinetochores bind the same pole.

Merotelic attachment: a wrong attachment in which one kinetochore binds microtubules from both poles; the checkpoint does not detect it.

Aurora B: a kinase at the inner centromere that releases kinetochore–microtubule attachments that are not under tension.

Spindle assembly checkpoint: the control that blocks anaphase while any kinetochore is unattached.

Mitotic checkpoint complex (MCC): the inhibitor of APC/C–Cdc20 (Mad2, BubR1, Bub3 and Cdc20), made at unattached kinetochores.

Separase: the protease that cleaves cohesin to start anaphase once its inhibitor securin is destroyed.

Securin: the inhibitor of separase, destroyed after ubiquitylation by APC/C–Cdc20.

Abscission: the final cut of the midbody bridge between daughter cells, done by ESCRT-III.

Aneuploidy: having a wrong number of chromosomes, as in trisomy 21 or many cancers.

Controlling cell numbers

See Controlling cell numbers.

Mitogen: an extracellular signal protein, such as PDGF or EGF, that makes a cell pass the restriction point and divide.

Survival factor: an extracellular signal that keeps a cell alive by suppressing apoptosis.

Contact inhibition: the reversible stop of division when normal cells touch neighbours on all sides; lost in transformed cells.

Population doubling: one doubling of the number of cells in a culture, used to count a culture’s divisions.

Hayflick limit: the limited number of divisions (about 40–60 population doublings) that normal human cells can make in culture.

Replicative senescence: permanent cell-cycle arrest caused by short telomeres after many divisions.

Telomere: the repeated TTAGGG DNA and its protein cap (shelterin) at the end of each chromosome.

End-replication problem: the loss of DNA at chromosome ends at each replication, because the last lagging-strand primer gap cannot be filled.

Telomerase: a reverse transcriptase (TERT) with its own RNA template that adds telomere repeats to chromosome ends.

Oncogene-induced senescence: permanent arrest of a normal cell triggered by an activated oncogene, a barrier to cancer.

SASP: the senescence-associated secretory phenotype, the inflammatory cytokines and proteases that senescent cells secrete.

Crisis: the stage when cells without p53/Rb keep dividing until critically short telomeres cause chromosome fusions and most cells die.

Senolytic: a drug that selectively kills senescent cells, for example by blocking BCL-xL.

Apoptosis

See Apoptosis.

Apoptosis: a programmed, tidy form of cell death in which the cell shrinks, blebs and breaks into apoptotic bodies that are eaten without inflammation.

Caspase: a cysteine protease, made as an inactive procaspase, that cuts proteins after an aspartate; initiators (caspase-8, -9) activate executioners (caspase-3, -7).

BCL-2 family: proteins that decide on MOMP: the guardians (BCL-2, BCL-XL, MCL-1), the effectors (BAX, BAK) and the BH3-only sensors.

BH3-only protein: a stress-activated BCL-2-family member (e.g. BIM, BID, PUMA, NOXA, BAD) whose BH3 helix blocks the guardians or activates BAX/BAK.

BAX/BAK: effector proteins of the BCL-2 family that, once activated, form pores in the outer mitochondrial membrane.

MOMP (mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilisation): the all-or-none opening of the outer mitochondrial membrane by BAX/BAK, which releases cytochrome c and SMAC.

Apoptosome: a wheel of seven APAF1 molecules, assembled by cytosolic cytochrome c, that activates caspase-9.

IAP (inhibitor of apoptosis): a protein such as XIAP that binds and blocks active caspases; SMAC released at MOMP removes it.

Death receptor: a cell-surface receptor (e.g. Fas, TNFR1, TRAIL receptors) that recruits FADD and caspase-8 into the DISC when its ligand binds.

tBID: the caspase-8-cut form of BID, which activates BAX/BAK and links the extrinsic pathway to the mitochondria.

Efferocytosis: phagocytosis of apoptotic cells, guided by “find me” (e.g. ATP) and “eat me” (phosphatidylserine) signals; it calms the phagocyte.

Annexin V/PI assay: a flow cytometry test in which annexin V binds exposed phosphatidylserine and PI stains cells with a broken membrane, separating live, early apoptotic and late apoptotic cells.

TUNEL: a staining method in which terminal transferase adds labelled nucleotides to DNA ends, marking apoptotic nuclei in cells or tissue sections.

BH3 mimetic: a drug that copies the BH3 helix and fills a guardian’s groove, releasing BH3-only proteins (e.g. venetoclax).

Venetoclax: a BH3 mimetic that selectively blocks BCL-2, used in chronic lymphocytic leukaemia and acute myeloid leukaemia.

Cell death and disease

Beyond apoptosis: necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis

See Beyond apoptosis: necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis.

Regulated necrosis: cell death in which the cell’s own proteins break the plasma membrane, so it can be blocked by a drug or a gene knockout.

Accidental necrosis: cell death caused directly by overwhelming damage (heat, freezing, detergent, loss of ATP), with no programme involved.

Necroptosis: regulated lytic death in which RIPK1 and RIPK3 activate MLKL, which breaks the plasma membrane; a backup when caspase-8 is blocked.

MLKL: the executioner of necroptosis; once phosphorylated by RIPK3, it forms oligomers that rupture the plasma membrane.

Necrostatin-1: a small-molecule inhibitor of the RIPK1 kinase, used as a research tool to block necroptosis.

Pyroptosis: inflammatory lytic death, mainly of immune cells, in which caspase-1 cuts gasdermin D into pore-forming fragments.

Inflammasome: a protein complex (e.g. NLRP3 + ASC + pro-caspase-1) that senses danger and activates caspase-1.

Gasdermin D: a protein whose N-terminal fragment, released by caspase-1, forms large pores in the plasma membrane.

IL-1β (interleukin-1β): a powerful inflammatory cytokine, activated by caspase-1 and released through gasdermin pores.

Ferroptosis: iron-dependent cell death caused by runaway peroxidation of polyunsaturated membrane lipids.

Lipid peroxidation: a radical chain reaction that oxidises polyunsaturated fatty acid tails in membranes.

GPX4 (glutathione peroxidase 4): a selenium enzyme that uses glutathione to reduce lipid peroxides in membranes, protecting cells from ferroptosis.

Ferrostatin-1: a radical-trapping antioxidant that stops lipid peroxidation and blocks ferroptosis.

DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns): molecules normally inside cells (e.g. HMGB1, ATP, DNA) that trigger inflammation when released by dying cells.

z-VAD: a pan-caspase inhibitor that blocks apoptosis and also pyroptosis (via caspase-1).

Cancer as a disease of cell biology

See Cancer as a disease of cell biology.

Oncogene: a mutated or overactive form of a normal growth-promoting gene (proto-oncogene) that drives cancer when one copy is altered.

Proto-oncogene: a normal gene whose protein promotes growth, division or survival, such as RAS or EGFR.

Tumour suppressor: a gene whose protein brakes growth, repairs DNA or triggers death; both copies must be lost for it to contribute to cancer.

Two-hit hypothesis: Knudson’s idea that both copies of a tumour suppressor must be inactivated, one of which can be inherited.

Hallmarks of cancer: the set of capabilities a cell must gain to become a cancer cell, such as sustained growth signals and escape from apoptosis.

Genome instability: an increased rate of mutations or chromosome changes in a cell lineage, which speeds up cancer evolution.

Clonal evolution: the process by which new mutations and selection produce competing subclones within a tumour.

Tumour heterogeneity: the presence of genetically and functionally different subclones within one tumour.

Carcinoma in situ: a carcinoma whose cells have not yet crossed the basement membrane.

Epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT): a programme in which epithelial cells lose junctions and polarity and become migratory, mesenchymal-like cells.

Metastasis: the spread of cancer cells from the primary tumour to form new tumours in distant organs.

Tumour microenvironment: the non-cancer cells, vessels and matrix around tumour cells that influence their growth and spread.

Warburg effect: the use of glycolysis by many cancer cells even when oxygen is present.

Immune checkpoint inhibitor: an antibody that blocks a T-cell brake such as PD-1, PD-L1 or CTLA-4, so that T cells can attack the tumour.

When cell biology goes wrong: a disease casebook

See When cell biology goes wrong: a disease casebook.

Loss of function: a mutation that removes or weakens a protein’s normal activity; usually recessive.

Gain of function: a mutation that makes a protein too active or gives it a new, harmful activity; often dominant.

Toxic aggregate: misfolded protein that clumps (often as amyloid) and damages the cell.

Corrector: a small molecule that helps a misfolded protein fold and leave the ER (e.g. elexacaftor for CFTR).

Potentiator: a small molecule that holds an ion channel open longer (e.g. ivacaftor for CFTR).

Enzyme replacement therapy: infusion of a missing enzyme that cells take up by receptor-mediated endocytosis and deliver to lysosomes.

Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO): a short, modified nucleic-acid strand that pairs with one RNA to change its splicing or have it destroyed.

siRNA (as a drug): a short double-stranded RNA that guides RISC to cut one mRNA, so less of its protein is made.

Gene therapy: delivery of a working gene, often in an AAV virus shell, so cells make a missing protein.

I-cell disease: lack of the Golgi enzyme that adds the mannose-6-phosphate tag, so lysosomal enzymes are secreted instead of reaching lysosomes.

Heteroplasmy: the presence of both normal and mutant mitochondrial DNA in one cell; disease appears above a threshold fraction.

Primary ciliary dyskinesia: an inherited loss of axonemal dynein function, so motile cilia do not beat.
