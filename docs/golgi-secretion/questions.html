Study questions: the Golgi and the secretory pathway

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Describe the route of a secreted protein from its synthesis to the outside of the cell. Name each compartment and say which kind of carrier moves it between them. [ELO 4]

Model answer

The protein is made by ribosomes on the rough ER and enters the ER lumen during synthesis. After folding and quality control it leaves the ER in COPII-coated vesicles and reaches the ERGIC (ER–Golgi intermediate compartment). From there it enters the cis Golgi and crosses the stack (cis → medial → trans), mainly by cisternal maturation: the cisterna that contains it matures while COPI vesicles bring Golgi enzymes back. In the trans-Golgi network (TGN) it is sorted; a protein with no special signal leaves in a constitutive carrier (vesicle or tubule) that fuses with the plasma membrane, releasing the protein outside. In a cell with regulated secretion it may instead be stored in a secretory granule until a Ca²⁺ signal. Throughout, the protein stays in the lumen of membrane compartments and never crosses a membrane after the ER.

2. Explain how a COPII vesicle forms at the ER. Include the role of the small GTPase and how cargo is selected. [ELO 4]

Model answer

A GEF in the ER membrane (Sec12) loads GTP onto the small GTPase Sar1. Sar1-GTP inserts an amphipathic helix into the cytosolic leaflet, which starts to bend the membrane, and recruits the inner coat (Sec23/24). The inner coat binds sorting signals in the cytosolic tails of membrane cargo (for example a di-acidic DxE signal) and of cargo receptors, which in turn bind soluble cargo in the lumen. The outer coat (Sec13/31) forms a cage that shapes the bud, and the vesicle pinches off. GTP hydrolysis by Sar1 then releases the coat, so the vesicle can fuse and the coat proteins can be reused. Because the GEF is only in the ER, COPII coats form only there.

3. Compare the three main vesicle coats: where does each work, which way does it carry cargo, and what switches it on? [ELO 4]

Model answer
Coat Where Direction Switch
COPII ER exit sites forward: ER → ERGIC/Golgi Sar1
COPI ERGIC, Golgi backward: Golgi → ER, and between cisternae ARF1
Clathrin (with adaptors AP1, AP2) TGN, plasma membrane TGN → endosomes; plasma membrane → endosomes (endocytosis) ARF1 at the TGN

All three bend the membrane and select cargo through binding of sorting signals (directly, or through adaptors in the case of clathrin). All are released after the vesicle forms.

4. Describe how SNARE proteins fuse a vesicle with its target membrane, and what NSF does. [ELO 4]

Model answer

The vesicle carries a v-SNARE (e.g. VAMP/synaptobrevin) and the target membrane carries t-SNAREs (e.g. syntaxin and SNAP-25). When the vesicle has been tethered close to the target, the SNARE motifs (one from the v-SNARE, three from the t-SNAREs) wind together into a four-helix bundle. Assembly zips from the end far from the membranes towards the membranes, pulling the two bilayers into contact; the energy released overcomes the repulsion between them and the lipids merge (fusion). Afterwards all four helices sit in the same membrane (a cis-SNARE complex). The ATPase NSF, with its adaptor α-SNAP, uses ATP to pull the complex apart, so the SNAREs can be used again. Different SNARE pairs work at different membranes, which adds specificity on top of Rabs and tethers.

5. What are Rab GTPases, and how do they help a vesicle find the right target membrane? [ELO 4]

Model answer

Rabs are a family of about 60 small GTPases in human cells. In the GTP-bound form a Rab is anchored to a membrane by a lipid tail and binds effector proteins; in the GDP form it is removed into the cytosol by GDI. A GEF switches it on at the right membrane and a GAP switches it off. Each compartment and vesicle type carries its own Rabs (e.g. Rab1 between ER and Golgi, Rab27 on secretory granules), so Rabs work as address labels or membrane identity markers. Effectors on the target, especially tethers (long coiled-coil golgins or multi-subunit complexes such as the exocyst), bind the Rab-GTP on an arriving vesicle and catch it, holding it close enough for the SNAREs to pair.

6. What happens to N-linked glycans in the Golgi, and why are the processing enzymes arranged in a particular order through the stack? [ELO 4, 5]

Model answer

The glycan arrives from the ER as a high-mannose tree (trimmed during ER quality control). In the cis Golgi mannosidases remove more mannose; in the medial Golgi N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) is added and more mannose removed; in the trans Golgi galactose and finally sialic acid are added, giving a complex glycan. Each step needs the product of the step before, and each enzyme sits in the cisterna where its substrate arrives, so the stack works like an assembly line: cis enzymes first, trans enzymes last. The Golgi also adds O-linked sugars, builds proteoglycans and marks lysosomal enzymes with mannose-6-phosphate. Once a glycan has passed the medial Golgi it is resistant to Endo H, which is used to test whether a protein has reached the Golgi.

7. Compare constitutive and regulated secretion: which cells use them, how is the cargo stored, and what triggers release? [ELO 4, 5]

Model answer

Constitutive secretion happens in every cell, all the time: carriers from the TGN fuse with the plasma membrane as soon as they arrive, delivering secreted proteins (collagen, albumin, antibodies) and new membrane proteins and lipids. It is the default route for proteins without a sorting signal, and it is not stored. Regulated secretion happens only in specialised cells (endocrine cells such as β cells, exocrine cells such as pancreatic acinar cells, neurons, mast cells). The cargo aggregates in the TGN and is packed into secretory granules, which mature (acidify, concentrate their content, cut prohormones) and are stored, some docked at the plasma membrane. Release needs a signal that raises cytosolic Ca²⁺; the Ca²⁺ sensor synaptotagmin triggers SNARE-driven fusion.

8. How are lysosomal enzymes sorted away from secreted proteins in the trans-Golgi network? [ELO 4]

Model answer

In the cis Golgi, lysosomal enzymes are recognised by a phosphotransferase that adds a phosphate to mannose on their N-linked glycans: the mannose-6-phosphate (M6P) tag. In the TGN, the M6P receptor, a transmembrane protein, binds the tagged enzymes on the lumen side, and its cytosolic tail is collected by AP1 and clathrin into vesicles that go to endosomes. In the more acidic endosome (pH about 6) the enzyme lets go of the receptor and moves on to the lysosome; the receptor returns to the TGN for another round. Enzymes that lack the tag are not captured and are secreted by the default route.

VG-style questions

9. [VG] A yeast sec mutant grows at 25 °C but stops secreting at 37 °C, and its cells fill with small vesicles. A second mutant fills with ER membranes at 37 °C. Predict what you would see in the double mutant at 37 °C, and explain how such experiments revealed the order of the steps in the secretory pathway. What kind of proteins might the two genes encode? [ELO 1, 4]

Model answer

The double mutant would fill with ER, like the ER-block mutant alone. Its block comes earlier in the pathway: if cargo cannot leave the ER, no vesicles that would later fail to fuse with the plasma membrane are ever made. So the earlier block decides the phenotype (it is “epistatic”), and comparing double mutants lets you place each gene in the sequence of steps: ER exit before Golgi before post-Golgi vesicles. The vesicle-accumulating gene probably encodes something needed for vesicle targeting or fusion at the plasma membrane, such as a Rab (Sec4), a SNARE (Sec9, related to SNAP-25) or a tether subunit (exocyst). The ER-block gene probably encodes a part of the COPII machinery (e.g. Sec12, the Sar1 GEF, or a coat protein) or something needed for every fusion event, such as NSF (Sec18). Temperature-sensitive alleles are essential here, because a complete loss of secretion would kill the cell, so the mutant could not be grown.

10. [VG] You want to know whether a new drug candidate blocks transport from the ER to the Golgi, or a later step. Design a live-cell imaging experiment, with controls, and say what result would point to each answer. [ELO 1, 4]

Model answer

Use a synchronised cargo wave: cells expressing a RUSH reporter (a GFP-tagged cargo with a streptavidin-binding peptide, held on an ER hook with streptavidin), or VSVG-GFP ts045 held in the ER at 40 °C. Add the drug (and a solvent-only control) for a short time, then release the cargo with biotin (or shift to 32 °C) and image every minute for an hour. Mark the Golgi with a second colour (e.g. a red Golgi enzyme) and measure the green fluorescence in the ER, the Golgi and at the cell surface (surface: an antibody against an outside epitope on non-permeabilised cells, or a pH-sensitive GFP). Controls: solvent only (normal wave: Golgi peak after about 10–20 min, surface after 30–60 min); brefeldin A or the 15 °C block as a known ER-to-Golgi block; the 20 °C block as a known TGN block; cell viability, to rule out a general toxic effect. Interpretation: if the drug blocks ER exit, the cargo stays in the ER-like network and the Golgi never lights up (as with BFA or 15 °C; with 15 °C, cargo collects in ERGIC puncta). If it blocks a later step, the cargo reaches the Golgi on time but stays there or in the TGN (like 20 °C), and the surface signal does not rise. If it blocks fusion at the plasma membrane, cargo accumulates in vesicles near the cell edge. A biochemical check: Endo H resistance of the cargo over time tells you when it reaches the medial Golgi.

11. [VG] Patients with I-cell disease secrete their lysosomal enzymes into the blood. Yet enzyme replacement therapy, in which a recombinant lysosomal enzyme is given into the blood, works for several other lysosomal storage diseases. Explain both observations with the mannose-6-phosphate pathway, and predict whether giving a normal lysosomal enzyme (with M6P) by infusion could help a patient with I-cell disease. [ELO 4, 9]

Model answer

In I-cell disease the phosphotransferase that adds M6P in the cis Golgi is missing, so all lysosomal enzymes lack the tag, are not bound by the M6P receptor in the TGN and leave the cell by constitutive secretion: the blood has high enzyme levels while the lysosomes are empty of enzymes. In other storage diseases, only one enzyme is missing, and the cell’s sorting machinery is intact. Some M6P receptors also sit at the plasma membrane: they bind M6P-tagged enzymes from the blood and take them in by endocytosis, delivering them to endosomes and lysosomes. Recombinant enzymes made with M6P on their glycans therefore reach the lysosomes of the patient’s cells. In I-cell disease, an infused enzyme with M6P could in principle be taken up the same way, because the patient’s M6P receptors are normal. But the patient lacks M6P on dozens of different lysosomal enzymes, so replacing one would fix only a small part of the problem; replacing all of them is not practical. (Treatment is currently mainly supportive, with haematopoietic stem-cell transplantation tried in some patients.)

E1. Membrane protein, 37 °C. Release the cargo and follow it for 60 minutes. When does the Golgi signal peak, and what fraction of the cargo is in the Golgi stack at that moment? When has half of the cargo reached the cell surface? Why is the Golgi never filled with all of the cargo at once? [ELO 4]

E2. Predict first. Set 15 °C before release and run for 90 minutes. Where will the cargo collect, and why there? Then set 37 °C: what happens? [ELO 4]

E3. [VG] Predict first. Reset, set 20 °C, release and run for 90 minutes. Where does the cargo collect? Then switch to 37 °C and measure how long it takes until half of the cargo is at the surface. Compare with E1 (half at the surface measured from release at 37 °C). Why do cell biologists often use a 20 °C block before imaging the last step of secretion? [ELO 1, 4]

E4. Membrane protein, 37 °C. Release and let the wave run for 20 minutes, then add brefeldin A. Watch for 20 minutes. What happens to the Golgi and to the cargo that was in it? What happens to the cargo in the TGN and at the surface? Then remove brefeldin A and watch for another 20 minutes. Explain with ARF1 and COPI. [ELO 4, 5]

E5. [VG] Predict first. Choose the hormone cargo, 37 °C. Release and wait 60 minutes: where is the hormone, and how much has been secreted? Then switch on the Ca²⁺ stimulus and read the secreted fraction after 1, 5 and 10 minutes. Describe the shape of the secretion curve and explain it with docked and reserve granules. Which feature of this curve is lost early in type 2 diabetes? [ELO 4, 5, 9]

E6. [VG] Add the toxin before release and repeat (a) the membrane protein for 60 minutes, (b) the hormone with a Ca²⁺ stimulus at 60 minutes, and (c) the lysosomal enzyme for 60 minutes. Where does the cargo end up in each case? Why is the lysosomal route not blocked? Finally, choose the lysosomal enzyme without the toxin and untick “M6P tag on the enzyme”: where does the enzyme go now, and which disease does this model? [ELO 4, 9]

Solutions

E1. Half of the cargo has left the ER after about 6 minutes. The Golgi stack peaks after about 11 minutes, holding only about 35 % of the cargo; the TGN peaks a little later (about 30 % at 20 minutes). Half of the cargo is at the surface after about 28 minutes, and about 95 % after an hour. These times are of the same order as real VSVG and RUSH experiments (Golgi after 10–20 min, surface within an hour). The Golgi never holds everything at once because the wave spreads out: each step is random for each molecule, so some cargo leaves the Golgi before the last cargo has left the ER. The cargo is like a group of runners who start together but spread out along the course.

E2. At 15 °C the cargo leaves the ER slowly (every step is slower in the cold) and collects in the ERGIC: about 90 % after 90 minutes (in the model, about 7 % is still in the ER), and none reaches the Golgi. At 15 °C transport from the ERGIC to the Golgi stops, so cargo accumulates just before that step; this is how the ERGIC was first identified as a separate station (it looks like a set of dots near the Golgi). When you warm to 37 °C, the cargo moves into the Golgi within a few minutes (the Golgi peaks at nearly 60 % about 5 minutes after warming, much higher than the 35 % in E1), then on to the TGN and the surface as a sharp wave (about half at the surface 20 minutes after warming).

E3. At 20 °C the cargo passes the ER and the Golgi slowly but cannot leave the TGN: after 90 minutes about 86 % is in the TGN (11 % still in the Golgi) and none at the surface. After the shift to 37 °C, 10 % is at the surface after about 3 minutes and half after about 10 minutes (about 22 % at 5 min, 49 % at 10 min, 79 % at 20 min). From release at 37 °C (E1), half took about 28 minutes, and the first 10 % alone took 14 minutes. The 20 °C block collects the cargo at one station, so the last step starts from the same place at the same moment: a sharper, synchronised wave that separates the TGN-to-surface step from the earlier ones. (Real 20 °C blocks are usually 1–2 hours, often with a protein-synthesis inhibitor so that no new cargo enters.)

E4. At 20 minutes about 26 % of the cargo is in the Golgi, 30 % in the TGN and 25 % at the surface. With brefeldin A the Golgi fades within about 5 minutes (the banner says it has merged into the ER), and the cargo in the Golgi and ERGIC returns to the ER: after 20 minutes in BFA, about 34 % of the cargo is in the ER, nothing is in the Golgi, about 27 % stays in the TGN and the surface stops rising at about 39 % (the cargo already in vesicles still arrives). BFA blocks the GEF that activates ARF1; without ARF1-GTP, COPI coats (and the ARF1-dependent clathrin/AP1 coats at the TGN) cannot form. The Golgi is kept apart from the ER only by constant traffic, so its membranes tubulate and fuse with the ER. In the model, cargo cannot leave the ER in BFA, and the TGN (which in real cells merges with endosomes, not with the ER) keeps its cargo. After washout the Golgi re-forms in about 10–20 minutes and the cargo moves on: about 60 % is at the surface 20 minutes after washout. In a real experiment, the cargo that went back to the ER now carries Golgi-modified (Endo H-resistant) glycans although it sits in the ER, which was one of the clues that Golgi enzymes really move into the ER.

E5. After 60 minutes almost all of the hormone (about 95 %) is in secretory granules and only about 1 % has been secreted: regulated cargo is stored, not released. With the Ca²⁺ stimulus, secretion is about 14 % after 1 minute, 26 % after 5 minutes and 35 % after 10 minutes: a fast first phase followed by a slower second phase (if you keep Ca²⁺ high, about 50 % after 20 minutes). The first phase is the docked granules (about a fifth of the stored granules in the model), which fuse within a minute or two once Ca²⁺ rises. The second phase is limited by how fast reserve granules move to the membrane and dock. When the stimulus stops, secretion stops. In β cells, insulin release has the same two phases, and loss of the first phase is one of the earliest signs of type 2 diabetes.

E6. (a) The membrane protein reaches the end of the pathway but does not fuse: after 60 minutes about 95 % sits in vesicles docked at the plasma membrane and 0 % is at the surface. Docking needs Rabs and tethers, which the toxin does not touch; fusion needs the SNARE zipper, which is cut. (b) The hormone is stored in granules as before (about 95 % at 60 min), but with Ca²⁺ nothing is secreted (0 % after 10 and 20 minutes): docked granules cannot fuse. (c) The lysosomal enzyme still reaches the lysosomes (about 93 % after 60 min). Vesicles to endosomes and lysosomes fuse with different SNAREs that the toxin does not cut; botulinum and tetanus toxins cut only the SNAREs of exocytosis (VAMP, SNAP-25, syntaxin of the plasma membrane). In a real body the toxins act only in nerve cells, because only neurons have the receptors that take the toxin in. Without the M6P tag, the enzyme is not captured in the TGN and follows the default constitutive route: about 95 % is secreted after 60 minutes and the lysosomes get none. This models I-cell disease (mucolipidosis II), in which the enzyme that adds M6P is missing.
