Study questions: beyond apoptosis

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. Define regulated necrosis and explain how it differs from accidental necrosis and from apoptosis.

Model answer

Regulated necrosis is cell death in which the cell’s own proteins break the plasma membrane, so the cell swells and bursts. Because proteins carry it out, it can be prevented by an inhibitor or a gene knockout of those proteins. Accidental necrosis is caused directly by overwhelming damage (heat, freezing, detergent, crushing, total ATP loss); no programme is involved and it cannot be prevented that way. Apoptosis is also regulated, but the membrane stays intact: the cell shrinks, fragments into apoptotic bodies and is eaten without inflammation. Regulated necrosis (necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis) is lytic and inflammatory.

2. Describe the mechanism of necroptosis, starting from TNF binding its receptor. Explain the role of caspase-8.

Model answer

TNF binds its receptor (TNFR1), and a complex forms with the kinase RIPK1 and caspase-8. Usually TNF switches on survival genes through NF-κB. If death is triggered, active caspase-8 cuts RIPK1 and RIPK3, which keeps necroptosis off; the cell dies by apoptosis (or survives). If caspase-8 is blocked (by z-VAD or a viral inhibitor) or absent, RIPK1 and RIPK3 bind each other and form a fibre, the necrosome. RIPK3 becomes active and phosphorylates MLKL. Phospho-MLKL forms oligomers that move to the plasma membrane and break it; the cell swells and bursts and releases DAMPs. Necrostatin-1 blocks the RIPK1 kinase. Caspase-8 is thus both an initiator of apoptosis and the brake on necroptosis.

3. Why is it useful for the body to have necroptosis as a backup programme?

Model answer

Many viruses need the infected cell alive while they multiply, so they make inhibitors of apoptosis, for example caspase-8 inhibitors (poxviruses). Blocking caspase-8 releases RIPK1/RIPK3 and triggers necroptosis, so the infected cell dies anyway, removing the virus’s “factory”. Because necroptosis is lytic, DAMPs are released, which alerts the immune system. Some viruses have in turn evolved RIPK3 blockers: an evolutionary arms race.

4. Explain how the inflammasome leads to pyroptosis. Include the two signals, caspase-1, gasdermin D and IL-1β.

Model answer

Pyroptosis happens mainly in immune cells such as macrophages. Signal 1 (priming), e.g. LPS on TLR4, activates NF-κB, and the cell makes pro-IL-1β and the sensor NLRP3. Signal 2 (danger), e.g. a pore-forming toxin, K⁺ loss, uric acid crystals or lysosome damage, activates NLRP3, which recruits the adaptor ASC and pro-caspase-1: the inflammasome. Here caspase-1 activates itself. It cuts gasdermin D, releasing its N-terminal half from the inhibitory C-terminal half; the N-terminal halves insert into the inner leaflet of the plasma membrane and form large pores (about 20 nm). Caspase-1 also cuts pro-IL-1β to active IL-1β, which leaves through the pores. Water enters, the cell swells and bursts, releasing DAMPs: a strongly inflammatory death.

5. Explain what happens in ferroptosis. Include iron, polyunsaturated lipids, GPX4 and glutathione.

Model answer

Membrane phospholipids with polyunsaturated tails have weak C–H bonds next to their double bonds. A radical removes such a hydrogen, the tail reacts with O₂ and becomes a peroxyl radical, which attacks the next tail: a chain reaction of lipid peroxidation. Free iron (Fe²⁺) accelerates it by turning lipid hydroperoxides into new radicals. The guard is GPX4, a selenium enzyme that reduces lipid hydroperoxides (L–OOH) to harmless alcohols (L–OH) using two molecules of glutathione (GSH → GSSG). Glutathione needs cysteine, imported as cystine through system xc⁻. When GPX4 is blocked (RSL3), cystine import is blocked (erastin) so glutathione runs out, or iron is high, peroxides accumulate, the bilayer becomes leaky and ruptures. No caspases are involved. Ferrostatin-1 (a radical trap) and iron chelators prevent it.

6. Explain what DAMPs are and why the mode of cell death matters to the tissue.

Model answer

DAMPs (damage-associated molecular patterns) are molecules that are normally inside cells and act as alarms when they appear outside: HMGB1, ATP, IL-1α, DNA, uric acid. Immune cells detect them with receptors of the same families used for microbes (Toll-like receptors, P2X7) and respond with inflammatory cytokines and recruitment of neutrophils. Apoptosis keeps the contents inside an intact membrane; the cell is eaten and the phagocyte releases calming signals (TGF-β, IL-10), so tissue is cleaned up silently. Lytic deaths release DAMPs (and IL-1β in pyroptosis) and cause inflammation. This helps fight infection but harms the tissue when it runs too much, as in sepsis, gout or after a stroke or heart attack.

7. How is autophagy related to cell death?

Model answer

Autophagy wraps cytoplasm in a double-membrane autophagosome and delivers it to lysosomes. It is mostly a survival process: it recycles material in starvation and removes damaged organelles (e.g. mitochondria). Dying cells are often full of autophagosomes, but when an essential ATG gene is knocked out, cells usually die sooner, showing that autophagy was trying to save them: death with autophagy, not by it. True autophagy-dependent death is rare (e.g. fly salivary glands in metamorphosis). Autophagy can also promote ferroptosis, because digesting the iron-storage protein ferritin releases free iron.

8. For apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis and ferroptosis, give one marker you can measure and one inhibitor you can use as a research tool.

Model answer
Death Marker Inhibitor (tool)
Apoptosis Cleaved caspase-3; annexin V⁺ PI⁻ cells z-VAD (all caspases)
Necroptosis Phospho-MLKL Necrostatin-1 (RIPK1 kinase)
Pyroptosis Cleaved gasdermin D (N-terminal fragment); IL-1β release MCC950 (NLRP3); caspase-1 inhibitors
Ferroptosis Lipid peroxides (e.g. C11-BODIPY) Ferrostatin-1; iron chelators (deferoxamine)

VG-style questions

9. [VG] Mouse fibroblasts that express RIPK3 are treated with TNF + cycloheximide and die with cleaved caspase-3. (a) Predict what happens when you add z-VAD, and which marker appears. (b) What must you add to save the cells, and why? (c) What does this tell you about the relation between apoptosis and necroptosis?

Model answer
  1. z-VAD blocks caspase-8 (and caspase-3), so cleaved caspase-3 disappears, but the cells still die: RIPK1 and RIPK3 are no longer cut by caspase-8, the necrosome forms and phospho-MLKL appears. The cells swell and become PI⁺ early, without an annexin V⁺ PI⁻ stage. (b) Add necrostatin-1 (RIPK1 kinase inhibitor) together with z-VAD, or use RIPK3- or MLKL-knockout cells with z-VAD. Both routes must be blocked. (c) The two programmes start from the same signalling complex; caspase-8 is the switch. Apoptosis is the first choice, and necroptosis is the backup when caspase-8 cannot act. Blocking one death programme can switch the cell to another, which matters when designing drugs.

10. [VG] A new compound kills cultured macrophages. Design a set of experiments that decides whether they die by apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis or accidental necrosis. Say which controls you need and which result would point to each answer.

Model answer

Controls: untreated cells (vehicle), and the compound alone, measured at the same time. Step 1: annexin V/PI by flow cytometry over time: an annexin V⁺ PI⁻ population before PI⁺ points to apoptosis; early PI⁺ without that stage points to a lytic death. Step 2 (markers), by western blot or ELISA: cleaved caspase-3 (apoptosis), phospho-MLKL (necroptosis), N-terminal fragment of gasdermin D and IL-1β in the medium (pyroptosis; macrophages need priming to make pro-IL-1β), lipid peroxides with C11-BODIPY (ferroptosis). Step 3 (rescue), pretreat with z-VAD, necrostatin-1, MCC950 and ferrostatin-1, each alone, and measure viability. The answer is the death whose marker appears and whose specific inhibitor rescues: z-VAD only with caspase-3 → apoptosis (remember z-VAD also blocks caspase-1); necrostatin-1 + phospho-MLKL → necroptosis; MCC950 + gasdermin D cleavage + IL-1β → pyroptosis; ferrostatin-1 + lipid peroxides → ferroptosis. No rescue, no marker, near-immediate PI⁺ → accidental necrosis (e.g. a detergent-like compound). Confirm with knockouts (e.g. MLKL⁻/⁻, GSDMD⁻/⁻, or GPX4 overexpression), since inhibitors are not perfectly specific.

11. [VG] In mice, necrostatin-1 makes brain infarcts smaller after a stroke. (a) Which cells in the infarct can such a drug save, and which can it not? (b) Give two reasons why a drug that works in mice may still fail in patients.

Model answer
  1. In the core, cells lose ATP within minutes, the Na⁺/K⁺ pump stops, they swell and burst: mostly accidental necrosis, which no inhibitor of a programme can prevent. In the border zone (penumbra) and during reperfusion, cells die over hours to days, partly by regulated death (necroptosis, ferroptosis, apoptosis) and inflammation; these are the cells a RIPK1 inhibitor can save, because RIPK1 is necessary for their death. (b) Possible reasons: in mice the drug is often given before or right at the stroke, while patients arrive hours later; mouse strokes are standardised (young, healthy animals), patients are old with other diseases; several death programmes run in parallel, so blocking one may not be enough; the drug may not reach the brain at the right dose in humans, or have side effects; animal studies are small and may overestimate effects. (You meet trial design in Kliniska prövningar och läkemedelsutveckling.)

Explore

Which death is it? Each case gives you cells and a stimulus. Design experiments: with or without the stimulus, with any of four inhibitors (given 1 h before), and with the readouts you choose. Press Run experiment for each condition; the results collect in a table. When you are sure, name the death type. The feedback tells you which results decide the case and whether your own experiments already prove it. The results come from a rule table based on the textbook experimental logic (open “How the results are made” at the bottom of the demo), not from a simulation; the numbers are illustrative.

E1. Case Staurosporine. Run a control without the stimulus and the stimulus alone, with annexin V/PI, morphology and cleaved caspase-3. Then add z-VAD. Which two results point to apoptosis, and which result shows that caspases are necessary for this death?

E2. Predict first. Compare TNF alone and TNF + cycloheximide in the same cells. Why does adding a protein-synthesis inhibitor turn TNF into a killer?

E3. [VG] Case TNF + cycloheximide. Predict what z-VAD will do before you run it, then run it with annexin V/PI, cleaved caspase-3 and phospho-MLKL. What happened, and what must you add to save the cells? Explain with the role of caspase-8.

E4. Case Bacterial toxin (nigericin). Show that z-VAD saves the cells. Does that prove apoptosis? Which two readouts and which inhibitor decide the case?

E5. Cases GPX4 inhibitor (RSL3) and Cystine uptake block (erastin). Which readout and which inhibitor show ferroptosis? The readouts for erastin are taken later (16 h) than for RSL3 (12 h). Explain why erastin needs more time.

E6. [VG] Press Mystery compound three times (each press gives a new compound in primed macrophages). Solve each case with as few experiments as possible, and get a “complete argument” in the feedback. Describe your strategy. What is the smallest number of experiments that can give a complete argument for a lytic death?

Solutions

E1. Control: about 90 % live. Staurosporine: about 29 % live, 44 % annexin V⁺ PI⁻, 27 % PI⁺, a strong cleaved caspase-3 band, and shrunken, blebbing cells with a fragmented nucleus. The large annexin V⁺ PI⁻ population (phosphatidylserine outside while the membrane is intact) and cleaved caspase-3 point to apoptosis; these are markers, so they show the programme is active. With z-VAD about 93 % are alive: caspases are necessary for this death. (The demo notes that the mitochondria are still permeabilised; in real cells many die later by a slow, caspase-independent death.)

E2. TNF alone: about 91 % live, like the control (about 93 %), no markers: no death. TNF switches on NF-κB, which makes survival proteins. With cycloheximide the cell cannot make those proteins, so the death signal from the same receptor wins: about 27 % live, 47 % annexin V⁺ PI⁻, cleaved caspase-3: apoptosis.

E3. With z-VAD the cells still die (about 29 % live), but now 68 % are PI⁺ and only 3 % annexin V⁺ PI⁻, cleaved caspase-3 is gone, and phospho-MLKL appears; the cells are swollen: necroptosis. z-VAD blocked caspase-8, which normally cuts RIPK1 and RIPK3; without that brake the necrosome forms and MLKL breaks the membrane. z-VAD + necrostatin-1 saves them (about 94 % live): both routes must be blocked. Apoptosis is the first choice, necroptosis the backup.

E4. z-VAD saves the cells (about 93 % live), but this does not prove apoptosis: z-VAD also blocks caspase-1. Cleaved caspase-3 stays negative. The deciding results are the N-terminal fragment of gasdermin D and a large IL-1β release (about 2500 pg/mL against about 20 in the control), and rescue by the NLRP3 inhibitor MCC950 (about 91 % live): pyroptosis. Necrostatin-1 and ferrostatin-1 do not help.

E5. Lipid peroxides rise about 8-fold over control (RSL3 about 8.6×, erastin about 8.3×), no caspase-3, phospho-MLKL or gasdermin D cleavage, and ferrostatin-1 rescues (about 92–93 % live), while z-VAD, necrostatin-1 and MCC950 do not. RSL3 blocks GPX4 directly. Erastin blocks cystine import, so the cell must first use up its glutathione before GPX4 stops working; this takes longer.

E6. A good strategy: (1) a control without the compound, with annexin V/PI and morphology; (2) the compound alone with all readouts (one experiment then shows which marker appears); (3) the compound plus the one inhibitor that matches that marker, with a viability readout. Three experiments give a complete argument (control, marker, rescue) for apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis or ferroptosis. Accidental necrosis needs more, because you must show that no inhibitor rescues (the demo asks for at least three of the four, e.g. one experiment with three inhibitors together still counts as tried for each). For a compound that does not kill, the control and the compound alone with a death marker are enough. In the first three presses the compounds cause pyroptosis, ferroptosis and no death.
