Study questions: sorting proteins to the right compartment

Study questions in the style of the written exam. Write your answer first (a few sentences, or a labelled sketch where it helps), then compare with the model answer. Each question is tagged with its learning outcome (ELO). Questions tagged [VG] are of the kind used for the higher grade: predict, compare, explain an unexpected result or design an experiment.

G-level questions

1. What is a sorting signal? Describe three different sorting signals: where each sits on the protein, what it looks like, and where it sends the protein. [ELO 4]

Model answer

A sorting signal is a part of a protein that is recognised by a receptor and decides where the protein goes, like a zip code on a parcel. Most are short stretches of amino acids in which the pattern matters more than the exact sequence. Examples: the ER signal peptide, at the N-terminus, 15–30 amino acids with a hydrophobic core, sends the protein into the ER and is cut off. The NLS, a short basic stretch (e.g. PKKKRKV) almost anywhere in the protein, sends it into the nucleus and stays. PTS1, the last three amino acids (-SKL), sends it into peroxisomes. Others: the mitochondrial presequence (N-terminal positively charged amphipathic helix, cut off in the matrix), KDEL (C-terminal, returns proteins to the ER), the NES (leucine-rich, export from the nucleus), and the mannose-6-phosphate signal, which is a patch on the folded surface rather than a linear sequence.

2. Describe the three ways in which proteins move between compartments. Use the idea of topology (cytosol side and lumen side) to explain why each compartment uses the kind of transport it does. [ELO 4, 5]

Model answer

Gated transport between cytosol and nucleus: the nucleoplasm and the cytosol are one space joined by nuclear pores, so no membrane is crossed; the pore lets small molecules diffuse and lets large folded proteins through only when bound to a transport receptor. Transmembrane transport from the cytosol into the ER, mitochondria and peroxisomes: these are separate spaces, so the protein must cross a membrane through a protein translocator, usually as an unfolded chain (peroxisomes import folded proteins). Vesicular transport from the ER onwards: the ER, Golgi, endosomes, lysosomes and the outside are all on the lumen side, so vesicles can bud from one and fuse with the next and carry proteins without any membrane crossing.

3. Describe the import of a protein with an NLS into the nucleus, step by step. Include the roles of importin α, importin β, the FG repeats and Ran. [ELO 4]

Model answer

In the cytosol, importin α binds the NLS and importin β binds importin α. Importin β interacts with the FG repeats, the disordered Phe-Gly-rich chains that fill the pore channel, and the complex passes through the pore with the cargo folded (about 10 ms). In the nucleus, Ran-GTP binds importin β, the complex changes shape and releases importin α and the cargo. Importin β returns to the cytosol bound to Ran-GTP; there RanGAP makes Ran hydrolyse GTP, Ran-GDP lets go and importin β is free again (importin α is returned by its own exportin). Because Ran-GTP is made in the nucleus by RanGEF on chromatin and destroyed in the cytosol, the cargo is always released on the nuclear side: import is one-way.

4. Compare nuclear import and nuclear export. What is the same, and what is opposite? [ELO 4]

Model answer

Same: both use soluble transport receptors (karyopherins) that pass the FG meshwork of the pore with folded cargo, and both use the Ran-GTP gradient, so both get their energy from Ran’s GTP hydrolysis. Opposite: importins bind cargo (with an NLS) in the cytosol, where Ran-GTP is absent, and release it when Ran-GTP binds in the nucleus. Exportins (e.g. XPO1/CRM1) bind cargo (with a leucine-rich NES) in the nucleus together with Ran-GTP, and release it in the cytosol when GTP is hydrolysed.

5. Describe how a protein made in the cytosol reaches the mitochondrial matrix. Name the complexes and the forces involved. [ELO 4, 5]

Model answer

The precursor is made on free ribosomes and kept unfolded by cytosolic Hsp70 chaperones (post-translational import). Its N-terminal presequence, a positively charged amphipathic helix, binds receptors of the TOM complex in the outer membrane. The chain passes the TOM channel and, at a contact site, the TIM23 channel in the inner membrane. The membrane potential across the inner membrane (about 150–180 mV, matrix negative) pulls the positive presequence in, and mitochondrial Hsp70 pulls the rest using ATP. The matrix processing peptidase removes the presequence, and the protein folds. A hydrophobic segment after the presequence can stop transfer in TIM23 and leave the protein in the inner membrane.

6. How does peroxisomal import differ from import into mitochondria and the ER? [ELO 4, 5]

Model answer

Peroxisomal matrix proteins carry PTS1 (C-terminal -SKL) and are recognised after translation by the cytosolic receptor PEX5, which docks on the peroxisome membrane and takes the cargo in. Unlike the ER and mitochondria, peroxisomes import proteins fully folded, even oligomers, and the receptor enters with its cargo and is recycled. The ER imports during translation (co-translational, SRP-dependent) and mitochondria import unfolded chains through TOM and TIM using the membrane potential. Defects in the PEX genes stop import (Zellweger syndrome).

7. Explain what happens to (a) a soluble ER protein with a C-terminal KDEL, (b) a lysosomal enzyme with a mannose-6-phosphate signal patch, and (c) a soluble protein with only an ER signal peptide. [ELO 4]

Model answer

All three enter the ER lumen by their signal peptide and leave the ER in COPII vesicles. (a) The KDEL protein reaches the cis-Golgi, where the KDEL receptor binds it and returns it to the ER in COPI vesicles: retrieval, so at steady state it is in the ER. (b) In the cis-Golgi an enzyme recognises the signal patch and adds a phosphate to mannose; in the trans-Golgi network the M6P receptor binds the tag, and clathrin-coated vesicles carry it to endosomes, where the acid pH releases it; it ends up in lysosomes. (c) With no further signal it follows the default route through the Golgi and is secreted.

8. Describe an experiment that shows that a protein is imported into mitochondria rather than just bound to their surface. [ELO 1, 4]

Model answer

Make the precursor in a test tube with radioactive amino acids; incubate with isolated mitochondria; then add a protease, which cannot cross membranes and digests everything outside. Analyse by SDS-PAGE and autoradiography. An imported protein is protected from the protease and is often shorter (presequence removed). Controls: without mitochondria the precursor is digested; an uncoupler (CCCP), which collapses the membrane potential, blocks import, so the protein is digested; detergent dissolves the membranes and makes the protein sensitive again, showing that protection was due to the membranes.

VG-level questions

9. [VG] A researcher adds the SV40 NLS to the C-terminus of human serum albumin, which has an N-terminal ER signal peptide. She expects the protein in the nucleus, but finds it in the culture medium. Explain the result, and propose one change to the construct that would put it in the nucleus. [ELO 4]

Model answer

The ER signal peptide is read first, by SRP, while the protein is still being translated. The ribosome is brought to the ER and the whole chain, including the NLS, is threaded into the ER lumen. Importins are in the cytosol and never see the NLS, so it has no effect; with no other signal the protein follows the default route and is secreted into the medium. To send it to the nucleus, remove the signal peptide: the protein is then finished in the cytosol and the NLS can be read by importin α. (Albumin is about 66 kDa, so without an NLS it would stay mainly in the cytosol; with the NLS it is imported. It may fold less well in the cytosol, which is another thing to check.)

10. [VG] A patient’s fibroblasts secrete large amounts of several lysosomal enzymes into the culture medium, and their lysosomes are full of undigested material. The enzymes themselves have normal sequences. (a) Propose which step is defective. (b) Predict what happens if you add purified, correctly tagged lysosomal enzyme to the medium, and explain. [ELO 4, 9]

Model answer
  1. All the enzymes are normal but go to the wrong place, so the shared sorting step is broken: the enzyme in the cis-Golgi that adds the mannose-6-phosphate tag (the GlcNAc phosphotransferase), as in I-cell disease. Without M6P, the M6P receptor in the trans-Golgi network cannot bind the enzymes, and they follow the default route and are secreted. (A defective M6P receptor would give a similar picture.) (b) Some M6P receptors are also on the plasma membrane. Tagged enzyme added to the medium binds them, is taken up by endocytosis and delivered to lysosomes, so the stored material should be degraded. This is the principle of enzyme replacement therapy for lysosomal storage diseases (endocytosis lecture).

11. [VG] Design an experiment to test whether a new 80 kDa protein, which has a short basic stretch in the middle, is imported into the nucleus because of that stretch. Say what result you expect if the hypothesis is right, and include the controls. [ELO 1, 4]

Model answer

Make GFP fusions and express them in cultured cells; image living cells by fluorescence microscopy with a DNA stain (e.g. Hoechst) to mark the nucleus, and quantify the nuclear/cytosolic ratio in many cells. Constructs: (1) the full protein–GFP: if the hypothesis is right, it is nuclear. (2) The same protein with the basic stretch mutated (e.g. lysines to alanine or threonine): it should stay cytosolic (necessary). (3) The basic stretch alone fused to a large cytosolic reporter (e.g. GFP fused to a large enzyme, or two to three GFPs in a row, too big to diffuse): it should become nuclear (sufficient). Controls: GFP alone (small, spreads everywhere) and the large reporter without the stretch (cytosolic). A dominant-negative or depleted importin α/β, or a drug that blocks import, should keep construct 1 out of the nucleus.

Explore

Demo: zip-code game. Build a protein from sorting signals: an N-terminal ER signal peptide or mitochondrial presequence, a transmembrane (TM) segment, an NLS, a C-terminal KDEL or PTS1, and a mannose-6-phosphate signal patch; choose whether the protein is small (like GFP) or large. Or start from a real protein. Predict where it ends up, then press Run and follow its route on the cell map. Time runs in real time by default (the clock shows minutes since translation began); choose a faster speed if you do not want to wait, or “Show the end now”. The result box explains the outcome, greys out signals that have no effect, and says why.

The rules are the textbook ones: the ER decision is made during translation, and a signal works only on the side of the membrane where its receptor is. Combinations that real cells resolve in complicated ways (e.g. two cytosolic signals on one protein) are marked not predicted rather than given an invented answer. The times are typical, rounded values.

E1. Choose Albumin. Predict, then run it at real time for one minute: what happens first, and where is the ribosome? Then speed up. Where does albumin end, and how long does the whole route take? Now add KDEL at the C-terminus and run again. Where does it end, and does KDEL stop the protein from leaving the ER? [ELO 4]

E2. Compare GFP alone, the same protein made large, and GFP + NLS. Where does each end up, and why? Which one is the “necessary and sufficient” experiment from the lecture? [ELO 4]

E3. Choose Cathepsin D. Predict and run. Then remove its ER signal peptide (N-terminus: none) and run again. What happens to the M6P signal, and why? [ELO 4]

E4. [VG] Predict first. (a) Add an NLS to Albumin. (b) Add KDEL to the LDL receptor. For each, write down your prediction with a reason before you run. Then explain why the extra signal has no effect in both cases, using the word topology. [ELO 4, 5]

E5. [VG] Choose Citrate synthase and add an NLS. The game says “not predicted”. Why is this combination not decided by a simple rule? Design an experiment that would tell you where such a protein really goes. [ELO 1, 4]

E6. Give a protein a mitochondrial presequence and a TM segment. Where does it end up, and which way does it face? Compare the time it takes with the route of the LDL receptor to the plasma membrane. Why is one so much faster? [ELO 4, 5]

Solutions

E1. In the first minute the ribosome makes the chain; SRP takes the ribosome to the ER, and the chain enters the ER while it is made (the ribosome moves to the ER and lets go after translation). Albumin is secreted: ER (folding, about 5 min) → COPII vesicle to the Golgi (about 10 min) → through the Golgi (about 20 min) → secretory vesicle to the plasma membrane (about 10 min), about 46 min in total. With KDEL it ends in the ER lumen after about 21 min: it still leaves the ER in a COPII vesicle, and is brought back from the cis-Golgi by the KDEL receptor in a COPI vesicle. KDEL does not stop exit; it works by retrieval.

E2. GFP alone (27 kDa) ends in the cytosol and nucleus: it has no signal, but is small enough to diffuse through the pores (it spreads in about 10 min in the game). The large version stays in the cytosol: too big for the pores and no NLS. GFP + NLS ends in the nucleus (importins carry it in; it builds up over a few minutes). The test of sufficiency is “large protein” versus “large protein + NLS” (try making GFP + NLS large too): the NLS alone moves an unrelated protein. Necessity would be a mutated NLS, which the game shows as removing the NLS.

E3. Cathepsin D with its signal peptide goes ER → Golgi (where it gets the M6P tag) → endosome → lysosome, about 61 min. Without the signal peptide it is made and stays in the cytosol; the M6P patch is greyed out. The M6P tag is added by an enzyme in the cis-Golgi lumen; a protein that never enters the ER never meets it, so the patch has no effect. (Real cathepsin D would also fold badly in the cytosol.)

E4. (a) Albumin + NLS is still secreted. The signal peptide is read first, during translation, so the whole chain, NLS included, is in the ER lumen. Importins are in the cytosol: the NLS is on the wrong side of the membrane. (b) LDL receptor + KDEL still goes to the plasma membrane. With the signal peptide first and one TM segment, the N-terminal part is in the lumen and the C-terminal tail is in the cytosol (a type I membrane protein). KDEL is at the C-terminus, so it faces the cytosol, and the KDEL receptor, which binds in the lumen, never sees it. In both cases a signal works only on the side of the membrane where its receptor is: that is topology. (ER membrane proteins use a different retrieval signal in their cytosolic tail.)

E5. Both the presequence and the NLS are read in the cytosol, by different receptors (TOM and importins), so they compete for the same newly made protein. Which wins depends on how strong each signal is, how fast the protein folds (mitochondrial import needs an unfolded chain, nuclear import works on folded protein) and the amounts of the receptors; real proteins like this are often found in both places. An experiment: express a GFP fusion of the protein in cells and image with a mitochondrial marker (e.g. MitoTracker or a red mitochondrial protein) and a DNA stain; quantify the fraction in each. Confirm with cell fractionation (nuclear, mitochondrial and cytosolic fractions, Western blot with marker proteins for each fraction), and mutate each signal in turn to see which one is needed for each location.

E6. It goes to the inner mitochondrial membrane: the presequence starts import into the matrix through TOM and TIM23, and the TM segment stops transfer in TIM23 and moves sideways into the inner membrane, with the N-terminus in the matrix and the C-terminal part in the intermembrane space. It takes about 2 min, compared with about 46 min for the LDL receptor. Mitochondrial import is a direct, post-translational crossing of two membranes from the cytosol, while the plasma membrane is reached through the whole secretory pathway: folding and quality control in the ER, vesicle steps to and through the Golgi, and a vesicle to the surface.
